# CHAPTER TWO

## Reichstagsufer

**Berlin — September 1927**

Sixteen years before she put her fingers inside Ludwig Schoeler's chest, Sylwia Kotarska stood on the granite embankment of the Spree with wind whipping her pleated navy skirt around her knees and decided that Berlin smelled of wet coal smoke, roasted chestnuts, benzine exhaust, and intolerable self-satisfaction.

Across the green-brown river, gilded autumn sunlight struck the glass dome of the Reichstag, four hundred yards upstream from the red-brick neoclassical facade of Reichstagsufer Number Six, where golden letters above the tall oak doors proclaimed *COLLÈGE FRANÇAIS — FRANZÖSISCHES GYMNASIUM, FONDÉ EN 1689*. Coal barges from Upper Silesia—Polish Silesia now, half of it, a geographical fact sufficient to give every retired Prussian colonel within ten miles an apoplectic stroke over his morning *Kreuzzeitung*—chugged low in the water beneath the iron arches of the Marschallbrücke.

"Stop scowling like a Cossack about to sack a post-chaise," murmured Kazimierz Kotarski beside her, resting one kid-gloved hand lightly on his daughter's shoulder while tipping his Homburg to two French Embassy attachés descending the steps. At forty-six, Counselor Kotarski of the Polish Legation on Kurfürstenstraße possessed silver-threaded dark hair, a dueling scar from his student years in Lwów hidden neatly beneath his trimmed mustache, and the bone-deep, ironic courtesy of an old Mazovian family that had lost three estates to Russian tsars and two to Prussian partitions without once lowering its chin. "Your stockings are straight, your French is cleaner than Monsieur le Proviseur's, and your Latin could put half the Wilhelmstraße to shame."

"The headmaster looked at my Warsaw certificate yesterday as though I had written it in berry juice on birchbark, Papa," Sylwia said in Polish, shifting her heavy leather satchel of Teubner classics onto her left hip. At sixteen she had already shot up to five feet eight inches, all coltish long legs, fierce grey eyes, and two thick chestnut braids pinned into coiled loops behind her ears because she despised wasting ten minutes every morning with curling tongs like the diplomatic daughters on Tiergartenstraße.

"Oberstudiendirektor Becker is a Prussian civil servant," Kazimierz replied serenely, switching to French as three *Obersekunda* boys in dark Norfolk jackets hurried past them through the iron gates, turning their heads overtly to stare at the tall new girl. "If Almighty God applied for admission to the *Obersekunda* with transcripts from Kraków or Warsaw, Herr Becker would test Him in irregular Greek verbs before assigning Him a bench. Remember the rule, *córeczko*."

"Never lose my temper," Sylwia recited dutifully, "because anger makes a Pole careless and a German righteous."

"And?"

A wicked glint lit her grey eyes. "Never sit in the back row, and if anyone calls Poland a *Saisonstaat*—a seasonal state—beat him by twenty marks on Friday's examination rather than boxing his ears on Monday."

Kazimierz laughed softly, kissing her temple. "Ten marks will suffice. Leave the poor Teutons a shred of vanity; they become noisy when cornered. Driver will fetch you at four—do not walk through Friedrichstraße alone if the Communists and Brownshirts are breaking beer-hall chairs again."

Ten minutes later, shown into the tall, chalk-scented classroom of *Obersekunda A* on the second floor overlooking the river, Sylwia discovered that thirty-six pairs of eyes—thirty-one male and five female, children of Weimar ministers, Jewish bank directors from Grunewald, Swiss diplomats, and old Brandenburg landowning clans—had paused in mid-chatter to inspect *die neue Polin*.

Only one boy did not look up.

He sat by the third tall window in the sunlit prime seat of the front row, one long leg stretched lazily into the aisle, leaning back slightly in his scarred oak bench while reading an uncut French edition of Valéry with a silver pencil balanced across his knuckles. Even seated, he was tall—broad through the shoulders for sixteen, lean-hipped in a tailored charcoal suit whose lapels whispered Savile Row rather than Konfektionshaus Wertheim. Thick dark-blond hair, the color of tarnished guilder gold, fell across a high forehead above straight dark brows, a blade-straight nose, and a mouth cut with cool, faintly disdainful precision.

"Fräulein Kotarska, take the empty place second from the aisle, across from Fräulein Oppenheim," rasped Herr Doktor Emil Glass, limping behind the raised lectern on his built-up left boot. Half of Doktor Glass's lower left jaw had been carried away by a French seventy-five splinter at Douaumont in nineteen-sixteen and rebuilt out of silver wire and vulcanite, giving his smile a permanent, terrifyingly sardonic twist. Every student in Berlin feared Glass's tongue more than the rod. "Open Thucydides, Book Five, Chapter Eighty-Nine. Since our guests from across the Vistula rumor themselves Latinists rather than Hellenists, we shall spare Fräulein Kotarska on her first morning and allow Schoeler to construe."

Around the room, tiny knowing smiles flickered. Evidently *Schoeler* did not "construe"; Schoeler handed down tablets from Sinai.

The dark-blond boy by the window closed his Valéry without haste, laid down his silver pencil, and stood. He had eyes the exact cold blue-slate of winter Baltic water before snow. As he rose, those slate eyes flicked sideways for the first time across the aisle—taking in Sylwia's face, her coiled chestnut hair, her inexpensive Warsaw leather satchel, and dismissing her to the outer provinces of creation inside half a heartbeat.

Then he began translating directly from the unpointed Greek page into crystalline, cadenced German, without once glancing at a lexicon:

*"Wir werden also unsererseits keine schönen Worte machen—als ob wir mit Recht herrschten, weil wir den Perser geschlagen haben—noch sollt ihr meinen, uns durch Reden zu überzeugen... Sondern wir wissen wie ihr, dass im menschlichen Verhältnis das Recht nur unter Gleichen an Macht gilt; die Starken aber tun, was in ihrer Kraft steht, und die Schwachen leiden, was sie leiden müssen."*

*The strong do what they can, and the weak suffer what they must.*

When he finished the period, there was the small, hushed silence reserved for effortless perfection. Even Doktor Glass's vulcanite chin inclined half an inch in gratification. "Lucid as ever, Schoeler. Observe, gentlemen and ladies, how he renders *dunata*—what lies within their power—without sentimentalizing Athenian *Realpolitik*. Have you any quarrel with that rendering, Schoeler?"

"None, Herr Doktor," Ludwig Schoeler said pleasantly. His voice had already deepened into a clean, resonant baritone, clipped with the effortless assurance of Dahlem drawing rooms and Masurian riding stables. "Thucydides was a general before he was a historian. Generals seldom confuse morality with arithmetic."

Something hot and lethal uncoiled behind Sylwia Kotarska's ribs. Perhaps it was the word *general*; perhaps it was the lazy half-inch flick of those Baltic eyes across her Warsaw satchel; perhaps it was knowing very well how men in Berlin drawing rooms quoted that exact sentence whenever the borders of Poland came up over brandy.

Her hand did not rise; she simply spoke from her bench in clear, bell-pure German without a trace of provincial slur:

"Except that Herr Schoeler mistranslated *prassousin* in the second clause and turned a diagnostic observation into a Prussian nursery rhyme."

Forty heads snapped around so hard necks cracked. Beside Sylwia, dark-eyed Ruth Oppenheim sucked in an audible hiss through her teeth. Up at the window, Ludwig Schoeler did not turn immediately; his spine merely stiffened half an inch under charcoal wool before he rotated his head slowly down toward the second bench.

At the lectern, Doktor Glass's good eyebrow climbed halfway into his iron-grey hairline. "Did I hear a squeak from the Vistula, Fräulein Kotarska? Stand up when you accuse my *Primus* of faulty Greek."

Sylwia stood. Tall, chin level, hands clasped loosely behind her waist so nobody would see her fingertips tingling. "Thucydides does not write *tun, was in ihrer Kraft steht* as a boast of brute force, Herr Doktor. Look at the participial balance: *dunata d' hoi prouchontes prassousin kai hoi astheneis xunchorousin*. *Prassousin* here carries the transactional sense—'exact what is feasible'—paired directly with *xunchorousin*, which does not mean 'suffer passively' at all, as Herr Schoeler melodramatically rendered it. *Xunchorein* means 'to yield ground, to come to terms, to concede.' And if Herr Schoeler had bothered reading twenty pages ahead to Book Seven in the Sicilian quarries, he would recall what happened to those invincible Athenian 'strong' once they confused arrogance with arithmetic. Seven thousand of them died of dysentery in a hole in Syracuse."

Dead, catastrophic silence hung beneath the painted plaster ceiling. Out on the Spree, a coal tug hooted twice—*whooo, whooo*—mournful and absurd.

Slowly, horribly, the uninjured corner of Doktor Glass's mouth curled upward. "Well, well," murmured the old classicist, tapping his chalk stick softly against his silver-wired jaw. "*Xunchorousin*—'grant, accede, yield.' Liddell and Scott will bear Fräulein Kotarska out, Schoeler. You substituted Schopenhauer's *leiden* for Attic Greek because you fell in love with the sound of your own cadence. Nine out of ten for you today, Schoeler. Ten for Fräulein Kotarska. Sit down, both of you."

Nine out of ten. According to Ruth Oppenheim's breathless, scandalized whisper over buttered rye bread during eleven-o'clock recess in the paved courtyard, Ludwig Schoeler had not received lower than a full *Sehr gut* ten in Greek since November nineteen-twenty-four.

Sylwia was standing alone by the cast-iron drinking fountain rinsing chalk off her fingers when a tall shadow fell across the basin.

Up close, he was three inches taller than she—one of the very few sixteen-year-old boys in Berlin who forced her to tilt her chin slightly upward rather than stare straight across his bridge of nose. His slate-blue eyes were neither angry nor flustered; they were worse—intently, surgically curious, dissecting her from temple to throat the way one inspects a wasp found alive inside a winter glove.

"Kotarska," he said conversationally, hands tucked into his coat pockets. "Your father is the commercial counselor who filed the swine-tariff protest at the Foreign Office last month."

"Legal counselor," Sylwia replied coolly, drying her hands on a linen handkerchief. "Though I understand Prussian agrarians have difficulty distinguishing law from pigs."

Not a muscle moved in his lean face, save a tiny, lethal flicker at the corner of his mouth. "Clever trick with *xunchorein*. Did your tutor in Warsaw drill that passage all August in case someone asked you to sing for your supper?"

"In Warsaw," Sylwia smiled sweetly, "children finish Book Five in *Untersekunda*. Don't take it to heart, Herr Schoeler. Nine is a very respectable mark for someone reading Greek out of French cribs."

His eyes narrowed—just a fraction—catching the hit on his uncut Valéry. "Enjoy your morning in the sun, Fräulein. Glass likes novelties in September. By November, tourists usually discover the air along the Spree is too brisk for them."

"My lungs are excellent, Herr Schoeler. Watch your own."

By November of nineteen-twenty-seven, the masters' common room at the *Französisches Gymnasium* had stopped calling it academic competition and begun referring to it—half in exasperation, half in secret awe—as the Second Silesian War.

Nothing escaped it. When Professor Mercier posted the French essay rankings on Montaigne, Kotarska and Schoeler stood pinned side by side at nineteen-point-five out of twenty, each glaring furiously at the half-point lost rather than the thirty-four classmates trailing four points behind. When Ludwig organized the *Obersekunda* debating union to argue revision of the eastern frontiers at Locarno, Sylwia entered from the floor uninvited, quoted Bismarck's own 1863 warnings against swallowing indigestible Polish provinces back into Ludwig's teeth from memory, and stole seven votes from his own bench. Three days later, Sylwia opened her locker before organic chemistry practicals to discover that someone had thoughtfully replaced her distilled water wash-bottle with dilute ammonium sulfide, filling half the corridor with the stench of rotten eggs ten seconds before Oberstudiendirektor Becker walked in—and yet she calmly ran a blank control, deduced the contamination molarity on slate in four minutes, and handed in clean crystals anyway while Ludwig leaned against the fume hood watching her through narrowed, almost appreciative slate eyes.

She paid him back the following Tuesday. Having bribed the school porter two Reichsmarks to learn that Ludwig kept his prized rowing scull polished to mirror varnish at the *Berliner Ruder-Club* boathouse on Wannsee for Saturday's junior regatta against Potsdam, Sylwia cycled nine miles in drizzle on Friday evening, climbed a spiked iron fence in boy's breeches, and painted three neat inches of colorless lanolin mixed with powdered rosin in a diagonal drag-strip just aft of his fin—undetectable to the eye, just sticky enough in cold water to pull his bow two degrees to port every fifth stroke and cost him first place by half a canvas length while his furious rowing coach screamed about his left oar.

On Monday morning, Ludwig walked past her bench before prayers, dropped a curl of scraped rosin-lanolin wax wrapped in a leaf from *Le Figaro* onto her blotter without breaking stride, and murmured under his breath: *"Crude hydrodynamics, Kotarska. Next time weight the sternpost with lead shot; asymmetric drag ruins the wrist."*

Ruth Oppenheim stared after him open-mouthed. "Are you two insane? If Oberstudiendirektor Becker catches either of you, you'll be rusticated! Why do you hate him so much?"

Sylwia crumpled the *Figaro* scrap inside her fist, feeling the hard little pellet of wax bite her palm while across the room Ludwig laughed quietly at something Kurt von Bredow said, never once looking back—except that in the brass reflection of the tall window latch beside his shoulder, she could see his eyes watching her reflection the entire time.

"Because," Sylwia said tightly, "he breathes as though the rest of Europe exists by his permission."

And neither Ruth nor anyone else in Berlin understood the real secret of their feud: that in a noisy, glittering city of four million souls hurtling toward an abyss over jazz horns and marching boots, nobody else at the *Französisches Gymnasium* existed for Ludwig Schoeler or Sylwia Kotarska at all. Other classmates were furniture; professors were scorekeepers. Every morning each woke up wondering solely what trap the other had laid since midnight.

**Warsaw — October 20, 1943, 04:15 AM**

Pain arrived before memory—a white-hot iron crowbar wedged beneath his left collarbone and levered slowly upward every time his diaphragm dropped.

*Breathe shallow,* trained instinct ordered before his eyelids ever moved. *Left chest. Splinter or round. Don't cough or you'll tear whatever's stitched.*

Ludwig Schoeler kept his eyes shut and catalogued the dark by ear and nose, the way four years of war from Sedan to Orel had taught him to wake in unfamiliar dugouts.

No artillery rumble. Therefore not Russia.

Rain ticking steadily against windowpanes shrouded in heavy paper—blackout blind, rattle of loose putty in rotten wooden sashes. Smell: carbolic soap, damp lime plaster, unwashed wool somewhere outside a closed door, ether clinging sour and cold at the back of his own nasal passages, and beneath his left ribs a faint, rhythmic *glug-tick* of water inside glass every time he exhaled.

*Hospital. Chest drain.*

Now memory came back in jagged, razor-bright shards of safety glass: twenty-two-forty hours, pulling out of Dworzec Główny in the rain instead of Dworzec Wschodni because Brandt had switched stations at the last stop in Małkinia—*no, wait*. Nobody in Warsaw had known he switched off the Königsberg express onto the suburban mail loop except two signals clerks at Mauerwald and the liaison notification forwarded thirty minutes prior to SS-Sturmbannführer Reinhardt's duty officer at Aleja Szucha.

And yet four hundred meters down Aleje Jerozolimskie, a Filipinka percussion grenade had blown the Horch's left front tire off its rim and two men in civilian trench coats had stepped out of a bombed gateway firing nine-millimeter Parabellum submachine guns straight into the rear left door where he sat.

*Reinhardt,* Ludwig thought coldly through the grinding red haze under his clavicle. *That soft-voiced Munich policeman tried to have the Poles tidy me into a zinc box before I even opened my safe.*

His tongue felt coated in dry flannel and copper rust. His right arm lay outside a coarse boiled-linen blanket, strapped lightly to a wooden splint board at the bend of the elbow where an intravenous cannula had been tied off. His left arm was immobilized in a Velpeau sling across his bandaged ribs. And someone was standing eighteen inches to his right in the dim cobalt glow of a shaded blackout night-lamp.

Not a Wehrmacht *Sanitäter*—no leather belt creak, no tobacco reek, no heavy boots. Wooden-soled hospital clogs, set down noiselessly on linoleum. The crisp starch rustle of a white surgical smock. Cool, steady fingers—un-gloved, long, calloused faintly along the inner side of the middle joint from years of needle-holders—resting lightly over his right radial pulse while a second hand leaned down to inspect the glass water-seal bottle beside the iron bedframe.

Through the ether fog clinging to the base of his skull, a fragment of hallucination echoed from miles underwater: a woman's voice speaking clean, icy Charlottenburg German beneath harsh white lamps. *Take your pistol out of his throat, or your colonel will bleed to death on my floor while you play executioner.*

Impossible. Fever plays grotesque jokes after blood loss; at Kursk, when Konrad died, Ludwig had sworn for ten minutes he heard his brother whistling Schubert outside the aid bunker. And yet—that cadence. That exact, crisp, faintly Slavic precision on the *r* behind the upper teeth, preserved under glass in his memory across thirteen winters—

Ludwig opened his eyes.

The room was narrow, high-ceilinged, whitewashed gone grey with wartime coal soot. Outside the frosted-glass upper panel of the corridor door, the silhouetted helmet of one of Brandt's *Feldgendarmen* stood motionless under a hall bulb. Inside, bathed in ghostly blue night-light, a tall woman in a blood-flecked surgical smock straightened from checking his pleural bottle.

Time had pared away every trace of sixteen-year-old softness from her face. Her cheekbones stood out stark and sculptural beneath translucent, exhausted pale skin; dark violet shadows bruised the hollows beneath wide-set storm-grey eyes; her heavy dark-chestnut hair was twisted ruthlessly back into a knot at her nape, a single loose strand curving damply against the long, taut line of her throat.

Before she even realized his eyes were open—before she could step back out of reach—Ludwig drove his splinted right arm upward on pure reflex and clamped his fingers hard around her bare left wrist.

Beneath his palm, gauze slipped slightly over the crook of her elbow, and a sharp intake of breath hissed silently between her teeth—not fear; irritation at sudden pressure on a fresh sixteen-gauge venipuncture.

For five full seconds neither of them spoke. Under his fingers her radial pulse jumped once—fast, fierce—and then steadied by sheer willpower. Slowly, Sylwia Kotarska looked down into his eyes. Neither surprise nor mercy softened a line of her mouth.

"Kotarska," Ludwig whispered. His throat was raw from ether and thirst, scraping the syllables out barely louder than the rain on the glass. "Am I dead and in hell, then?"

"Worse, Herr Oberstleutnant," she answered in the exact low, level Berlin German that had haunted half his twenties. "Warsaw. And if you pull on that right basilic cut-down again, you'll tear four stitches and soil my clean linen."

Even with a shattered collarbone and half a lung full of blood-stained fibrin, the corner of Ludwig Schoeler's pale mouth twitched. Something fierce, incredulous, and darkly electric flared behind the slate-blue eyes—utterly eclipsing war, ambush, uniforms, and four years of corpses between them for one dizzying heartbeat.

"Infant Jesus Hospital," he rasped softly, reading the faded blue stamp woven sideways across the turned-down sheet on his chest before lifting his gaze back to her face. "Good God. Brandt carried me into *your* butcher shop?"

"Had I known whose carcass was under the greatcoat before I clamped the subclavian," Sylwia murmured, trying smoothly—and failing—to twist her wrist free without jerking his chest wall, "I might have saved six inches of five-zero silk. Let go of my arm, Schoeler."

Instead of releasing her, his thumb shifted half an inch upward inside the pushed-back cuff of her smock, brushing squarely across the figure-of-eight gauze pad taped over the bruised blue vein at her inner elbow. His eyes flicked from her bandaged elbow to the matching cut-down dressing on his own right arm—and then up to the bloodless, translucent pallor of her high cheekbones.

No *Abwehr* colonel needed an interpreter for that arithmetic.

"No plasma bank," he said very quietly. All mockery vanished from his voice, leaving something stripped and dangerous in the blue dark. "Group Zero. You gave me your own blood."

"Don't flatter yourself," she whispered back—throwing his own fifteen-year-old Halensee words straight into his teeth without turning a hair. "Aleja Szucha promised to shoot a hundred hostages from Pawiak and my surgical staff at six o'clock this morning if you died on the table. I would have bled a stray dog into your veins if dogs carried Group Zero. Now take your hand off me before I call your sentry and tell him you're delirious."

"Call him," Ludwig murmured, though the effort of holding her wrist was already bringing cold beads of sweat out along his hairline and above the thin white crescent scar at his right eyebrow. His eyes locked onto hers—searching, ruthless, missing nothing: not the exhausted tremor she was hiding in her knees, not the ferocious hatred blazing behind her grey irises, and not the fact that she had checked his water-seal drain herself at four in the morning instead of sending a night nurse. "Let him hear how fluent Frau Doktor Kotarska's Berlin German is. Better yet—wait until seven o'clock when Sturmbannführer Reinhardt walks through that door and asks why a Polish woman surgeon fought for an hour to save a German staff officer."

A cold needle slid down Sylwia's spine. Even half-dead, soaked in ether and running ninety-nine point eight degrees of post-traumatic fever, his mind moved three moves ahead on the board—just as she had dreaded it would the instant she saw his face under the floodlamp.

"I am a surgeon," she said glacially. "I repair damaged tissue when a Luger is held to my director's head. Whatever intrigues you and your SS butchers brought from Berlin are none of my affair."

"Liar," Ludwig breathed softly, almost tenderly, his fingers finally loosening a fraction as a jagged spasm of pleural pain hitched his ribs. He refused to groan; only his jaw clenched white, teeth grinding once before he mastered it. "Nothing in Warsaw happens by accident, Sylwia—least of all those two men with Parabellums who knew which car I sat in. How long before you tell your friends outside these walls that you missed my heart by two inches?"

Sylwia leaned down six inches closer—as though adjusting the fold of the blanket across his good right shoulder—so that had Brandt's sentry pressed his nose against the frosted glass, he would have seen only a conscientious doctor tending a feverish officer. Beneath her collar, the tiny glass bead of cyanide rested cold against her skin; six inches lower, the heat rising off Ludwig Schoeler's bare throat and bandaged chest smelled of her own blood, fever sweat, and dark, terrifying memory.

"Listen to me carefully, Herr Oberstleutnant," she whispered against his ear, soft as silk and sharp as a #11 Parker blade. "My suture on your left subclavian artery is five millimeters wide under a shattered collarbone. If you breathe one word to the Gestapo connecting me to Berlin, or bring your hounds sniffing through my wards, you won't live long enough to sign the denunciation—and no coroner in Greater Germany will ever find what stopped your heart. Sleep quietly, don't cough, and get out of my hospital the first day you can stand."

She straightened, pulling her wrist cleanly out of his slackening grip, and reached toward the enamel tray on the nightstand where a prepared Pravaz glass syringe of one-sixth grain *Morphium hydrochloricum* lay on alcohol cotton. "Turn your deltoid slightly. You need six hours' sedation before the lung consolidates."

"Put that needle down," Ludwig whispered sharply.

She paused, needle poised. "Bravery doesn't prevent pain shock or atelectasis, Schoeler. Your ribs are torn—"

"Keep your morphine," he said, slate-blue eyes glittering up at her through sweat-darkened lashes with cold, indomitable stubbornness. "Nobody clouds my head in this city—least of all you, Kotarska. Leave the water glass where I can reach it, and turn off the lamp."

For three seconds they stared at each other across twelve inches of blue darkness—thirty-two years old, oceans of corpses and ruined cities between them, bound together by five stitches of oiled black silk and four hundred fifty cubic centimeters of living Polish blood beating steadily inside a Prussian heart.

Sylwia laid the unused morphine syringe back onto the alcohol pad without a word, set a glass of boiled water with an angled drinking straw beside his right elbow, clicked off the blue night-switch, and walked out into the freezing corridor without looking back.
