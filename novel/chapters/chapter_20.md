# CHAPTER TWENTY

## No Man's Mercy

**Warsaw (Praga) — February 1, 1944**

By nine o'clock that Tuesday night, fresh east-wind snow had buried the morning's dirty slush beneath four clean white inches, and Left Bank Warsaw across the black, ice-grinding Vistula glowed sullen orange under German searchlights and punitive flares.

All afternoon following Franz Kutschera's death at nine-ten and the slaughter of Otto Reinhardt's garrison at Number Eight Litewska two minutes later, loudspeaker trucks had crawled down Marszałkowska barking martial decrees: curfew moved forward to seven PM; one hundred million zlotys punitive tribute levied upon the population of Warsaw; every Vistula road bridge sealed behind twin machine-gun nests; and three hundred Pawiak prisoners—Mercifully minus forty-two *Wintersturm* targets whose only master list was already burning page by page inside Major Rawicz's cellar stove once copied—scheduled for tomorrow's sandpits.

Over here on the eastern bank in Praga, tucked behind the soot-blackened brick roundhouses of Dworzec Wschodni marshaling yards where steam locomotives hissed white plumes twenty feet into swirling snow, Słowik's greased *Ostbahn* wheels turned silently in the dark.

Twenty minutes earlier, inside a dark goods brake-van smelling of Baltic pine resin and Stockholm tar, Viktor Brandt—cleaned, stitched above his swollen left eye, broken right fingers immobilized neatly in a curved aluminum splint Sylwia had molded over boiled felt, dressed in grey *Organisation Todt* civil-engineer wool bearing immaculate Danzig harbor passes—had gripped Sylwia's hand fiercely with his good left paw before clasping his colonel's forearm in wordless East Prussian farewell as the northbound timber freight clanked slowly out toward Małkinia and the Baltic coast.

Now, inside the deserted, unlit brick sand-drying shed fifty yards behind Platform Four—eighteen minutes before the twenty-one-forty Königsberg–Danzig night courier express backed onto the civilian passenger siding—only two shadows stood beside a dying coke brazier while locomotives shunted iron couplings outside.

Gone forever was Oberstleutnant Schoeler's crimson-tabbed field-grey wool; Słowik had weighted tunic, cap, and blood-stained riding breeches with forty pounds of scrap rail-iron and sunk them through an ice-hole midstream in the Vistula at dusk. Dressed in a heavy dark Scandinavian tweed overcoat, wool scarf, and soft felt hat, leaning lightly against a timber upright to take weight off his bandaged left thigh muscle where Reinhardt's final bullet had passed cleanly through vastus lateralis without touching bone, Ludwig drew two stiff dark-blue Swedish Foreign Office *Skyddspass* booklets out of his breast pocket.

Opening both slightly toward the dull red glow of the coke brazier, he showed Sylwia two passport photographs Słowik's studio camera had snapped in the Leszno bunker forty-eight hours ago beneath dry embossed crowns of Uppsala and Stockholm:

*LINDQVIST, Lars Henrik — Skogsingenjör (Timber Commissioner).*
*LINDQVIST, Karin Maria, född Moreau — Läkare (Physician).*

"Stamped clean by the German Harbour-Master's proxy in Gdynia and counter-endorsed by the Swedish Consulate attaché on Słowik's payroll," Ludwig said quietly over the rhythmic *chuff-hiss, chuff-hiss* of a switching engine drifting past the frosted glass outside. "Sixteen hours in a first-class sleeper compartment through Marienburg to Danzig; Thursday night Słowik's Swedish collier *Sveaborg* clears Neufahrwasser mole with coal ballast for Malmö. By Saturday morning you wake up behind windows without blackout paper and drink real milk without listening for boots on the stairs."

He held the second blue passport out toward her gloved hands.

Sylwia stood three feet before him in her worn charcoal wool coat—cleaned and mended once more by Słowik's housekeeper, her black leather surgical bag resting beside her ankle on the cinders—and did not raise her hand to take the blue booklet.

Looking down at the stillness of her hands, then slowly up into wide storm-grey eyes luminous in the dim red brazier-light, Ludwig went cold to the marrow before she spoke a single word.

"Don't," Ludwig said softly, roughly, stepping half a pace forward despite the hot pull in his stitched thigh. "For the love of Almighty God, Sylwia, don't say what I see behind your eyes right now."

"Look me straight in the face first, *Herr Lindqvist*," Sylwia said in quiet, crystal-clear Berlin German, "and tell me what *you* plan to do Thursday night on Neufahrwasser pier once you watch me climb up the gangplank of the *Sveaborg* for Malmö."

For two heartbeats Ludwig didn't answer—and between Ludwig Schoeler and Sylwia Kotarska, two heartbeats of silence screamed louder than brass bands.

"You aren't boarding that Swedish steamer in Danzig at all, are you?" Sylwia whispered, stepping close enough to rest one gloved palm flat against the rough dark tweed over his chest, feeling the heavy, steady beat beneath. "Once Viktor Brandt puts you in touch with Königsberg's reserve signals desk on Thursday, you're taking Słowik's second set of Wehrmacht convalescent-furlough papers straight west into East Prussia and Berlin—to warn Henning von Tresckow and Stauffenberg's circle that Kaltenbrunner has caught the Smolensk scent, and stay until somebody puts a live bomb under Adolf Hitler's table."

Every muscle in Ludwig's lean jaw locked hard. Lying to her had failed in nineteen-twenty-eight, failed in December, and stood zero chance tonight.

"If nobody finishes Berlin from the inside before summer, Sylwia, five million more people die between the Vistula and the Elbe before next spring," he rasped fiercely, dropping both passports momentarily onto the sand barrel so he could grip both her shoulders hard in his gloved hands. "And I know—*down to the ton of TNT*—what waits across that river behind you if you stay in Warsaw! You photographed *Fall Warschau* on my desk yourself! Listen to me not as a man who loves you past reason, listen to a General Staff planner who has read both sides' logistics!"

White breath steamed sharp and ragged between their faces in the freezing shed.

"By July or August," Ludwig drove every word home like iron spikes, "Russian tank armies will reach this exact railway yard in Praga! Your Home Army—brave, proud, starved for four years, armed with one Sten gun for every ten teenagers—will rise inside Left Bank Warsaw expecting five days' street fighting before Allied relief! And Joseph Stalin will halt Rokossovsky's armored spearheads dead cold right here along the eastern bank of the Vistula without firing one artillery shell to help you—sitting back smoking his pipe while Himmler sends Dirlewanger's penal battalions, Cossack brigades, and six-hundred-millimeter siege mortars block by block from Wola to the river to murder every living thing that breathes and dynamite every stone down to cellar vaults! Two hundred thousand corpses inside sixty days, Sylwia!"

For the first and only time in sixteen years, pride cracked completely out of Ludwig Schoeler's voice, leaving raw, naked terror—not for his own neck walking back toward Gestapo gallows in Berlin, solely for hers.

"Don't walk back across that ice into an open furnace!" he breathed harshly, framing her cold cheeks between his bare hands after ripping his gloves off with his teeth. "You've already paid ten lifetimes' blood debt! You saved Janek, you saved Jacek and little Staś, you pulled Halina out of Reinhardt's cellar and put Reinhardt four inches deep on his own floor! Get on that train with me to Danzig tonight, wait for me in Stockholm where winter doesn't smell of lime pits, and if I'm still breathing when Hitler rots in petrol, I'll swim the Baltic if I have to—"

Hot, scalding tears spilled silent and unashamed down Sylwia's pale cheeks into his warm palms—the first time since nineteen-thirty she had let him see water fall down her face in open air—and yet her storm-grey eyes never wavered a fraction of an inch from his.

"And if those twenty men waiting for you in Bendlerstraße asked *you* to sit safe in a Stockholm hotel drinking coffee while they walked into Wolfsschanze carrying plastic explosive," Sylwia whispered, turning her mouth half an inch to kiss the scarred heel of his right palm, "would Ludwig Schoeler board the *Sveaborg* on Thursday night?"

Silence struck him dumb. Outside, twenty yards beyond frosted panes, iron couplings clanged hard—*BANG-clack-clack*—as the Königsberg–Danzig express backed seven dark green sleeping coaches onto Platform Four and steam brakes vented with a long, mournful roar.

"Across that dark river tonight," Sylwia said softly, voice trembling once before ringing clear as finest surgical steel, "Halina Nowak has one working arm, Professor Majewski is dead, Karski is dead, and four clandestine cellar hospitals between Mokotów and Stare Miasto have three trained chest surgeons left among a million souls. Everything you just said about August and Russian tanks and six-hundred-millimeter mortars is probably dead true—I'm no romantic fool, Ludwig, I know how Moscow loves free Poles as well as Berlin does. Which means in August sixteen-year-old boys and girls—our age when I walked into Doktor Glass's Greek hour—are going to be carried down cellar steps on ripped window-shutters with shrapnel in their lungs and femoral arteries pumping into brick dust."

Rising half an inch onto her toes, gripping the rough tweed lapels of his coat in both scarred hands, she looked straight into the bottom of his soul.

"If I desert my tables tonight to drink Swedish milk while Warsaw burns, Ludwig, the woman waiting for you in a clean Stockholm room next year won't be Sylwia Kotarska—she'll be a hollowed-out ghost who flinches every time a tram bell rings, and before two winters passed we would poison each other with silence. Go finish your monsters in Berlin; I'll keep breath inside whoever falls on my cellar doors here."

Looking down into those storm-grey eyes shining wet and unconquerable in red ember-light, Ludwig closed his eyes for two seconds—knowing with a crushing, terrible pride that swelled his chest nearly to bursting that one might sooner ask the Vistula to flow uphill toward Cracow than bend this woman half an inch away from honor.

Opening his eyes, he reached inside his wool vest over his healed left collarbone, drew out the heavy two-ounce silver **Humboldt *Primus Omnium* medallion**—deeply dented on the rim where October lead had bounced off Berlin silver two inches above his heart—and pressed it firmly into her bare right palm alongside her mother's green Colombian emerald.

"Keep your half of one-point-zero-zero in your left breast pocket," Ludwig murmured hoarsely, closing her fingers tight over the warm silver. "Apparently Prussian silver turns nine-millimeter lead decently enough when luck runs thin."

In answer, Sylwia reached into the chamois lining of her surgical roll, lifted out Cécile Moreau-Kotarska's tarnished silver Mazovian willow-leaf hair-clasp, and slid it deep inside the inner breast pocket of Ludwig's dark tweed overcoat, patting the wool flat over his heart.

"And you bring my mother's silver back to me in person, Herr Oberstleutnant," she whispered fiercely. "Don't trust the German post office; their rates from Sachsenhausen were exorbitant."

Out on Platform Four a conductor's whistle shrilled long and sharp through blowing snow: *"Einsteigen bitte—Schnellzug Richtung Marienburg–Danzig!"*

Neither had words left for gentleness. Ludwig dragged her crushing-hard up against his chest with both arms, kissing her cold, salt-wet mouth open with bruising, starving desperation—tasting tears, coal smoke, snow, and sixteen years of fire—until the second whistle blasted and locomotive drive-wheels spun once with a heavy *CHUFF-hiss* on icy steel rails.

Tearing his mouth half an inch from hers, forehead pressed hard against hers while snow swirled white through the open shed doorway around their boots, Ludwig spoke straight against her lips:

"Do not dare die on me in those sewers, Kotarska! Nobody ruins your life in this world except me—remember that when ceilings fall! First day of November after the guns stop—twelve noon sharp, Reichstagsufer Six, inner courtyard under Glass's classroom window! If Berlin is twenty feet of bombed rubble, dig down to the paving stones—I will be standing there!"

"Twelve noon, First of November!" Sylwia choked out, shoving his chest with both hands toward the moving lantern light outside before her knees betrayed her—"Run, Ludwig—*idź z Bogiem*!"

Limping swiftly three strides through deep track-side snow, Ludwig caught the icy brass handrail of the moving last sleeping-car vestibule as it glided past Platform Four, swung cleanly aboard onto the iron step, and stayed leaning half out into swirling white snow and orange firebox sparks without taking his bare head out of the wind until darkness and coal-steam swallowed the solitary tall woman standing motionless beside the sand-shed.

Ten minutes later, hidden beneath tarpaulin straw in the back of a Praga butcher's horse-sleigh carrying forged municipal carcass-permits back across Kierbedź Bridge's ice-floes into darkened Left Bank Warsaw, Sylwia Kotarska gripped two ounces of dented Berlin silver tight inside her pocket and went back to work.

**Warsaw — September 2, 1944 / Berlin — April 24, 1945**

Six months to the day after Dworzec Wschodni, every prophecy Ludwig Schoeler had spoken beside the Praga brazier came down out of smoky August heaven in white-hot iron.

On the thirty-third day of the Warsaw Uprising, beneath the collapsed brick bakery vaults of Number Sixteen ulica Hoża in burning Śródmieście while six-hundred-millimeter *Thor* siege-mortar shells weighing two metric tons apiece walked down Krucza every eight minutes—each impact lifting the cellar stone floor four inches beneath Sylwia's bloody clogs and raining powdered lime straight onto open abdomens if Halina didn't hold an umbrella tarp one-handed over the zinc table—Sylwia clamped the torn femoral vein of a fifteen-year-old boy scout courier from *Szare Szeregi* by flickering bicycle-dynamo bulb light.

Water mains at Lindleya had blown dry three weeks ago; ether had run out on Tuesday; outside in eighty-five-degree summer stench, unburied dead swelled beneath barricades of overturned tramcars while across the sunlit Vistula three miles east, silent Soviet artillery batteries sat motionless behind field glasses without firing a shell.

Four days earlier in Old Town on Długa before crawling five hours bent double through chin-deep black human sewage in the storm mains beneath Krasiński Square guiding thirty-two blindfolded stretcher-boys on ropes, a phosphorus shell splinter bursting through a window sandbag had laid Sylwia's left temple open down to zygomatic periosteum—Halina had stitched it one-handed with unboiled linen thread on the cellar stairs—and when Sylwia washed crusted blood out of her cropped dark-chestnut hair in half a cup of well slush afterward, a single lock an inch wide rising back from that white temple scar grew in shock-white as winter snow at thirty-two, soon thirty-three years old.

Whenever the air turned thick enough with methane down in the four-foot brick sewer pipes that strong men tore off soaked shirts clawing at stone walls in claustrophobic madness—whenever German demolition squads dropped grenades and burning benzine drums down open street manholes ahead of her column—Sylwia slid two blistered, blood-caked fingers inside her torn blouse pocket, closed them tight around the sharp bullet-dent on the rim of a two-ounce silver Humboldt medallion, heard a furious Prussian baritone rasp *Do not dare die on me in those sewers, Kotarska!* straight inside her eardrum, bit blood from her lip, and dragged the boy behind her another hundred yards through the dark.

Four hundred miles west, inside solitary Cell Nineteen in the subterranean brick bowel of the Gestapo's special *Hausgefängnis* at Number Three Lehrter Straße in Moabit, former Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler had lost track of Tuesdays in February after the third week without daylight.

All summer of forty-four he had run ghosts on razor wire between Potsdam and Mauerwald. When Count von Stauffenberg's English clam-bomb blew four oak walls outward at Wolfsschanze on July twentieth and left Adolf Hitler screaming alive with charred trousers behind a heavy oak map-trestle—and Henning von Tresckow walked alone into no-man's-land near Białystok the next morning to pull the pin of a rifle grenade against his own chin rather than hand Kaltenbrunner names under torture—Ludwig had stayed underground inside Berlin six weeks longer burning courier lists and slipping hunted widows over the Swiss border, until a turned railway clerk spotted him near Savignyplatz on September nineteenth.

For five months inside Prinz-Albrecht-Straße and Lehrter Straße, RSHA *Sonderkommission 20. Juli* had worked steadily over him with iron bars and cold baths—breaking three ribs on his right side and cracking his left tibial plateau at the knee joint with a rifle-butt blow so the leg healed ten degrees stiff—hunting the Warsaw link to Tresckow's rail leaks. He had given them zero names, smiling faintly through split bloody teeth whenever an investigator screamed about Poland because hidden inside the stitched double-leather tongue of his left boot, completely missed even when guards took his belt and shoelaces, rested two inches of thin chased Mazovian silver willow-leaf.

Sentenced to hang by Judge Roland Freisler's screaming red-robed *Volksgerichtshof* on January thirtieth—execution delayed solely because on the morning of February third a thousand-pound American bomb dropped straight through the roof of the Bellevuestraße courtroom crushing Roland Freisler dead beneath a falling beam while gripping Ludwig's file folder—Ludwig sat chained wrist-to-wrist on frozen stone on the night of April twenty-fourth, nineteen-forty-five, listening to Marshal Zhukov's four thousand Russian heavy guns pulverize Berlin two miles north across Wedding.

At midnight, knowing Red Army T-34 tanks had already crossed the Spandau Canal, six drunken SS *Totenkopf* prison guards unlocked fifteen solitary doors on the cellar tier, marched fifteen starving, bearded skeletons out under flaming crimson sky into a ruined factory park off Invalidenstraße, and ordered them onto their knees beside a shell crater for neck-shots.

Ludwig refused to kneel on his stiff left knee.

As the SS corporal behind him cursed and raised a Walther toward the back of Ludwig's skull, a Russian one-hundred-fifty-two-millimeter howitzer shell screamed flat across Moabit rooftops and detonated with a blinding orange-white thunderclap ten yards right against a brick transformer wall—shredding four SS guards instantaneously in flying shrapnel and knocking the corporal sprawling half-stunned across Ludwig's boots!

Starved down to one hundred forty-eight pounds of scarred bone and whipcord tendon, Ludwig dropped both knees squarely onto the stunned SS corporal's chest in swirling red brick-smoke, looped eighteen inches of forged steel chain linking his own manacled wrists straight across the throat bearing silver runes, twisted his scarred forearms ninety degrees until hyoid cartilage snapped cleanly in the dark, pulled the dead corporal's ring of handcuff keys off his belt, unlocked his own bleeding wrists, pried his split left boot-tongue open to check that Cécile Kotarska's silver leaf was still safe, and limped westward through burning Berlin cellars toward British reconnaissance lines on the Elbe.

**Berlin — November 1, 1945**

All Saints' Day came cold and slate-grey down out of the Baltic across a dead imperial capital.

Along Unter den Linden—where not a single linden tree stood uncut for firewood between the shell-pocked pillars of the Brandenburg Gate and the headless bronze fragments of Prussian kings—columns of silent Berlin women in faded headscarves and wooden-soled shoes stood fifty deep in human chains across mountains of scorched brick, passing intact stones hand-to-hand to clear tramlines while rhythmic iron hammers clinked brick-mortar clean: *tink-tink, tink-tink, tink-tink*.

Painted Cyrillic directional boards at Friedrichstraße pointed east toward Marshal Zhukov's sector; fifty yards west, white-stenciled English and French military police signs marked the boundary of the British Tiergarten and French Wedding sectors along the green-brown water of the Spree.

At twelve minutes before noon, walking unhurriedly north along the cracked granite river embankment from a parked olive-drab Polish Red Cross Jeep carrying UNRRA displaced-children ledgers, came Dr. Sylwia Kotarska.

At thirty-four, in a belted dark-olive British Army surplus trenchcoat turned high against raw November river-wind and sensible flat leather boots muddied across six refugee camps between Lübeck and Magdeburg, men in uniform still turned their heads twice as she passed—and dropped their eyes respectfully when she looked back. High, clean Slavic cheekbones stood carved in pale ivory above a generous mouth no war had ever turned small; along her left temple ran a thin, two-inch white shrapnel line from where a pure, startling streak of snow-white hair swept back through heavy coils of dark chestnut sable.

Five hundred yards ahead across broken iron girders where engineers had laid temporary Bailey planking over the dropped center span of the Marschallbrücke loomed the gutted, fire-blackened colossal shell of the Reichstag, its glass dome blown to jagged iron ribs against low grey sky.

And four hundred yards downstream on the right, behind chipped wrought-iron railings half buried in fallen cornice stone, stood the bombed red-brick skeleton of **Reichstagsufer Number Six**.

Sylwia slowed five paces outside the twisted iron gates.

All the way from Warsaw via Poznań—nine months without one line of ink across severed frontiers, checking Allied survivor lists from Dachau, Oranienburg, Moabit, and Plötzensee where four thousand *20. Juli* officers and suspects had swung on meat-hooks or vanished into unmarked bomb craters—she had kept her surgeon's brain locked behind six inches of calm clinical ice: *Eighteen out of nineteen prisoners marched out of Lehrter Straße on the night of April twenty-fourth were shot behind Invalidenstraße, Frau Doktor,* a weary British major at Charlottenburg war-crimes registry had told her gently yesterday afternoon. *Only two bodies were never identified in the crater. Don't build castles on missing tags.*

Inside her right trenchcoat pocket, her bare, carbolic-scarred fingers closed so tightly around the bullet-dented rim of the silver Humboldt medallion that metal bit white crescents into her palm.

*Twelve noon sharp, First of November. If Berlin is twenty feet of bombed rubble, dig down to the paving stones.*

Across the river in Moabit, a cracked surviving Lutheran church bell began striking midday slowly through thin grey snow-flurries just beginning to drift down off the Spree:

*One... two... three...*

Stepping carefully over fallen brick lintels where gilt letters reading *COLLÈGE FRANÇAIS — FONDÉ EN 1689* lay snapped in half across wet weeds, Sylwia walked alone into the open, roofless inner courtyard of the *Französisches Gymnasium*.

Three walls still stood two stories high to open November sky; high overhead on the exposed second-floor interior plaster wall of what had once been *Obersekunda A*, a single slate blackboard hung miraculously crooked on one iron hook above vanished floorboards, washed grey by rain. Down in the weeds lay half a marble bust of Voltaire minus his nose, and over against the west brick wall stood the rusted cast-iron basin of the courtyard drinking fountain—bone-dry, frosted white at the rim—where a tall sixteen-year-old boy had looked down at her Warsaw satchel eighteen autumns ago and warned her the air along the Spree grew brisk by November.

*...ten... eleven... twelve.*

The final cracked bell-stroke died away into the slap of cold river water against barges and the distant *tink-tink* of rubble women two streets off.

The courtyard was empty.

Not a bootprint crossed the thin dusting of fresh November snow settling over broken red brick between the gate and the colonnade.

Sylwia stood motionless beside the dry cast-iron fountain. She did not lower her chin; she did not weep; only her lungs seemed abruptly unable to draw cold Berlin air past her ribs as eighteen years of fighting, hating, cutting, bleeding, and hoping came quietly to rest against silent stone. *You tried to walk back, kochanie,* she thought into the falling snow. *Even you couldn't walk through four Russian armies and meat-hooks.*

Behind the soot-blackened Doric stone colonnade along the far shadowed cloister gallery—sheltered out of the river wind where snow hadn't drifted—an iron ferrule tapped once, dry and crisp, on wet granite paving-stones.

*Tap... step... tap... step.*

Accompanied on the raw air by the sharp, unmistakable blue drift of cheap dark Russian *makhorka* shag rolled in newspaper.

Sylwia spun completely around so fast her olive trenchcoat flared above her boots, heart slamming so violently against her breastbone the roofless brick walls blurred white around the edges.

Stepping slowly out from behind the third blackened sandstone pillar into the drifting snow came a tall, lean man in a threadbare charcoal civilian overcoat two sizes too thin for winter, collar turned up around a gaunt, sharp-planed face pared down to pure hawk bone. His left leg swung slightly stiff from the knee, resting light weight at every second pace upon a plain ash walking-stick; silver threaded thick through cropped tarnished-gold hair at both temples; and just above the outer tail of his right eyebrow curved a thin, half-inch white crescent scar.

Twenty paces separated them across broken stone and falling snow.

Ludwig stopped, dropped the half-smoked twist of Russian tobacco beneath his worn heel, and studied her from head to boot—taking in the matching white scar and single snow-white streak sweeping back from her left temple, the proud tilt of her chin, and her bare right fist clenched white inside her pocket—while his winter-Baltic slate eyes blazed suddenly bright enough to melt every stone left in Prussia.

"You're two minutes late, Kotarska," Ludwig Schoeler said across the ruins in crisp, maddeningly dry Charlottenburg German, though his baritone cracked rough down the middle on the final word. "Herr Doktor Glass would dock you half a mark."

A sound broke out of Sylwia's throat—half choked sob, half fierce, breathless, incandescent laugh, the first true laugh that had left her ribs since nineteen-thirty-nine—as twenty paces of broken empire vanished beneath her running boots.

Ash stick clattered forgotten onto granite stones as both his lean arms caught her full against his chest, lifting her clear off her feet for one dizzying turn despite his stiff knee before setting her down pinned tight from collarbone to hip against warm, living, breathing wool and bone, burying his cold face deep into the snow-dusted curve of her neck while her bare hands tangled wildly in silver-streaked golden hair, touching ears, jaw, cheekbones, ribs—*real, warm, alive, not cellar fever, not dead tags in Invalidenstraße*—before dragging his mouth down onto hers.

He tasted of terrible Russian tobacco, snowflakes, iron willpower, and home.

When they finally tore apart two inches solely to breathe white clouds into each other's faces, foreheads resting hard together beneath the roofless sky where *Obersekunda A*'s crooked blackboard watched silently overhead, Ludwig reached inside his threadbare dark overcoat with scarred fingers, drew out Cécile Moreau-Kotarska's gleaming silver Mazovian willow-leaf clasp—polished bright on prison wool through three hundred nights—and pinned back the single shock-white lock at her scarred left temple with steady, reverent care.

Sylwia opened her aching right hand, drew out the bullet-dented two-ounce silver Humboldt *Primus Omnium* medallion, and dropped it straight back into his breast pocket over his healed left subclavian artery.

"We can't saw silver in half without losing weight," Sylwia whispered unevenly, grey eyes shining clear as sunlit slate six inches from his, "so we'll have to keep it in the same house. Where are you going, Schoeler? You look like three bad meals away from rickets, and that left knee needs passive flexion before fibrous ankylosis locks the patella."

Looking down at her across eighteen years and two burned capitals—not one pfennig of Dahlem silver left to his name, East Prussia gone under Russian plows, Warsaw lying in brick mountains waiting to be rebuilt brick by brick by stubborn hands like hers, and eighty years or eighty days ahead of them belonging to no general, no flag, and no man's mercy save their own—Ludwig Schoeler smiled: clean, young, and completely unconquered.

"Wherever your next operating table stands, *Frau Doktor*," Ludwig said, lacing his scarred right fingers tight through hers and picking up his ash stick with his left as snow thickened gently over the Spree. "Provided you still have that Polish pocket grammar. Lead on."

**THE END**
