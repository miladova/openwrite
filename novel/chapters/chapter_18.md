# CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

## Into the Ruins

**Warsaw — January 23–31, 1944**

Fifteen minutes inside wet wool at sixteen below zero kills faster than nine-millimeter lead.

Even inside the roofless brick shell of the Philharmonic Hall on Jasna, Sylwia's skirt and coat from waist to ankles had already frozen into stiff, crackling boards of foul Lindleya sewer ice; violent, involuntary shivers racked her shoulders so hard her teeth clicked despite every ounce of surgical self-control she possessed.

Releasing her only long enough to strip off his own dry, heavy charcoal loden overcoat, Ludwig wrapped the sun-warmed wool double around her trembling shoulders over her ruined coat, knelt two seconds beside the dead Saxon SD troopers to yank both short-barreled Bergmann MP-35 submachine guns, four loaded thirty-two-round curved magazines, and their stamped *Sicherheitspolizei* silver warrant disks off their belts, and caught Sylwia's freezing bare hand inside his wool-gloved fist.

"Can you run six blocks west if we stay off Marszałkowska?" he whispered close to her ear, white breath mingling under icy stars as distant police sirens wailed again south toward Nowogrodzka.

"I can run to the Vistula before I freeze," Sylwia whispered back through blue lips—and only then added in three ragged breaths what waited behind them: "Tadeusz Majewski is dead. Władek is dead. Halina slammed the boiler sewer hatch down over my head from the hospital side when a steam pipe burst so Jacek and Staś and I got clear—Reinhardt has Halina."

Ludwig's hand tightened once around hers—not wasting breath on empty sorrow when blood was still freezing on their skin. "And Reinhardt took Viktor Brandt off the midnight Königsberg platform at Dworzec Główny. Which means tonight every address either of us ever slept under is burned to ash. Only one roof left in Warsaw, Sylwia. Move."

Keeping to bombed courtyard passages, ruined fire-walls, and rubble-choked alleys west across Bagno and Grzybowska—twice flattening motionless into black coal-arch shadows while green *Ordnungspolizei* patrol trucks rumbled past on chained tires sweeping searchlights across frosted brick—they crossed Żelazna at two-ten in the morning and slipped up the creaking dark timber stairs behind ulica Chłodna's shuttered feather-bed warehouse.

When Ludwig rapped Słowik's night-distress cadence on the steel-plated oak, forty sleepy canaries stirred rustling behind crimson velvet inside, a brass spy-hole slid open an eighth of an inch, and three heavy iron bolts slammed back inside four seconds.

Ignacy Słowik—barefoot inside fur-lined Persian slippers, nightshirt stuffed hurriedly into striped trousers under his moth-eaten astrakhan smoking jacket, holding a cocked pre-war Mauser C96 broomhandle pistol steady in one plump ringed hand—gaped for exactly one heartbeat as Warsaw's ice-caked woman surgeon stumbled across his threshold supported by a tall, blood-streaked German staff officer in shirt-sleeves carrying two captured Gestapo submachine guns.

Then *The Nightingale* bolted all three steel bars shut behind them, holstered the Mauser inside his velvet pocket, and shouted zero questions.

"Merciful Mother of Częstochowa, don't drip sewer water onto my Tabriz, come straight to the tile stove!" Słowik hissed rapidly, kicking the firebox draft wide until anthracite roared cherry-red and splashing four generous fingers of seventy-proof Łącko plum *śliwowica* into two Sèvres teacups. "Drink—all of it, down the hatch before pneumonia settles in your pleura, Pani Doktor! And you—Herr Oberstleutnant, I presume, from the aristocratic nose and those boots—sit before you bleed on my ottoman! Half of Aleja Szucha rolled out in armor at midnight; my boy at the switchboard says Reinhardt just put an all-points *Sonderfahndung* flash on the Berlin and Cracow wires for both your heads!"

Ludwig tossed two heavy gold British sovereigns onto Słowik's boulle desk. "We can't stay under your canaries ten minutes, Herr Słowik, or we hang you with us. Where is a hole in Warsaw without rats on two legs for six days?"

Słowik looked down at the two gleaming gold coins, then across at Sylwia shivering violently with both bleeding-cold hands wrapped around the porcelain teacup. Walking to his velvet desk drawer, the fat impresario unlocked the false bottom, lifted out Cécile Moreau-Kotarska's square-cut Belle Époque Colombian emerald brooch in its twist of tissue paper, and dropped both Ludwig's two gold sovereigns *and* the emerald brooch straight into Sylwia's coat pocket.

"Ignacy Słowik fleeces fat Quartermasters and Bavarian policemen, Herr Oberstleutnant," Słowik said with sudden, magnificent quiet dignity beneath his waxed theatrical mustache. "People who slit *Volksdeutsche* throats in Sienna Alley and make Otto Reinhardt chase dead rats around Wilcza drink on the house. Put on my cousin's sheepskin carter's coat from that wardrobe—and follow me across the street before the three-o'clock patrol comes down Chłodna."

Five minutes later, laden with two heavy canvas rucksacks Słowik packed swiftly with tinned Danish butter, smoked horse sausage, hardtack, methylated spirit cans, clean woolens, and surgical dressings, they slipped out a rear courtyard postern, waited sixty seconds until the lone *Schutzpolizei* sentry stamping past the corner of Żelazna turned his back into the wind, and sprinted crouched across ulica Leszno—diving straight through a jagged three-foot shell breach in the ten-foot red-brick boundary wall of the former Warsaw Ghetto.

Beyond that brick breach, time and human habitation ended.

Sylwia had smelled the smoke from Pavilion Three's tall windows in April and May when the northern sky burned copper-black for four weeks and gray ash snowed onto her sterile instrument trays; she had never stood inside it.

Under cold, glittering January constellations stretched nearly four square miles of utter, silent, lunar annihilation.

Not one intact building stood upright as far as eyesight carried toward Muranów and Dzielna. Dynamite squads and flame-throwers had collapsed five hundred city streets into rolling twenty-foot mountain ranges of pulverized red brick, twisted iron bedframes, rusted sewing machines, and charred roof-beams buried beneath smooth white snowdrifts. Four hundred thousand human beings had lived, argued, studied, wept, and prayed here twenty months ago; tonight not a dog barked, not a window gleamed, and neither superstitious German auxiliary patrols—who whispered of typhus spores and armed Jewish ghosts lurking in rubble shafts—nor Polish looters dared set boot fifty yards inside the wire after sundown.

Threading unerringly through chest-high snowed rubble by landmark chimneys for three hundred yards north, Słowik ducked beneath the collapsed granite arch of a burned eighteenth-century kosher wine-cellar and leather-currier's yard, brushed unbroken snow off a tilted slab of boiler-plate iron with a dead branch to leave no broom marks behind them, hauled open a greased trapdoor sunk into intact subterranean masonry, and guided them down twelve dry stone steps before pulling a counterweighted rubble camouflage tray smoothly shut overhead.

When Słowik struck a match to a blue-flame denatured-alcohol spirit stove—burners chosen specifically because alcohol threw zero smoke and zero smell up the hidden two-inch periscope ventilation pipe emerging through a heap of burned stovepipes above—Sylwia looked around in silent astonishment.

Twenty feet below the frozen desert of dead brick arched a dry, barrel-vaulted stone chamber twelve feet by sixteen, built in King Stanisław August's reign with walls four feet thick. Wooden pallets raised off dry sand held thick Zakopane sheepskin pelts and clean wool army blankets; shelves stacked along the rear arch held twenty-liter glass carboys of boiled water, tinned sardines, hard biscuit, candles, two spare Mauser carbines, a copper wash-boiler, and six bottles of French Martell cognac.

"My personal Noah's Ark," Słowik murmured, setting down his rucksack and checking the thermometer on the stone pier: four degrees above freezing already, climbing steadily as two brass alcohol stoves purred blue and hot. "Prepared in forty-two when gentlemen in black leather began asking impolite questions about my grandmother's maiden name in Lublin. Nobody on God's earth knows this stone exists save me and you two. Keep inside during daylight; I return Friday after midnight with news of Nurse Nowak, Hauptmann Brandt, and Captain *Grot*."

Pausing on the third stone step with his hand on the inner iron bar-latch instructions, Słowik looked back down once at the Prussian colonel and the Polish surgeon standing together in the blue spirit-glow.

"May heaven forgive my taste," murmured *The Nightingale* softly, "you two deserve either a firing squad or fifty years together—possibly both. Keep warm."

The overhead iron slab thudded softly home; exterior counterweights clicked; snow whispered back across the seam.

Silence—deeper than anything left on earth above ground, vast and entombed beneath four miles of silent graves—closed around them.

Sylwia stood motionless in the center of the stone vault, trying to unbutton her frozen coat with purple, wooden fingers that refused to bend at the middle joints. Suddenly the subterranean warmth hit skin numb since midnight, setting off excruciating pins-and-needles agony up both legs while violent hypothermia shivers seized her whole torso at once.

"Stand still—don't fight your fingers," Ludwig said quietly, setting down both submachine guns on the dry crate and filling the wide copper kettle from a water carboy over both roaring blue alcohol rings before stepping directly in front of her.

Unfastening his borrowed carter's sheepskin, he peeled away first his dry loden coat, then her frozen, foul-smelling charcoal street coat, unbuttoning her ice-stiffened wool skirt, torn blouse, chemise, and crusted stockings piece by piece—lifting each bare icy foot onto his thigh to pull off sodden leather shoes and chafing blue-white toes briskly between both warm calloused palms until fierce pink arterial sting flooded back and drew a sharp hiss between her teeth. Wrapping two thick white Zakopane sheepskin fleeces around her naked trembling body on the pallet closest to the blue stoves, Ludwig waited five minutes until the copper kettle steamed, poured hot water into an enamel tub with half a bar of Słowik's Marseilles olive-oil soap, knelt on the sand beside her pallet in his unbuttoned wool shirt, and washed her from forehead to ankles.

Never as long as Sylwia lived would any luxury on earth touch that copper basin twenty feet underground.

With steady, infinitely patient hands—hands that thirty minutes earlier had snapped a man's radius and driven eight inches of Solingen steel into two throats without raising his pulse past ninety—Ludwig sponged frozen black sewer silt, brick dust, and the invisible phantom smear of Tadeusz Majewski's and Władek's death off her shins, her thighs, her belly, her ribs, unpinning her damp, soot-tangled dark chestnut hair and pouring warm soapy water cup by cup over her bowed nape into the tub until clean water ran clear and her skin smelled solely of olive soap and warm wool.

"Your turn," Sylwia whispered once colour returned to her lips, refusing to stay bundled passive while dried dark blood crusted the ripped left seam of his dark wool shirt.

Pulling the sheepskin snug around her bare shoulders like a cloak, kneeling upright on the pallet before him as he stripped his torn shirt off over his head, she caught her breath softly in the blue-gold candle glow—he had lit two tallow candles now on the crate beside the Martell bottle—examining the four-inch crimson furrow scored diagonally across his left eighth rib six inches below the healed white star of her October chest-tube scar.

The nine-millimeter jacket had grooved skin and superficial external oblique fascia without cracking bone or entering pleura, though black unburned powder grains peppered the raw edges. Pouring Słowik's French Martell cognac straight over sterile gauze because alcohol stung cleaner than weak iodine, Sylwia scrubbed every black powder grain out of live muscle—Ludwig sat stone-still on the pallet edge, warm amber candle-light sculpting sweat-sheened pectoral muscle and the flat scar from Kursk on his right flank, watching only her downcast lashes—before laying four neat fine-silk skin stitches along his ribcage with fingers rock-steady once more.

Snipping the final thread, Sylwia didn't drop her hands from his bare ribs.

Looking down at those four tiny black silk knots sitting six inches below her October subclavian scar—remembering old Professor Majewski's shattered gold pince-nez lying in blood at the top of the stairs two hours ago, remembering Halina's dark eyes shining through scalding white steam half a second before iron slammed shut—everything locked behind Sylwia Kotarska's breastbone since September nineteen-thirty-nine cracked silently wide open.

No loud sobbing; she simply leaned her forehead straight forward against Ludwig's bare sternum between his collarbones, shoulders shaking hard and silent as scalding tears spilled unchecked down across his skin.

"Tadeusz stood right in front of their guns quoting Sanitary Paragraph Nine in Vienna German so two boys could climb down iron rungs," she whispered raggedly against his chest, fingers curling tight around his bare ribs. "Fifty-eight years old—hands shook too badly for vascular work anymore, yet every morning he shared half his bread soup with child wards. And Halina—God in heaven, Ludwig, Halina pushed that iron lid shut over her own head from the inside so they wouldn't follow wet footprints down the ladder! Right now Reinhardt has her in chains because of *me*—"

"Because of Reinhardt," Ludwig said fiercely, wrapping both bare arms hard around her sheepskin-draped shoulders and lifting her effortlessly sideways onto his lap on the wide pallet so her bare legs tucked warm beneath the fleece against his thighs, pressing his cheek tight against her damp hair. "Listen to me, Sylwia—we aren't across any border yet, and Tuesday hasn't dawned. Until we see corpses, neither Halina Nowak nor Viktor Brandt is dead."

For a long minute she breathed raggedly against the warm pulse in his neck, letting his arms hold the shattered pieces of tonight together—before lifting her tear-wet grey eyes three inches from his in the quiet candle-glow, stripped so utterly bare of defenses down here beneath four hundred thousand dead that even the last unspeakable secret left between them refused to stay buried.

"On the night of October nineteenth," Sylwia whispered, voice trembling once and steadying into terrible honesty, "when Brandt carried you into Theater One and Halina wiped the soot off that scar over your eyebrow... I had my gloved left index finger and thumb pinched directly around your torn subclavian artery behind the broken bone."

Ludwig didn't move a muscle; his slate-blue eyes held hers in total stillness.

"Three full minutes before your sergeant ran upstairs with Reinhardt's threat about Pawiak hostages," she confessed starkly, holding up her bare, scarred right and left hands between their faces in the amber light, "nobody in that room could see five centimeters deep into the blood pool. One two-millimeter shift of my finger against the sharp bone spike would have torn the parent trunk wide open. Forty seconds to empty your heart; no coroner on earth could have proved murder. For six heartbeats—looking down at the face I thought threw ten marks at my feet at Wannsee, wearing the uniform that burned my country and killed my father—I held your artery against that jagged bone edge and weighed letting go."

Silence hummed soft around the blue alcohol flames.

Slowly, Ludwig reached up with both hands, took her bare left hand—the hand that had clamped his artery in the dark, and whose inner elbow still bore the faint violet mark of four hundred fifty milliliters pumped straight into his dying veins—turned it palm-upward between them, and pressed his open mouth reverently, lingeringly, dead center into the hollow of her palm.

"I know," Ludwig murmured against her skin, lifting dark-slate eyes luminous with something deeper and fiercer than forgiveness. "Why do you think I woke up cursing at four in the morning refusing your morphine, Sylwia? Even miles down under shock and ether, some animal part of me heard your voice bark at Brandt in Charlottenburg German—knew *exactly* whose hand was inside my ribs holding my heart—and refused to die before looking at you again."

His thumb traced slowly across her lifelines before his own face darkened, stripping away his final iron plate in answer to hers.

"You weighed six seconds on an operating table against an armed invader," Ludwig said very quietly. "In November forty-one, outside Borisov on the Berezina highway, my reconnaissance staff car was blocked twenty minutes at a forest rail crossing while an *SS-Einsatzkommando* auxiliary battalion finished clearing a ghetto ditch two hundred meters off the road in fifteen-below frost. Women stripping coats off three-year-old children and holding them against their chests so one rifle bullet killed both together cleanly. I had a pistol on my belt and six staff riders behind me; the SS major outranked me by zero and had three hundred rifles. I sat upright on leather upholstery, wrote a blistering four-page formal protest to Field Marshal von Bock's headquarters that went straight into a wastebasket, and drove past without firing a shot."

His jaw set like carved stone in the candlelight, pale eyes staring straight into hers without blinking or begging absolution.

"That afternoon outside Borisov," Ludwig whispered, "is when Ludwig Schoeler of Dahlem died, Sylwia—and when I swore to Tresckow at Smolensk that if I had to burn sixty-four years of Prussian oaths to ash and hang from a butcher's hook in Berlin to put Adolf Hitler and Heinrich Himmler six feet underground, I'd light the fuse with my own teeth. If God exists above those bricks overhead, you had every right on earth to let that artery tear on October nineteenth. Every breath since belongs to you."

Reaching up with both bare hands to frame his lean, stubbled jaws, Sylwia pulled his mouth straight down onto hers—kissing away Borisov, kissing away Aleja Szucha's cellar, kissing away thirteen wasted winters while the heavy white sheepskin slipped silently off her bare shoulders onto the pallet behind her.

Down here under twenty feet of stone and snow, with no German uniform left on a chair and no clock ticking toward ward rounds, urgency dissolved into something vast, slow, sacred, and unbearably intense.

Pushing Ludwig gently flat onto his back across the wide double spread of white Zakopane sheepskins—careful even in blind hunger of the four fresh silk stitches gleaming along his left eighth rib—Sylwia unbuttoned his heavy wool trousers herself with steady hands, tugging boots, wool socks, and dark cloth cleanly away until both of them lay completely naked in the warm golden circle of candle and blue spirit-fire.

For long, unhurried minutes she worshiped every scar war had written across his lean, powerful six-foot-two frame as though claiming contested territory back inch by inch with her own mouth: pressing open, lingering kisses first to the crescent scar she herself had cut above his right eyebrow at seventeen; down across his firm throat to the pale pink suture line and hard bone-callus beneath his left collarbone; carefully circling the fresh white gauze strip over tonight's bullet graze on his left ribs; across his flat, hard-muscled belly to the livid purple Kursk shrapnel crescent low on his right hipbone—and lower still, sliding down between his parted muscular thighs on soft fleece until her warm fingers wrapped around the heavy, pulsing shaft already rigid against his groin and she took the smooth, velvety-hot crown straight between her parted lips.

Ludwig sucked in a sharp, ragged hiss through clenched teeth, head thrown back into white fleece, right hand tangling deep and reverently in the heavy dark river of chestnut hair spilling across his bare thighs as Sylwia took him deeper—slow, wet, shameless hollowed suction down the full scorching thickness of him while her tongue traced the sensitive vein along the underside and her left palm cupped the tight weight at his base.

"Sylwia—stop, *meine Liebe*, come here before you finish me in ten seconds—" he groaned rawly inside two minutes, catching her bare shoulders with both strong hands to draw her smoothly upward—rolling her smoothly flat onto her spine beneath him across the thick white furs, bracing his weight cleanly on strong forearms and knees so his stitched left rib touched nothing while he parted her bare pale thighs wide to the candlelight.

Dropping his head first between her breasts—sucking one dark rose nipple, then the other hard between teeth and tongue until her back arched off the white wool—he kissed slowly down the center line of her taut, narrow ribs and trembling navel straight into the soft dark curls between her legs, spreading wet, swollen labia open with both thumbs and licking deep inside her entrance before fastening his hot mouth squarely over her throbbing clitoris.

Down behind four-foot stone walls beneath an empty city, for the first time since they were born, **Sylwia did not have to muffle her voice.**

When two long fingers slid deep inside her drenched heat and curled hard upward in rhythm with the ruthless, flickering suction of his tongue, her cry rang out low, wild, and completely unchained off the eighteenth-century stone arch overhead—*"Ludwig—kochanie, teraz, wejdź we mnie—!"*—polish words tearing loose out of pure delirium as waves broke hot across her pelvis.

Rising straight up between her wide-parted knees in the golden candlelight, catching both her bare ankles briefly to hook her long pale legs high around his lean waist, Ludwig guided himself home and sank deep—one slow, continuous, devastatingly full thrust that pressed every bare inch of chest, belly, and groin skin-to-skin from breastbone to thighs, burying his face briefly in her neck before lifting himself six inches on braced elbows to lock his dark-dilated Baltic eyes squarely into her storm-grey gaze.

"Don't close your eyes," he whispered fiercely, echoing her own words back to her as he began moving inside her—deep, slow, grinding strokes that rocked her whole body upward into thick white sheepskin fleece, pulling back almost to the swollen rim before driving slow and heavy all the way back home until her inner walls stretched tight around every ridge and vein. "Look at me, Sylwia. Whoever comes through doors tomorrow—right here, tonight, nothing else exists."

Holding his gaze wide open through blurred, shining lashes, framing his lean stubbled cheeks between both scarred surgeon's hands while her heels locked tight behind his muscled buttocks pulling him deeper still, Sylwia met every thrust hip for hip—slow fire building hotter and higher without rushing, skin gleaming gold with sweat in the warm vault, breath breaking into shared ragged gasps against bruised mouths as pace finally tore free into hard, driving, primal rhythm, bed-pallets creaking beneath sheepskins until both of them shattered in the exact same blinding instant: Sylwia crying his name aloud against the vaulted stone as violent spasms milked him dry and Ludwig burying his mouth hard against the hollow of her throat with a deep, shuddering groan, pouring every drop of strength and life he owned deep inside her before collapsing carefully onto his right side with her tangled naked and breathless inside both arms.

For seven suspended days and nights at the end of January while Warsaw froze above them under martial cordons, the stone vault beneath Leszno remained an island outside time.

On the night of Sunday, January thirtieth, at half past one in the morning, three sharp iron scrapes sounded on the overhead counterweight hatch.

Ludwig stood silent in the dark rear shadow with a cocked Bergmann submachine gun leveled waist-high until Ignacy Słowik's plump boots appeared down the stone steps—followed immediately by a second man whose blindfold Słowik whipped off at the bottom step: lean, unshaven, blue Modlin powder-scar livid on his frozen temple.

Marek Zawadzki.

For five full seconds across twelve feet of yellow lantern light, Captain *Grot* of the Polish Home Army and Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler of the German General Staff looked straight into each other's eyes without blinking.

Neither reached for iron; neither offered a hand. Two professional wolves from opposite ends of the forest weighed bone, scar tissue, posture, and nerve in dead silence—Marek taking in the bare white bandage glimpsed beneath Ludwig's open wool collar and the quiet, unmistakable way Ludwig stood half a step nearer Sylwia's shoulder without touching her; Ludwig reading every hard, honorable line of the Polish engineer who had kept Sylwia alive in forty and refused to shoot her unread in Stare Miasto.

"Captain," Ludwig inclined his head one stark inch in clean soldier's acknowledgment.

"Colonel," Marek returned flatly—and turned straight to business, spreading a hand-drawn architectural street plan across the ammunition crate between them.

"Both prisoners are still alive as of twenty-two hundred hours tonight," Marek reported briskly, glancing once into Sylwia's fierce, shining eyes to give her Halina first. "Not in Pawiak, and not at Twenty-Five Aleja Szucha. Reinhardt is holding both Hauptmann Brandt and Nurse Halina Nowak inside the reinforced coal-cellar cells of Referat Four's private safe-villa at **Number Eight ulica Litewska**—two hundred meters east of Aleje Ujazdowskie. Our inside scrubwoman spotted Halina alive with one broken collarbone and Brandt with his left eye swollen shut—neither has cracked."

Ludwig's jaw tightened grimly with pride for scarred Viktor Brandt. "Guard strength at Eight Litewska?"

"Lighter than Szucha—eight SD guards normally, twelve tonight—because Reinhardt leaves Okęcie airfield for Berlin RSHA at **twelve noon sharp on Tuesday, February first**," Słowik chimed in, setting down a heavy canvas tailor's bundle onto the pallet alongside a flat leather forger's folder. "My clerk saw the flight manifest an hour ago! Reinhardt flies to Kaltenbrunner personally at noon Tuesday carrying Hauptmann Brandt drugged in irons *and* the black leather *Kronenbuch* from Litewska's cellar safe—leaving standing orders for Krauss to shoot Nurse Nowak at noon and launch *Aktion Wintersturm* across forty-two Warsaw addresses at two PM!"

Silence crackled around the lantern. Thirty-four hours left.

"Attacking Eight Litewska head-on in daylight two blocks from SS Police Headquarters on Aleje Ujazdowskie is suicide under normal conditions—thirty armored cars respond inside ninety seconds," Ludwig assessed instantly, eyes narrowing over Marek's street sketch. "At night they double-bolt steel shutters and wire an open alarm line straight to Szucha."

Marek allowed himself a tiny, grim, terrifyingly fierce wolf's smile in the yellow light.

"Who spoke of normal conditions?" Marek asked quietly. "Listen carefully—even telling you the clock window risks court-martial, Kora, save that Rawicz authorized one blind asset play to get that *Kronenbuch*. On **Tuesday morning, February first, between zero-nine-zero-five and zero-nine-fifteen**, something catastrophic is going to detonate right outside SS and Police Headquarters on Aleje Ujazdowskie three hundred yards north of Litewska."

Marek didn't name Franz Kutschera—no cell outside *Pegaz* knew the name until bullets flew—yet Ludwig's razor-sharp tactical eye leaped across the map in half a heartbeat.

"High-level hit on Ujazdowskie at nine-ten," Ludwig breathed, slate-blue eyes lighting cold and brilliant as drawn steel. "Every SS siren, armored car, and standby platoon from Szucha and Ujazdowskie will stampede north to that single block inside two minutes—leaving Litewska's guards rubbernecking toward gunfire in total command-and-communications chaos for twenty minutes!"

"Long enough," Słowik beamed, untying the canvas tailor's bundle with dramatic flourish to reveal Ludwig's immaculate spare field-grey General Staff Oberstleutnant's greatcoat, crimson-tabbed tunic, peaked cap, and polished riding boots—rescued Monday from his German military tailor on Nowy Świat before SD clerks checked laundry tickets—alongside a crisp *Deutsches Rotes Kreuz* sister's grey wool cape and white head-veil for Sylwia, and freshly forged red-bordered RSHA Berlin emergency prisoner-transfer warrants stamped five minutes dry! "Walk up to the front gate of Eight Litewska at nine-twelve AM in full Generalstab crimson shouting that Ujazdowskie is under terrorist attack and Berlin orders immediate evacuation of Referat Four's cellar safe and prisoners to Okęcie airfield—while Captain *Grot* and four Kedyw boys hit the rear garden coal-hatch with silenced Stens!"

Marek looked straight across the crate past Sylwia into Ludwig Schoeler's eyes.

"Once that front sentry spots your face against last week's *Fahndungsbefehl* circular, Colonel, you'll have about four seconds before lead flies," Marek said bluntly. "Get Halina and that black ledger out alive, and at Dworzec Wschodni tomorrow night nobody in my squad checks two Swedish timber passes boarding the Danzig express. Cross us by half an inch inside Litewska, and my fourth Sten magazine has your name on every brass casing."

"Fair arithmetic, Captain," Ludwig said calmly, picking up the crimson-tabbed General Staff tunic and running one steady thumb across the silver bullion eagle. "Make sure your Sten magazines aren't greased too thick for frost this time. Nine-ten Tuesday morning."
