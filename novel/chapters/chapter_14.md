# CHAPTER FOURTEEN

## Christmas in Ruins

**Warsaw — December 24, 1943**

When the first pale silver winter star pricked through gunmetal clouds above the ruined chimneys of Nowogrodzka at four-ten on Christmas Eve afternoon, Pavilion Three smelled of boiled red beets, dried mushrooms, carbolic acid, and pine needles.

Nobody owned an intact fir tree; old Władek had lopped four resinous green boughs off a cemetery spruce in Powązki and wired them above the scrubbed zinc instrument table in Minor Dressing, where Professor Tadeusz Majewski stood in a brushed black pre-war waistcoat holding a chipped porcelain saucer piled with paper-thin grey rectangles of unleavened *opłatek* wafer.

Eleven people stood in a quiet circle around the zinc table while blackout blinds shut out twenty-below frost: Majewski, Halina Nowak, two night sisters in starched wimples, one-armed fourteen-year-old Staś propped proudly in an oversize hospital bathrobe, Władek in clean collarless linen, three convalescent tramway workers, Dr. Roman Karski hanging ghost-pale near the radiator shadow, and Sylwia. Down in the brick niche behind the typhus boilers—where Halina would slip a warm cup of clear beet *barszcz* ten minutes from now—nineteen-year-old Jacek Nowak's typhus fever had broken cleanly at dawn on Day Ten, his sutured ileum passing gas and broth without a drop of leakage.

At the end of the table stood a single empty wooden chair draped with a clean white surgical towel before an untouched tin plate—the ancient Polish *Wigilia* place set for the wandering stranger, the imprisoned, and the dead who could not come home.

Every pair of eyes in the room touched that empty white chair and named its own ghosts in silence.

Majewski's thin, blue-veined hand trembled slightly as he broke off a corner of Sylwia's wafer and kissed both her lean cheeks. "That next Christmas finds Warsaw free, Pani Sylwia," the old professor murmured huskily, "and your hands still healing the living rather than sewing up the shot."

"And peace under your roof, Pan Profesor," Sylwia answered softly, breaking his wafer in turn before sharing hers with Halina—whose dark eyes shone luminous with unshed gratitude for the boy breathing quietly downstairs—and finally stepping across to Roman Karski by the radiator.

As Sylwia held out her grey wafer fragment toward him, Karski flinched microscopically before breaking off a tiny crumb with ice-cold, clammy fingers that avoided brushing hers. He couldn't meet her eyes; his gaze darted toward the empty white chair and back down to his shoes.

*"Zdrowia i spokoju, Roman,"* Sylwia wished him quietly—*Health and peace*—watching him swallow dryly as though unleavened flour choked his windpipe before he muttered an excuse about checking casualty admissions and slipped out into the dim corridor.

Forty-five minutes earlier, walking back from an emergency domiciliary call in Wola along the snow-choked lime avenue of Powązki Cemetery while early dusk turned ten thousand stone crosses blue-violet, Sylwia had heard dry snow crunch behind a granite mausoleum and turned to face Marek Zawadzki.

Carrying a wreath of frozen boxwood as cemetery cover, collar crusted white with rime from his own breath, Marek hadn't wasted ten seconds on holiday greetings.

"Rawicz convened sector security at noon today," Marek had told her, standing two paces off among snow-drifted graves while ravens cawed hoarsely in bare oaks overhead. "Twenty days have passed since we gave you those two ampoules on Żurawia, Sylwia. Schoeler isn't bedridden; our street spotters photographed him walking unassisted out of the Town Major's headquarters on Krakowskie Przedmieście yesterday morning without even a sling."

"I reported through Halina last week that Brandt seals every food tray and locks his medical cabinet," Sylwia replied calmly, cold air burning clean down her trachea. "And the Kampinos sweep map I pulled out of his study saved forty of *Grzyb*'s forest boys on December seventh, plus two Brest ammunition trains London bombed at Terespol."

"Which is the sole reason Rawicz hasn't convened a court-martial already," Marek snapped grimly, knocking snow off a marble plinth with his gloved fist. "Listen to me carefully—and don't interrupt, because what I am about to put in your ears carries your life on its back. Counter-Intelligence has taken Schoeler's liquidation out of your hands."

Inside Sylwia's wool gloves, every finger went cold as cemetery marble. Outwardly she didn't blink. "Poison failed, so he goes back to street shooting under Kutschera's hundred-for-one posters?"

"Special Kedyw six-man team—veterans of the Bürkl hit, three British Stens, two Gammon plastic charges, backup getaway car on Koszykowa," Marek said in flat, inexorable stone-cutter's Polish. "Monday morning, December twenty-seventh, at zero-eight-forty-five sharp. Every Monday and Thursday morning at eight-forty, Schoeler's Mercedes turns north off Aleja Róż onto Aleje Ujazdowskie and slows to second gear at the ulica Piękna corner where municipal snowbanks narrow the tramway."

Sylwia stared straight at the blue powder-burn scar on Marek's left temple—and saw the whole second trap yawn black beneath the snow before he even spoke the next sentence.

*Why on earth would an underground sector commander tell a suspected surgeon the exact street corner and minute of an upcoming Kedyw ambush?*

Marek's dark, tormented eyes met hers squarely across the frozen grave. He couldn't lie to her to save his soul.

"Nobody outside Rawicz, the assault lieutenant, and me knows Piękna at eight-forty-five," Marek said quietly, voice dropping raw with warning and grief. "Not the drivers until seven Monday morning; not Halina. Rawicz ordered me personally to brief you on the corner and hour this afternoon—ostensibly so you have emergency plasma ready at Lindleya if one of our boys takes a bullet pulling back along Koszykowa."

"And actually," Sylwia finished in a whisper colder than twenty below, "as a barium meal of Rawicz's own."

Marek didn't deny it. His jaw muscles bunched white beneath dark stubble. "If Schoeler cancels his Monday morning run to Krakowskie Przedmieście, switches streets to Marszałkowska, adds an armored scout car, or stays in bed with a convenient fever—Rawicz signs a *Wojskowy Sąd Specjalny* field execution warrant on you at nine-fifteen Monday morning, Sylwia, and sends two men I don't command to carry it out."

Stepping one stride closer between snow-buried iron grave-railings, Marek gripped her wool coat sleeve briefly—desperate, fierce, heartbreakingly loyal to the woman he thought he remembered from forty.

"Stay inside Pavilion Three from seven o'clock Monday morning, Sylwia," he whispered hoarsely. "Don't go near Aleja Róż after Sunday evening. Let six Stens finish what we missed on October nineteenth, and by Monday noon your slate with *Oddział II* is washed white forever. Swear to me you'll stay clear."

"I'll have plasma ready in Minor Dressing at eight-fifty," Sylwia had answered evenly, pulling free and walking down the long white avenue of graves without looking back while sixty-five hours began ticking steadily downward behind her ribs.

***

Two miles southeast at seven o'clock that same Christmas Eve, silver-tinsel swastikas glittered atop a twenty-foot Carpathian spruce inside the overheated crystal ballroom of the *Deutsches Haus* casino on Aleje Ujazdowskie, where eighty Wehrmacht, Luftwaffe, and civil administration officers sang *O Tannenbaum* over steamed carp and looted Reims Veuve Clicquot as loudly as men whistling past an open grave.

Standing near the tall frosted terrace doors holding an untouched glass of dry Sekt so hostesses wouldn't pester him, immaculate in field-grey with crimson collar tabs and the dented silver Humboldt medal riding silently inside his left breast pocket, Ludwig Schoeler watched Germany rot under electric candles.

Over by the buffet, fat SS-Doktor Krantz was drunkenly demonstrating tank pincers with breadsticks to two blonde Reich-post telephone girls; ten yards left, an artillery colonel freshly transferred off the Kiev salient stared blankly into his champagne glass with the grey, hollowed thousand-yard tremor of a man who had just watched sixty burned eighteen-year-old replacements frozen stiff into Ukrainian mud along the Zhitomir highway. Half the room knew the war in the East was irrevocably lost; a third drowned the knowledge in cognac; and the remaining sixth—men wearing black and silver runes near the punch bowl—grew deadlier by the week like scorpions trapped inside a ring of burning straw.

"Charming choir, isn't it, Herr Oberstleutnant?" murmured a mild, cultivated voice at Ludwig's left elbow.

Otto Reinhardt stood swirling two fingers of French Armagnac inside a balloon snifter, smelling faintly of yellow pear drops and pressed mouse-grey wool.

"Sturmbannführer," Ludwig inclined his head half an inch. "Quiet *Heiligabend* on Aleja Szucha?"

"Subversives respect church feasts surprisingly little," Reinhardt sighed amiably, eyes twinkling behind gold-wired lenses as he looked out at the snow-covered park trees. "Though Berlin gives us fresh administrative simplification for the New Year, I hear. Rumor along Prinz-Albrecht-Straße whispers that Admiral Canaris's *Abwehr* has suffered one embarrassment too many in Turkey and Madrid—Reichsführer Himmler may absorb military intelligence lock, stock, and cipher barrel before February. Imagine: all of us sitting under one tidy roof."

Beneath his field-grey wool, Ludwig kept every breath slow and bored, though the shaft went straight home—Canaris tottering in Berlin meant his own OKH shield could evaporate on forty-eight hours' teleprinter notice.

"Rumors warm Berlin clerks when coal runs short, Reinhardt," Ludwig drawled coolly, setting his untouched champagne flute onto a passing waiter's silver tray. "Wake me when the orders are printed. Frohe Weihnachten."

Walking briskly back through crisp, dry squeaking snow down Aleja Róż ten minutes later—boots ringing clear under iron streetlamps where sentries stamped numb feet beside striped sentry boxes—Ludwig let himself into Number Twelve and found Viktor Brandt waiting at the foot of the stairs, cheeks flushed cold bright pink from an errand near Dworzec Główny and eyes shining like a hunting terrier's.

"Christmas present from Ignacy Słowik's thirsty Baltic clerk on Szucha, Herr Oberstleutnant," Brandt murmured triumphantly as soon as they bolted the upstairs study door, drawing a folded photostatic sheet damp from fixer salts out of his leather map-case. "Cost us forty gold Reichsmarks in pre-war coin and three bottles of Martell—worth ten times the price. Original Referat Four internal liquidation receipt for *Sondertransport Hotel Polski — Liste B*, dated August nineteenth, nineteen-forty-three."

Ludwig carried the damp photostat directly under the green student lamp.

Typed in clean SS tabular columns ran seventy-four names of Polish Jews who had paid small fortunes for South American diplomatic papers at the Hotel Polski on ulica Długa in July—supposedly routed westward to Vittel internment camp in France for Allied civilian exchange. Column Four bore a single rubber-stamped notation opposite all seventy-four entries:

**SONDERBEHANDLUNG VOLLZOGEN — PAWIAK / RUINENGEBIET DZIELNA, 19.VIII.1943, 05.30 UHR.**
*Special treatment executed — Pawiak / Dzielna Ruins Area, August 19, 1943, 05:30 hours.*

Line Twenty-Nine read: **Goldblum, Estera — Geb. 14.V.1916 Warschau (Paraguay Nr. 419-B). Effekten verwertet.** *Personal effects salvaged.*

And scrawled neatly in green fountain-pen ink at the bottom under *Kenntnis genommen — Referat IV-A2*: **O. Reinhardt, SS-Sturmbannf.**

Ludwig stared down at the green-ink signature for five silent seconds. Even after Babi Yar briefings and Minsk transit reports, the tidy municipal-clerk neatness of *Effekten verwertet* beside a twenty-seven-year-old violinist's daughter whose dead handwriting Reinhardt was still forging onto Vittel blue note-cards four months later to buy cheap hospital espionage left an acrid, metallic taste at the back of his throat.

"Every blue note Reinhardt showed Doktor Karski since August was written over a lime pit," Brandt said quietly.

"Which means," Ludwig murmured, locking the damp photostat carefully inside his hidden wall-safe behind the landscape painting before checking his watch—quarter to eight, fifteen minutes before Sylwia's late *Wigilia* pass brought her across from Lindleya—"by tomorrow night or Sunday night at latest, Roman Karski belongs to us lock, stock, and syringe."

***

When Sylwia stepped inside the warm second-floor study at eight-fifteen, brushing dry powder-snow off her dark wool shoulders while Brandt tactfully retired to his ground-floor quarters with a bottle of Königsberg *Bärenfang* honey liqueur, Ludwig saw within half a second that something cold and heavy rode behind her grey eyes—deeper even than winter exhaustion.

Yet before he could ask, she glanced down at the mahogany table beside the roaring hearth and stopped dead.

Lying on a clean folded damask napkin beside two small crystal glasses of red Bordeaux sat two objects practically mythical in Warsaw of December nineteen-forty-three: a clean, rebound nineteen-twenty-eight first edition of Ferdinand Sauerbruch's *Die Chirurgie der Brustorgane*—the monumental two-volume Berlin bible of thoracic surgery whose plates she used to peer at hungrily through locked glass cases as a seventeen-year-old girl—and, resting right beside a bone-handled pocketknife, a single, glowing, pebble-skinned Sicilian blood-orange smelling impossibly of Mediterranean sun amidst twenty-below Polish frost.

Sylwia picked up the heavy leather spine of Sauerbruch gently with both bare hands as though touching warm bread in famine, opening instinctively to the chromolithograph plates of subclavian and transpleural approaches—and saw, penciled lightly on the flyleaf in razor-neat German script with no names signed in case Gestapo searches ever touched her shelves:

*1,00. — 24.XII.43.*

Her throat closed so tightly for three seconds she couldn't swallow.

"Where on earth did you steal Sauerbruch and an orange in Warsaw?" she whispered without looking up from the flyleaf.

"Sauerbruch from a bankrupt German book-dealer in Cracow via courier pouch; the orange off a Luftwaffe transport pilot flying lemons up from Reggio to Mauerwald mess," Ludwig answered quietly, stepping behind her to slide her damp charcoal coat off her shoulders and draw her back gently against his unbuttoned white shirtfront before the fire. Picking up the bone pocketknife, he sliced the small crimson-fleshed fruit cleanly down the equator into two fragrant halves, handing one half into her palm just as snow hissed against the hot chimney bricks overhead. "Still eat peel-pith and all when you're starving during study hour, Kotarska?"

Holding that fragrant red hemisphere of sunlight in her scarred surgeon's palm while sixty hours ticked inexorably toward Monday morning at eight-forty-five on the corner of Piękna Street—six Sten guns waiting behind snowbanks to tear this exact warm chest behind her spine into bloody shreds, and Major Rawicz's executioners waiting if Ludwig turned down Marszałkowska instead—Sylwia felt something fierce, cold, and incandescent crystallize cleanly inside her skull.

*Neither,* decided Dr. Sylwia Kotarska as clearly as clamping a bleeding vessel in pitch dark. *Rawicz doesn't get Ludwig's corpse, Reinhardt doesn't get our hospital, and Marek's six boys don't die in a German crossfire either.*

Turning smoothly inside the circle of Ludwig's arms to press a ruby segment of blood-orange straight between his parted lips—tart-sweet Sicilian juice bursting across both their tongues as he caught her wrist and kissed the juice cleanly off her mouth—Sylwia looked straight up into his slate-blue eyes when they parted for breath.

"Did Brandt get the Hotel Polski sheet?" she asked softly.

"Signed in Reinhardt's own green ink," Ludwig told her, dark satisfaction glinting in his gaze. "Estera Goldblum died at Pawiak on August nineteenth. Tomorrow night—or Sunday night after ward rounds—we corner Roman Karski alone in Pavilion Three's pathology basement and break Reinhardt's leash clean in half."

"Sunday evening at seven," Sylwia said instantly, brain racing six moves ahead across every street intersection between Lindleya, Aleja Szucha, and Piękna like a master chess player seeing a forced mate through smoke. "Not tomorrow—tomorrow is Christmas Day and half the ward sisters stay late for vespers. Sunday night at seven the pathology wing is dead empty, and Karski has night admission duty."

She rested both bare palms flat against Ludwig's warm chest—right over the healed pink scar beneath his left collarbone and the firm callus bridging the bone—looking up at him in the quiet Christmas firelight with eyes fierce as winter stars.

"Bring that August nineteenth execution sheet to the pathology cellar door at seven Sunday night, Ludwig," she whispered, rising on tiptoe to kiss the crescent scar above his right eyebrow before pulling his mouth down to hers. "Because by eight-thirty Monday morning, Roman Karski is going to hand Otto Reinhardt the biggest Christmas package Aleja Szucha ever unwrapped."

***

Later that Christmas Eve, after midnight bells had died away across frozen parks and the anthracite in the iron grate had burned down to deep, smokeless ruby caverns throwing rose-gold shadow across the ceiling moldings, neither of them slept.

Sylwia lay naked on her side beneath the heavy goose-down coverlet facing the hearth, Ludwig stretched behind her with one lean thigh hooked warm between both of hers and his left forearm resting lightly along the curve of her hip—healed solid enough now that five weeks of careful immobilization had turned shattered bone into living bridge. Outside on the mahogany table, peeled crimson rinds of the Sicilian blood-orange perfumed the cold room air with an impossible ghost of Palermo orchards and pre-war Mediterranean summers neither of them had ever seen together.

With slow, almost clinical absorption—because somewhere down behind her ribs ticked fifty-six silent hours until six Kedyw Sten guns waited in snowbanks along Piękna Street, and every surgeon knew even the cleanest operation carried five percent mortal hazard when grease froze or sentries panicked—Sylwia lifted Ludwig's bare right hand into the dim red firelight and traced every bone from radial styloid to fingertip with her thumb.

Callus on the outer side of the middle phalanx from seventeen years of pens and compass dividers; faded white sabre-fencing nick across the first knuckle from Königsberg; faint dark powder speckle near the thumb web from Orel.

"You're cataloguing bones again, *Frau Doktor*," Ludwig murmured into the loose dark hair behind her ear, voice low and lazy with rare midnight peace, kissing the sensitive hollow at the nape of her neck where fine curls met warm skin. "Planning an amputation?"

"Checking circulation," Sylwia lied softly, pressing his open palm flat down between her bare breasts so he felt the steady beat underneath before turning half around inside his arms to look at his face in the red coal-glow. Up close without uniform wool or Generalstab steel in his spine, thirty-two looked startlingly close to the eighteen-year-old boy who had sketched her hand by candle-lantern light high up in the Berlin State Library dome fourteen Decembers ago—save for the deep bracket war had chiseled beside his mouth and the quiet shadow behind his eyes whenever Konrad's name came near. "Is your mother still in the Dahlem house tonight?"

Ludwig's fingers stilled slightly over her ribs before resuming a slow, gentle stroke along her bare collarbone.

"No," he said quietly. "RAF Lancasters dropped two four-thousand-pound blockbusters onto Thielplatz in late August—blew the roof and conservatory glass clean into the garden pond. She moved east to my aunt's timber manor near Allenstein in Masuria. Writes me three formal lines once a fortnight on thick cream paper: *Frost arrived Tuesday; Pastor Ehlers sends regards; see that you wear flannel beneath your tunic.* Not one word about Konrad since July, and not one word about Hitler—because she knows censor clerks steam open Generalstab mail."

Sylwia studied the pale gleam of his Baltic eyes in the embers. "If she knew whose head was lying on your pillow tonight in Warsaw?"

A dry, genuine ghost of a laugh vibrated silently through Ludwig's bare chest against hers.

"My father would spin fast enough inside Invalidenfriedhof granite to power half Berlin's trams," Ludwig murmured, brushing a dark strand back behind her ear with infinite, lingering tenderness. "My mother? Margarethe Schoeler comes from Hamburg merchant bankers who counted gold marks long before Hohenzollerns wore crowns. After five minutes' icy silence over her teacup, she would inspect your posture, note your French accent beats hers, decide anybody tough enough to pull her surviving son back from dead shock in forty minutes flat had acceptable breeding after all, and complain your hands smelled of carbolic."

Despite Powązki's graves and Rawicz's gun waiting at Monday noon, a quiet, involuntary smile curved Sylwia's mouth in the dark—sharp and aching because imagining an ordinary teacup in a house without machine guns felt more indecent than nakedness.

Rolling her gently flat onto her back beneath the down quilt so red hearth-light washed warm across her bare throat and shoulders, Ludwig propped himself above her on his healthy right elbow, tracing one slow fingertip from the hollow between her collarbones straight down between her breasts to her navel—watching gooseflesh rise instantly in his wake and her breath hitch shallow and hot.

"When you walked down those terrace steps at Wannsee in red silk fourteen summers ago," Ludwig whispered, dropping his mouth to kiss the exact path his finger had just burned down her sternum, "do you know what two lines of Goethe ran straight through my skull while Bredow spilled Moselle down his cuff?"

Her hands slid into his cropped tarnished-gold hair, pulling him closer as his warm knee nudged her thighs wider beneath the quilt. "Tell me."

Against the bare, beating skin below her left breast, just before he slid slowly, hot and heavy and familiar, deep inside the waiting wet heat of her body—stealing her breath clean away in a silent, arching gasp under the warm feathers—Ludwig whispered two quiet German lines from the *West-östlicher Divan* straight onto her skin:

*"Gottes ist der Orient, Gottes ist der Okzident—"*
*"Und in deinen Augen allein liegt mein Vaterland."*

*East and West belong to God—and in your eyes alone lies my country.*

Sylwia pulled his mouth fiercely up to hers, wrapping both bare legs tight around his lean hips in the dark red glow, holding him deep inside her with every ounce of strength in her body while Christmas snow whispered against blacked-out glass and Monday morning waited out in the frost.
