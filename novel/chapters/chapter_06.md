# CHAPTER SIX

## The Nightingale of Kercelak

**Warsaw — October 26–28, 1943**

*Siekiera, motyka, bimber, szklanka,*
*W nocy nalot, w dzień łapanka...*

*Axe, hoe, moonshine, glass—air raid by night, roundup by day.*

A gap-toothed ten-year-old boy in a man's cut-down Wehrmacht tunic dyed muddy brown with walnut hulls sang the forbidden street jingle at the top of his lungs atop an overturned cabbage barrel at the edge of Kercelak Market, keeping one bright magpie eye cocked toward the Okopowa tram turn while hawking single homemade cigarettes out of a cardboard shoebox. Thirty feet away, ankle-deep in black October mire churned to soup by ten thousand wooden clogs, all of starving, scheming, indestructible Warsaw bought and sold whatever shreds of Europe had fallen off freight trains between Berlin and Smolensk.

With a plain wool kerchief tied peasant-fashion over her dark hair to hide her aristocratic brow and her battered doctor's satchel clutched close against her ribs, Sylwia threaded rapidly through the crush of stalls. Twenty paces behind her, hands shoved deep into frayed pockets and whistling off-key whenever blue Polish police caps appeared, ambled nineteen-year-old Tomek—known to Marek's cell as *Wróbel*, the Sparrow—a snub-nosed printer's apprentice from Powiśle with an angelic freckled grin and a cocked Belgian Browning nine-millimeter tucked flat against his groin.

Every smell of wartime misery hung thick in the wet Wola air: rancid horse-fat sizzling over charcoal braziers alongside grey tripe *flaki*, sour mash liquor, wet dog-fur coats, unwashed bodies, stolen Ukrainian leaf tobacco, and damp brick dust blowing south across Chłodna from the vast, silent moonscape where the Warsaw Ghetto had stood.

Sylwia did not slow at the food stalls, though the greasy smell of frying onions twisted her empty stomach violently; she had given half her morning bread ration to the fourteen-year-old amputee in Recovery. Today only one commodity in Warsaw mattered: injectable *Cibazol*—sulfathiazole sodium.

Down in the brick vault behind Pavilion Three's steaming typhus-laundry boilers, young Janek—the twenty-year-old Kedyw rifleman whose left femur had been shattered during the October nineteenth ambush of Ludwig Schoeler's Horch—was burning up at forty-point-two degrees with spreading hemolytic streptococcal septicemia. Oral white Prontosil tablets crushed in water were coming straight back up in bloody vomit. And two stories directly overhead in Isolation Room Two, by the grotesque symmetry of shared shrapnel and rainy Warsaw street mud, Ludwig Schoeler's left pleural fluid had turned turbid straw-clouded yellow this morning on diagnostic needle tap—early streptococcal empyema threatening to encase his lung in tough fibrinous rind and erode back up the chest wall.

Yesterday morning the German municipal *Amtsarzt* had crossed sulfathiazole cleanly off Infant Jesus Hospital's monthly requisition sheet: *Reserved exclusively for Wehrmacht field hospitals.*

Which left only Ignacy Słowik.

Turning quickly out of the mud-churned market square into ulica Chłodna, Sylwia kept her eyes down past the haunting ghost-scars etched across the asphalt where the wooden footbridge had once arched high over "Aryan" tramcars carrying doomed shadows between the Small and Large Ghettos. Halfway down a gutted courtyard behind a pre-war feather-bedding factory, she climbed three flights of creaking timber stairs smelling of cat piss and roasted barley, knocked four times—*two fast, pause, two slow*—and waited while three separate iron bolts slid back behind oak plated with sheet steel.

Stepping across that threshold was like tumbling out of nineteen-forty-three straight into an overheated, slightly mad nineteenth-century operetta salon.

Thick crimson plush curtains—salvaged from the bombed Teatr Wielki—muffled every window; porcelain stoves roared generously with real Upper Silesian anthracite coal; and hanging from gilded brass stands in every corner of the crowded parlor, four dozen yellow Harz Roller canaries trilled, warbled, and whistled in deafening, liquid chorus.

Ignacy Słowik—*The Nightingale*, sixty kilos overweight in a city of skeletons, wrapped magnificently in a burgundy velvet smoking jacket with a slightly moth-eaten astrakhan collar—rose from behind a boulle writing desk cluttered with gold twenty-dollar Double Eagles, Swiss wristwatches, German *Ostbahn* blank travel warrants, and crates of Danish tinned butter.

"Ah! My guardian angel of the scalpel!" Słowik boomed in rich, theatrical baritone Polish, sweeping Sylwia's chilled knuckles nearly to his waxed mustache while tiny shrewd black eyes weighed her hollow cheeks in half a second. "Come to the stove, Pani Doktor, come thaw your bones! No, don't glare at my birds—forty Harz Rollers singing together defeat even Telefunken's finest carbon wall-microphones, besides cheering a sensitive soul in barbarous times! Plum *śliwowica* from Łącko—seventy proof, genuine thirty-eight vintage, drink!"

He pressed a delicate Sèvres porcelain teacup half-full of clear, fiery plum brandy into her hand. Sylwia drank half a mouthful; liquid fire exploded down her esophagus, instantly warming her cold fingertips.

"I have six minutes, Pan Ignacy," she said without preamble, unbuttoning her coat cuff to draw out a twist of tissue paper from inside her wristwatch strap. Opening the paper on his blotter among the gold coins, she revealed a square-cut Belle Époque Colombian emerald set in platinum filigree—Cécile Moreau-Kotarska's engagement brooch from Paris, nineteen-ten, the very last object of her parents' house Sylwia had sworn never to sell. "I need twenty five-milliliter ampoules of sterile injectable *Cibazol* or *Eleudron*, twenty-percent solution, unbroken factory glass, today."

Słowik picked up a jeweler's loupe, squinted three seconds at the deep green fire of the emerald without touching it, and set the glass down with a theatrical sigh. "Clean garden, pre-war Cartier mount, worth two thousand paper *młynarki* in peacetime and three sacks of white flour today. Alas, Pani Doktor, injectable *Cibazol*! Since Kursk and the Dnieper crossings, every *Reserve-Lazarett* between Warsaw and Lemberg guards sulfa ampoules harder than machine-gun belts. My cousin's boy at Ujazdów risked hanging just slipping four cartons out inside an empty oxygen cylinder on Tuesday."

"Three cartons—fifteen ampoules—won't hold two grown men past Sunday," Sylwia said flatly. "Twenty ampoules, Pan Ignacy. You still turn that thick neck of yours without a stiff collar because I lanced that diabetic carbuncle off your occiput in forty-one when no German clinic would touch you."

Słowik chuckled richly, spreading plump white hands ringed in turquoise and gold. "Cruel woman! She reminds an artist of his mortality! Very well—for anyone else in Warsaw, fifteen ampoules; for Pani Doktor Kotarska, four full green cartons of five—twenty ampoules, *Heeres-Sanitätspark Warschau* stamp still wet on the cardboard, so for the love of Christ's wounds peel the eagle labels off before you put them in your ward cupboard!"

Unlocking a false bottom beneath a crate of sardines, Słowik laid four olive-drab cardboard boxes stamped with the black Wehrmacht eagle and *CIBAZOL — Ciba AG Basel / Heeres-Sanitätsdienst* onto the blotter—and then, instead of sweeping her mother's emerald brooch into his velvet drawer immediately, held one plump forefinger upright amid the singing canaries.

"And because I never leave a debt half-paid, Pani Doktor," Słowik murmured, all theatrical buffoonery vanishing from his black eyes inside one heartbeat, "one small canary song on the house. Keep your chin calm while you pack your bag."

Sylwia's hands continued smoothly sliding the four olive-drab cartons beneath the false oilcloth divider at the bottom of her leather medical satchel, right under her steel Pelkmann speculum and obstetrical forceps. "Sing, Pan Ignacy."

"Have you lived in Berlin, Pani Doktor?"

Cold silence dropped inside Sylwia's ribs beneath the trilling of forty birds. "When I was young. Why?"

"Because one of my regular customers—a thirsty Baltic German teleprinter clerk working night shift on the third floor of Aleja Szucha Twenty-Five who owes me six hundred zlotys for French cognac and gonorrhea powders—mentioned an odd little curiosity last night," Słowik said softly, polishing his Sèvres teacup with silk. "Usually Referat Four-A queries Berlin RSHA on Communist radio codes or London parachute drops. Yesterday afternoon at four-fifteen, SS-Sturmbannführer Otto Reinhardt sent a priority personal wire to Berlin Police Precinct Mitte requesting certified photostats of the nineteen-twenty-seven through nineteen-thirty enrollment, examination, and disciplinary archives of the *Französisches Gymnasium* on Reichstagsufer—cross-referenced under two names."

Sylwia pulled the leather strap of her doctor's bag smoothly through its brass buckle—*one notch, two notches*—without a single tremor in her fingers. "Which two names?"

"Kotarska," Słowik whispered, "and Schoeler. Berlin bureaucracy being thoroughly bombed every third night by the Royal Air Force, photostats by courier train take four to six days. Whatever game you are playing in Pavilion Three, my dear Pani Doktor—finish it before Monday, or buy a Swedish fisherman's passport from me while emeralds still spend."

***

Dusk fell early under low, sleet-bellied clouds as Sylwia hurried east from Chłodna through the narrow, rubble-choked tenement alleys linking Żelazna to Sienna, cutting five minutes off the Marszałkowska main boulevard so Janek could get two grams of intravenous *Cibazol* before the seven-o'clock ward lockup.

Behind her, nineteen-year-old Tomek closed the gap from twenty paces to six as shadows pooled black inside bombed-out ground-floor storefronts. Six weeks of autumn rain had turned plaster dust into slick grey tallow underfoot; somewhere three blocks south toward the railway cut, a solitary shot cracked—*pop!*—followed by silence. Nobody even turned around for single shots in Warsaw anymore.

*Four to six days,* Sylwia's mind raced like cold clockwork. *Today is Thursday the twenty-eighth. By Monday or Tuesday morning at latest, Otto Reinhardt opens an envelope from Berlin and learns that Sylwia Kotarska and Ludwig Schoeler did not merely inhabit the same city of four million people—they sat three benches apart for three years and graduated tied to the decimal. Once Reinhardt holds that paper, he pulls me straight down into the "tramway" at Aleja Szucha until every bone in my hands is crushed.*

And Marek wanted Schoeler dead by Sunday night.

As she turned into the narrow, crooked brick passage of Sienna Alley—a sixty-yard shortcut between high windowless warehouse walls and a collapsed three-story tannery cratered back in thirty-nine—two men wearing the bottle-green greatcoats and black-visored caps of the *Sonderdienst* auxiliary gendarmerie stepped abruptly out of a recessed coal archway ahead, blocking the narrow brick throat while blowing sour cabbage-and-vodka breath into the sleet.

*Volksdeutsche*—ethnic German recruits from Łódź or the Sudetenland, infinitely louder, greedier, and hair-triggered than regular Wehrmacht soldiers because half of them made triple their pay shaking down black-market smugglers in dark alleys thirty minutes before curfew and shooting witnesses who argued.

Behind Sylwia, Tomek slowed instantly at the alley mouth, lounging against a broken drainpipe as if tying his bootlace, right hand sliding casually inside his frayed jacket lapel. *Don't draw unless you have to, Wróbel,* Sylwia willed silently; a gunfight six blocks from Pavilion Three brought thirty police trucks down on the neighborhood in ten minutes.

"Halt! *Kennkarte* und Tasche auf!" slurred the bigger of the two auxiliaries—six feet of pork-fed Łódź butcher's boy with broken red veins across nose and cheeks, unslinging his Mauser carbine halfway off his shoulder while his leaner, rat-faced companion grinned and stepped to Sylwia's left elbow to box her against the wet brick wall.

Sylwia opened her coat collar just enough to produce her grey Polish *Kennkarte* alongside her red-bordered *Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus* night-surgeon's pass, speaking brisk, haughty Berlin German calculated to make low-ranking *Volksdeutsche* auxiliaries snap their heels together out of reflex:

"Night surgical emergency at Infant Jesus Hospital on Lindleya, *Wachtmeister*, where Oberstleutnant Schoeler of OKH Generalstab is currently quartered under Wehrmacht guard. Inspect the stamp quickly; I am overdue."

Rat-face actually hesitated half a step at *Generalstab*, blinking rapidly in the sleet—until the big red-veined corporal spat brown tobacco juice onto her shoe and snatched her leather doctor's satchel straight out of her hand.

"Generalstab my arse, Sepp—every Polish slut with lipstick and five words of Berlin dialect claims she's wiping some colonel's backside lately! Let's see how many kilos of bacon Frau Doktor's lugging to Lindleya!"

Thick, dirt-rimmed thumbs wrenched the brass frame wide, plunged past the Pelkmann speculum, hooked the loose edge of the oilcloth false divider—and ripped it upward.

In the dim grey sleet-light gleamed four olive-drab cardboard cartons stamped boldly in black Reich ink: **HEERES-SANITÄTSPARK WARSCHAU — UNVERKÄUFLICH WEHRMACHTSEIGENTUM**.

Rat-face sucked in a low, greedy whistle through yellow teeth. "Jesus Maria, Kurt—Wehrmacht sulfa! Five hundred zlotys an ampoule on Kercelak, and four full boxes!"

"Four boxes means firing squad at Pawiak," Kurt grinned slowly, dropping his Mauser back on its sling and shoving Sylwia backward by one shoulder straight into the pitch-dark, roofless brick vestibule of the bombed tannery off the alley so passing pedestrians out on Żelazna wouldn't see them strip the catch. "Keep an eye on the street mouth, Sepp! Let's see what else Frau Doktor's got taped between those long Polish legs before we ring up Aleja Szucha and split the bounty!"

Sylwia's spine hit wet, jagged brick.

Two things happened inside her brain in less than half a heartbeat—not panic, never panic, only that blinding, glacial slowness that descended over an operating table when a major vessel ruptured.

First: if these two lived to blow a whistle, she went to Aleja Szucha tonight with Wehrmacht stolen drugs in her bag—which meant Pawiak torture, Marek's nineteen addresses threatened, Halina arrested, Janek dying downstairs, and her teeth biting down on the eight grains of potassium cyanide sewn three inches above her left collarbone.

Second: Kurt had slung his rifle to use both hands, and her open leather doctor's bag dangled right against his left hip with the unrolled chamois surgical pocket-case hanging halfway out the side.

As Kurt's sour vodka-soaked mouth lunged down toward her throat and his heavy left paw tore the top two horn buttons clean off her wool coat and blouse—cold air and wet rough fingers scraping bare skin above her chemise—Sylwia did not push his chest away. Instead, she let her knees give two inches as though fainting in terror, dropping her right hand level with the dangling chamois instrument roll at his hip, and slid three inches of sterile, razor-honed #11 pointed Parker-Kerr bistoury scalpel cleanly out of its nickel clip.

"Please—" she breathed weakly in broken German, tilting her bare throat leftward so Kurt leaned eagerly into the dark arch after it, leaving the entire left carotid triangle between sternocleidomastoid and trachea stretched taut and uncovered four inches from her chin.

Then she drove the #11 surgical steel straight inward behind the angle of his jaw to the brass bolster, angled thirty degrees medially across common carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and lateral pharyngeal wall, and ripped forward two inches toward the windpipe.

No scream ever left him—only a wet, bubbling *khh-hiss* of severed trachea and hot air.

Scalding scarlet arterial blood erupted across Sylwia's chin, throat, and torn blouse with the force of a fire hydrant, blinding her left eye for half a second as two hundred pounds of dying butcher crashed forward onto her chest, driving her breath out against the brick wall while his boots drummed convulsively in slick plaster mud.

Six paces out at the alley threshold, Rat-face heard the wet thud and spun halfway around from watching the street—*"Kurt? Hat die Katze—"*

From the five-foot rubble pile behind the crumbled archway, nineteen-year-old Tomek launched himself silent as a bobcat onto Rat-face's shoulders, looping eighteen inches of braided steel piano wire rigged between two oak toggles squarely across the auxiliary's throat and wrenching backward with every ounce of wiry Powiśle muscle on his spine.

Rat-face clawed wildly at his buried throat, garbled purple-faced choking muffled to silence as his hobnailed boots kicked frantically against fallen corrugated tin sheeting—*clang! bang-clang!*—loud enough in the twilight alley to carry half a block toward Żelazna!

Shoving Kurt's slackening, spurting carcass sideways off her into the weeds, Sylwia lunged three steps through the dark like an avenging ghost—hair half loose, face painted crimson from cheekbone to collar, #11 blade gleaming black-red in her steady right fist—dropped onto one knee beside the thrashing gendarme's kicking legs, felt once with her left thumb for the femoral pulse below the inguinal crease, no, wool trousers too thick, snapped her hand up to the exposed hollow above Rat-face's straining right collarbone where Tomek's wire stretched the neck taut, and sank the pointed bistoury straight down behind the clavicle through right subclavian artery and trachea together.

*The exact vessel she had sewed shut on Ludwig Schoeler with five-zero silk nine nights ago.*

Three violent jerks, a hot dark gush soaking into green uniform wool, and the hobnailed boots went limp in the mud without striking the tin sheet again.

For ten seconds nobody breathed except the sleet ticking onto rubble and wet nettles. Out on Żelazna fifty yards away, a bicycle bell chimed twice and passed on without stopping.

Tomek unwound the wire from his raw knuckles, chest heaving fast, staring wide-eyed through the gloom at the tall, aristocratic woman surgeon kneeling in black mud beside two dead German policemen, calmly wiping her #11 scalpel clean on the dry inner lining of Kurt's greatcoat before clicking it back into its nickel guard so it wouldn't slice her satchel leather.

"Jesus and Mary, Pani Doktor," Tomek whispered in hushed, terrified awe, crossing himself rapidly before grabbing Rat-face under the boots. "Help me tip them down the flooded dye-vat pit in back before the seven-o'clock patrol comes down Sienna—take his ankles—don't get brains on your skirt hem—"

Working in grim, wordless speed for four minutes, they rolled both bodies—weapons, caps, and all, stuffed down with forty pounds of loose brick—into six feet of black, stagnant cellar water behind collapsed vaulting where rats would leave nothing recognizable before spring thaw, kicked clean wet gravel over the dark puddles in the vestibule, and retrieved all four olive-drab cartons of *Cibazol*.

Behind the ruined cistern stood a wooden rain-barrel filmed with thin evening ice. Cracking the crust with her elbow, Sylwia plunged both bare hands and wrists into freezing water until her bones ached, scrubbing human blood aggressively from beneath every fingernail with a lump of gritty brick-sand, splashing icy water across her cheeks, chin, and throat, drying off with her clean wool headscarf, and pinning her torn blouse and coat lapels high under her chin with two heavy surgical safety pins from her bag so not one speck of scarlet showed on dark charcoal wool.

Only when Tomek melted back into the shadows half a block from Lindleya did Sylwia pause three seconds under the dripping chestnut trees outside Infant Jesus Hospital—waiting patiently until her heart rate slowed from one hundred ten down to seventy-two—before walking cleanly past Hauptmann Brandt's courtyard sentry and Reinhardt's smoking SD plainclothes man in the lobby with a cool, weary nod.

Twenty minutes later, down behind the roaring copper typhus vats in the sub-basement, Halina held the kerosene lantern while Sylwia slid two full five-milliliter ampoules—two grams—of crystal-clear *Cibazol* slowly into delirious young Janek's left cephalic vein, after peeling every trace of German cardboard off and burning the eagle-stamped boxes straight inside the boiler firebox.

At eight-fifteen, washed in sublimate, dressed in a fresh starched white smock, hair re-pinned smooth as polished sable at her nape, Sylwia carried two unwrapped amber glass ampoules of *Cibazol* upstairs to Isolation Room Two.

Hauptmann Brandt was seated by the radiator decoding a Mauerwald flimsy; Ludwig lay propped on two bolsters reading Clausewitz—a fresh pocket edition Brandt had procured—with his good right hand. As Sylwia snapped the scored glass neck off the first unwrapped five-milliliter ampoule inside a gauze square to draw up his evening intravenous dose, a tiny crescent ring of amber glass neck popped sideways out of the gauze fold, bounced silently off her clog sole, and rolled three inches beneath the cast-iron radiator foot without ringing.

Neither Brandt nor Sylwia noticed it.

Approaching the bed with the loaded syringe, Sylwia caught the tourniquet rubber around Ludwig's lean right bicep. As she bent ten inches from his face to slide the bevel smoothly into his median vein, Ludwig lowered his book slowly—slate-blue eyes narrowing just perceptibly as he caught, beneath strong hospital carbolic soap, the faintest trace of freezing rainwater in damp hair behind her ear and a tiny red scratch just below her jaw where Kurt's thumbnail had grazed skin.

He said nothing at all in front of Brandt—only watched her long, steady fingers depress the glass plunger drop by drop, wondering silently whose blood she had washed off them between dusk and eight o'clock.
