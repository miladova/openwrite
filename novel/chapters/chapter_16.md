# CHAPTER SIXTEEN

## Unter den Linden

**Berlin — December 18, 1929**

Once—and only once across the thousand days they spent at each other's throats in Berlin—the war stopped for three hours.

It happened seven days before Christmas in nineteen-twenty-nine, during the great pre-solstice blizzard that buried Unter den Linden up to the bronze hooves of Frederick the Great's horse. Outside in the freezing dark around six-thirty in the evening, striking transport workers and brown-shirted *Sturmabteilung* street brawlers blew a high-voltage transformer box behind Alexanderplatz, plunging six square miles of central Berlin from the Brandenburg Gate to the Lustgarten into instantaneous pitch darkness.

High up on the fourth-tier cast-iron book-gallery of the Prussian State Library—sixty feet above the vast, echoing octagonal dome of the deserted main reading room where readers had already filed out before the seven-o'clock closing bell—eighteen-year-old Sylwia Kotarska stood on a rolling library ladder holding a sixteenth-century folio of Andreas Vesalius when every electric globe in the building winked dead.

Down on the marble floor far below, an elderly night attendant shouted *"Alle raus? Lichtstörung im Viertel!"*, swung the heavy bronze hall doors shut without realizing anyone remained up in the rare-anatomical iron cages, and turned two double-bitted keys in the locks.

"Marvelous," Sylwia muttered aloud in Polish into thick, leather-scented blackness. "Freeze to death beside dead Belgians until nine tomorrow morning."

Ten yards further down the narrow slatted-iron catwalk around the curve of the dome, a sulphur match scraped *scritch-flare*, illuminating a forgotten brass watchman's candle-lantern hanging on an iron hook—and, behind the yellow flame, the lean, unmistakable profile of Ludwig Schoeler holding three volumes of Mommsen and Schlieffen under one arm.

Sylwia nearly stepped clean off the ladder rung in sheer outrage. "Were you following me up into the medical stacks, Schoeler?!"

"Hardly, Kotarska," Ludwig replied dryly, lifting the brass candle-lantern off its hook and walking slowly along the ringing iron catwalk until warm yellow light fell across her dark pleated skirt and stacked folios. "Military cartography sits one bay west of sixteenth-century butchery, though the distinction escapes me. Apparently our attentive civil service just locked us inside until the municipal grid comes back or night rounds pass at ten."

Testing both spiral stair iron grilles proved it inside two minutes: locked solid top and bottom to prevent book theft. Heating steam was already dying down in the tall cast-iron pipes; outside the high clerestory windows sixty feet up in the dome ribs, snow hissed thick and white through Berlin darkness where not a single streetlamp burned.

Returning to the sheltered corner alcove between Renaissance Anatomy and Prussian Siegecraft—where oak paneling blocked drafts off the vast central dome—they sat down six feet apart on the warmish floorboards beside a dying radiator pipe, setting the single brass lantern squarely on the iron grating between them like a truce flag.

For fifteen minutes neither spoke. Ludwig read Schlieffen's Cannae notes by candlelight, collar turned up; Sylwia laid open the colossal 1543 Basel woodcuts of Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* across her knees.

At quarter to eight, Sylwia's stomach gave an audible, treacherous growl in the cathedral hush.

Without looking up from Cannae, Ludwig reached into his overcoat pocket, snapped a bar of dark Sarotti bitter chocolate cleanly in half inside its foil wrapper, and slid two squares across the oak plank beside Vesalius's spine.

Sylwia stared suspiciously at the chocolate for three seconds—half expecting laxative powder or quinine—before realizing nobody carried rigged chocolate into a random power outage. Reaching into her own Warsaw leather satchel, she produced a small Jaffa orange wrapped in tissue paper, peeled it neatly with her thumbnails, split the fragrant segments into two equal piles of five on the tissue, and pushed half back across the lantern ring.

"Even trade," she said crisply. "No Versailles reparations owed."

The corner of Ludwig's mouth curved faintly in the warm candle-glow. Eating bitter dark chocolate alongside sweet Jaffa segments eighty feet up in a dark palace of two million books while all Berlin rioted or froze outside did something strange and disarming to the air between them: without an audience, without Doktor Glass's scoreboard or fathers waiting in drawing rooms, three years of armor suddenly weighed twenty tons too much to keep strapped on.

Leaning sideways on one elbow to peer upside-down at the open page across Sylwia's knees, Ludwig studied Jan van Calcar's famous woodcut of a flayed human skeleton resting one bony elbow contemplatively upon a grave-digger's spade, skull tilted back toward heaven in silent interrogation. Below the woodcut ran the Latin motto: *Vivitur ingenio, caetera mortis erunt.*

*Genius lives on; all the rest shall belong to death.*

"Cheerful bedtime picturebooks you read, Kotarska," Ludwig murmured. "Why surgery? Every other diplomat's daughter in Tiergarten wants Paris couture or a legation secretary with decent horses. You spend Saturdays smelling ether in Sauerbruch's gallery."

Under normal daylight she would have snapped back an insult. Tonight, looking down at the quiet candle flame reflected in the glass lantern pane, she spoke the plain truth she had never once told Ruth Oppenheim or any classmate in Berlin.

"In February nineteen-nineteen, in Warsaw," Sylwia said quietly, "when I was seven years old, my mother caught the Spanish influenza during the third wave. My father brought the three most eminent male professors of internal medicine in Warsaw to our flat on Mokotowska. They wore frock coats smelling of eau-de-cologne, bowed beautifully, quoted Latin, bled six ounces from her arm with glass cups, and stood helplessly by the stove murmuring about Divine Providence while pink pulmonary edema drowned her lungs over fourteen hours."

Ludwig had stopped chewing; his slate-blue eyes rested wholly, quietly on her profile in the amber light.

"Around four in the morning, when she couldn't breathe lying down anymore," Sylwia said evenly, "I climbed onto the stool beside her pillow holding a hot towel and swore that when I grew up I would never stand beside a bed murmuring Latin while something mechanical killed a human being. You can't argue with pneumonia by scalpel—not yet—but when bone splinters or an artery opens or pus pockets behind ribs, steel works. No prayers required."

For a long moment only snow whispered against the high dome windows.

Reaching into his waistcoat pocket, Ludwig drew out a short carpenter's stick of soft vine charcoal he used for map shading, leaned across the lantern toward the wide, blank cream flyleaf at the back of her personal sketchbook—not the state folio, even Schoeler wouldn't deface library vellum—and glanced up once into her grey eyes. "Hold your right hand still on your knee for two minutes."

Curious despite herself, Sylwia rested her bare right hand—already long-fingered, lean, slightly square at the spatulate tips—relaxed across her dark wool skirt.

With astonishing speed and quiet absorption, Ludwig's charcoal whispered across heavy paper. She had known he drew clean geometric projections in mathematics; she had never guessed he possessed a draughtsman's living eye for light, tendon, and bone. Inside three minutes, rendered in twenty bold, sensitive strokes of black shadow and untouched cream highlight, her own right hand rested alive on the page—strong metacarpals, poised index finger, look of tensile strength waiting for an instrument.

"You shouldn't be going into Potsdam infantry school next autumn," Sylwia said before she could stop herself, staring down in genuine surprise at the drawing. "Men who draw bone like that build bridges or cathedrals—they don't march columns through mud."

Ludwig blew loose charcoal dust gently off the paper and laid the stick down, a wry, wintry shadow crossing his eighteen-year-old face that looked suddenly very much like the man who would stand in Warsaw fourteen winters later.

"Eldest Schoeler sons don't build bridges in Munich, Sylwia," he said without self-pity, simply stating gravity. "Konrad gets the piano and perhaps forestry if I stand between him and Father hard enough. Since seventeen-forty, eldest sons wear the crimson stripe and try to keep fools at the top from getting half the country slaughtered."

At quarter to ten, electric globes down in the great dome suddenly flickered yellow—half-voltage restored—and sixty feet below, iron keys jangled loudly as the horrified head night-watchman discovered two student coats missing from the downstairs check-rack and came clumping frantically up the spiral stairs shouting apologies.

Out on the wide marble portico beneath snow swirling thick through restored streetlamps along Unter den Linden, buttoning coats to the chin before parting ways toward Kurfürstenstraße and Dahlem, Ludwig paused at the foot of the steps, snow already dusting his bare tarnished-gold hair where he held his cap in hand.

"Keep that sketch," he told her lightly, eyes glinting cold blue-silver through falling flakes. "When our elders' idiocies finally set Europe on fire again inside ten years, Kotarska, keep a sharp knife in whatever cellar you're cutting people open in. Knowing my luck, I'll land squarely on your table."

"Heaven help you if you do, Schoeler," eighteen-year-old Sylwia called back over her shoulder into the snowstorm, laughing breathlessly as a taxi slid to the curb. "I charge Prussians double!"

**Warsaw — January 6, 1944**

Fourteen years and nineteen days later, on the night of Epiphany while twenty-below January frost cracked chestnut bark like pistol shots along the frozen Vistula escarpment, Sylwia walked blindfolded for the final hundred paces down three levels of medieval brick wine-cellars beneath Warsaw's Old Town Market Square.

When the rough hemp hood was lifted off her head, damp air smelled of saltpeter, river silt, tallow wax, and gun oil.

Beneath a low fourteenth-century groin vault reinforced against air raids by heavy oak pit-props sat an improvised plank table draped in coarse grey blanket wool. Two carbide miners' lamps hissed white-blue at either end, throwing harsh shadows upward onto three figures seated behind the planks while an armed twenty-year-old guard with a slung Sten stood immovable before the single iron door behind Sylwia's spine.

Center sat a gaunt, sixty-year-old man in threadbare dark worsted with white hair cropped to stubble and ice-water eyes behind steel-rimmed spectacles—known across occupied Poland solely as *Sędzia*, Presiding Magistrate of the *Wojskowy Sąd Specjalny*, the Underground State's Special Military Court, from whose three-word verdicts no appeal existed on earth.

To the magistrate's right, in his faded suburban priest's cassock, sat Major Rawicz of *Oddział II* Counter-Intelligence.

To the magistrate's left, face carved out of grey winter stone beneath the blue Modlin powder-scar on his temple, sat Marek Zawadzki.

Sylwia stood straight on the packed-earth floor in her charcoal wool coat, ungloved hands clasped loosely before her waist, heart beating slow and cold at sixty-eight. Before leaving Pavilion Three an hour ago, she had hidden her mother's silver Mazovian leaf-clasp and Sauerbruch's textbook behind loose brickwork in her hospital quarters; inside the false lining of her left coat lapel rode twelve fresh exposed frames of nine-point-five-millimeter Minox film.

"Sub-Lieutenant *Kora*—civilian designation Dr. Sylwia Kotarska," recited the white-haired magistrate in a quiet, passionless voice honed in pre-war Supreme Court chambers. "This extraordinary inquiry convenes under Article Seven of the Military Penal Code in Wartime. No stenographic record is kept; should exoneration follow, every word dies beneath this vault; should treason or unauthorized collaboration with enemy intelligence be established, sentence executes prior to dawn. Major Rawicz."

Rawicz laid two items onto the grey blanket: an enlargement of Ujazdowskie street reports from December twenty-seventh, and a slip confirming the disappearance of Dr. Roman Karski.

"On Christmas Eve," Rawicz stated crisply, "Captain *Grot* communicated to the accused—as an explicit counter-intelligence integrity test—the precise intersection and minute of the December twenty-seventh liquidation strike against Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler. On the morning of December twenty-seventh, two contradictory anomalies occurred simultaneously. First: Schoeler kept his exact Monday timetable into the kill-zone without extra escort, which ordinarily clears the accused of leaking the route. Second, however: fourteen minutes prior to H-hour, sixty *Sicherheitspolizei* troops under SS-Sturmbannführer Reinhardt raided Number Fourteen Wilcza one hundred fifty yards west, forcing *Grot* to abort the strike—and during the resulting tire-blowout skid on Ujazdowskie, Captain *Grot* personally observed the accused standing sixty yards from the ambush point, where Oberstleutnant Schoeler leaped from his damaged vehicle and shielded the accused's head and body with his own spine against potential gunfire."

All three pairs of eyes locked onto Sylwia under the hissing blue-white carbide glare.

Marek stared straight at the plank edge in front of his knuckles, jaw clamped so tight a muscle jumped beneath his ear. He hadn't assassinated her in the dark; torn in half between duty and four years of loving her shadow, he had dragged it honestly before the law even knowing the law carried lead.

Sylwia didn't lower her eyes by a millimeter.

"Had Captain *Grot* waited forty-eight hours before convening judges," Sylwia said in clear, unhurried Polish that rang cleanly off fourteen-century brick, "your own *Oddział II* registry at Aleja Szucha would have explained Number Fourteen Wilcza for you. Have your clerks checked why Dr. Roman Karski vanished from Infant Jesus Hospital at eight-thirty on Monday morning?"

Rawicz's grey eyebrows twitched fractionally. "Precinct circulars list Karski missing since December twenty-seventh—suspected by Reinhardt's office of having been our informant *Jaskółka*."

"Not *our* informant—*theirs*," Sylwia cut across him like cold surgical steel. "Roman Karski was Otto Reinhardt's double-agent *Jaskółka* inside Pavilion Three! Karski was the leak who tipped your Wola cell onto Schoeler's car in October on Reinhardt's strings, Karski gave Reinhardt our courier Tomek's movement schedule at Lindleya, and Karski was reporting my movements daily in exchange for morphine and forged Vittel postcards from his murdered Jewish fiancée!"

Now both Rawicz and the white-haired magistrate leaned sharply forward over the grey blanket. For sixteen weeks *Jaskółka* had bled Left Bank courier cells white without leaving a scent.

"Using ultraviolet anthracene powder on our hospital payphone and a salted disinfection schedule," Sylwia continued relentlessly, "I caught Karski red-handed before Christmas. On Sunday evening, December twenty-sixth, using an internal August liquidation manifest proving Reinhardt had already murdered Karski's fiancée at Pawiak four months ago, I broke Karski cleanly in our pathology basement. At eight-ten Monday morning, Karski telephoned his final poisoned report into Aleja Szucha—sending sixty of Reinhardt's SS men charging into four empty Lutheran laundry cellars on Wilcza!"

"Conveniently," Marek spoke up for the first time, voice low and raw as scraped iron, finally lifting tormented dark eyes straight into hers, "clearing six Kedyw Sten guns off Piękna fourteen minutes before Ludwig Schoeler's car rolled through!"

"Saving six of *your* boys from walking straight into two armored MG-42 half-tracks Reinhardt already had patrolling Koszykowa on holiday alert, while shattering Reinhardt's credibility with Kutschera and leaving Schoeler alive long enough to put *this* in my coat pocket last night!"

Unpicking three threads inside her left coat lapel with steady nails, Sylwia drew out the tiny aluminum Minox cassette and set it squarely in the middle of the grey blanket under the magistrate's spectacles.

"Develop that before midnight," she challenged calmly. "Two days ago Red Army spearheads crossed the pre-war Polish border at Sarny. On that film are fourteen frames of OKH Army Group Centre's January contingency demolition directive—*Fall Warschau*—detailing exact explosive tonnage, borehole diagrams, and pioneer wiring chambers already being cut inside Kierbedź Bridge, Poniatowski Bridge, the Cross-City rail tunnel, the Powiśle municipal power plant, and the Lindleya filtration waterworks to turn Warsaw into uninhabitable rubble before retreat!"

An electric silence dropped across the cellar vault. Even the Sten-gunner by the iron door stiffened slightly. Every officer in the Home Army High Command knew that capturing German bridge-and-power demolition wiring charts before an uprising was worth ten dead colonels—worth an entire division.

*Sędzia* picked up the tiny aluminum film cassette between thumb and forefinger, studying Sylwia across steel-rimmed lenses for five long seconds.

"Ten minutes' recess to inspect emulsion markings in the side chamber," the magistrate ordered dryly, rising with Rawicz and carrying one carbide lamp behind the timbered arch. "Captain *Grot*, remain with the witness."

The inner oak door latched shut.

Left alone in the half-dark vault with one hissing blue flame between them, Marek rose slowly from the bench and walked around the plank table until he stood three feet in front of Sylwia. Up close, four winters of cellars, cyanide teeth, and dead nineteen-year-old boys had carved deep vertical trenches beside his mouth.

"Drop the courtroom speeches for sixty seconds, Sylwia," Marek whispered roughly in the shadows. "Rawicz didn't hold those binoculars on Ujazdowskie—I did. I know how a soldier drops flat behind an engine block when lead flies, and I saw how Ludwig Schoeler threw his bare back over your skull in that snowbank without even drawing his Luger. Look me in the eyes right now—on Tomek's grave—and tell me you haven't let that German into your bed."

Every instinct of self-preservation screamed at her to lie—one brisk, indignant slap across Marek's cheek, *Have you gone mad with jealousy, Grot? He grabbed the nearest Polish coat for human shield!* Marek desperately *wanted* to believe a lie; give him half an excuse and he'd argue her innocent till dawn.

Looking up at the brave, scarred man who had shared his last crust of bread with her in the typhus winter of forty, Sylwia found she respected both Marek Zawadzki and what beat inside her own ribs far too much to hand him cheap brass.

"No," Sylwia said very quietly.

Marek flinched backward half an inch as if she had driven a #11 scalpel straight between his ribs. All color drained beneath his dark stubble. "Mother of God... Sylwia. A Wehrmacht colonel. While boys like Zbyszek and Tomek rot in lime—"

"While forty Kampinos partisans walk alive through snow tonight, six of your Sten gunners eat supper instead of hanging from balconies, Roman Karski's leak is plugged forever, and Powiśle power plant's demolition diagrams sit in Rawicz's hand behind that door!" Sylwia whispered back, fierce and unbowed, storm-grey eyes shining clear in the carbide hiss. "I didn't ask to see Ludwig Schoeler carried onto my table in October, Marek, and I won't insult four years of fighting beside you with fairy tales! He belongs to the German wing trying to blow Hitler to hell before Europe turns to cinders—and what passes behind closed doors between my soul and his has cost Poland zero drops of blood and bought you gold! If that makes me a traitor in your ledger, pull your Vis right now and save Rawicz a bullet!"

For five agonizing seconds Marek's right hand actually twitched two inches above his sheepskin coat pocket—agony, humiliation, rage, and helpless awe warring visibly across his lean face—before his fist clenched empty at his side and he turned half away toward the damp brick wall, swallowing hard.

"God damn both of you to hell," Marek breathed huskily just as the inner oak latch clicked open and *Sędzia* and Rawicz filed back behind the plank table—Rawicz holding a dripping wet strip of developed negative up toward the carbide flame with a magnifying loupe, eyes gleaming hard as flint.

*Kierbedź Bridge Pier Three... Powiśle Turbine Hall demolition conduits.* Authentic beyond forgery.

Sitting back down, the white-haired magistrate folded thin hands cleanly over the grey wool.

"The negative is genuine strategic grade," *Sędzia* pronounced quietly. "Unmasking Referat Four's agent *Jaskółka* is corroborated. Nevertheless, unauthorized operational autonomy with an enemy staff officer places Sub-Lieutenant *Kora* half a step from summary execution."

His ice-water eyes locked squarely onto hers.

"Judgment of this Court is suspended for twenty-five days—until January thirty-first—upon fulfillment of one final directive. Yesterday London and our Aleja Szucha cell confirmed that SS-Sturmbannführer Otto Reinhardt maintains inside his personal safe a bound black-leather master register—referred to in Referat Four dispatches as the *Kronenbuch*, the Crown Ledger—containing the true identities of thirty-eight active Gestapo informants across Warsaw alongside target arrest lists for an imminent mid-winter liquidation sweep code-named *Aktion Wintersturm*, aimed directly at Home Army medical and intelligence commands."

Rawicz leaned forward into the blue light, voice dry as coffin nails:

"You claim your German colonel hates Reinhardt enough to hand us bridge charts, Kora. Prove it. Use Schoeler to locate and deliver Otto Reinhardt's *Kronenbuch* into our hands before January thirty-first—and the hour that ledger reaches us, whether by your hand or ours, Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler's usefulness to Poland ends permanently. Fail, and sentence executes without further hearing. Hood the witness."
