# CHAPTER FIFTEEN

## Hotel Polski

**Warsaw — December 26–27, 1943**

Down in the subterranean pathology wing beneath Infant Jesus Hospital's north courtyard, twenty-below December frost seeped straight through three-foot granite foundation walls and coated the white floor tiles in glistening hoarfrost.

Four zinc-lined mortuary drawers stood latched along the east wall; down the center of the low-vaulted dissecting room ran two grooved Carrara marble autopsy tables smelling permanently of forty-percent formalin, carbolic acid, and cold tallow. Above Table One burned a single green-shaded hundred-watt porcelain lamp, casting stark chalk shadows across rows of stoppered glass specimen jars where cirrhotic livers and tuberculous lungs floated grey and timeless in yellow fluid.

At five minutes past seven on Sunday evening—Saint Stephen's night, when snow drove horizontally across empty Warsaw courtyards and only skeleton crews staffed the upper pavilions—Dr. Roman Karski pushed open the heavy zinc-sheathed swing door at the foot of the basement stairs, shivering inside his unbuttoned white doctor's coat.

"Pani Doktor?" Karski called nervously, squinting past the hanging lamp. Five minutes ago night porter Władek had knocked on Admissions to say Frau Doktor Kotarska needed a second physician's counter-signature on an unclaimed street-exposure corpse before the municipal wagon arrived. Yet both marble slabs lay bare and dry. "Władek said you had a frostbite mortality down—"

Behind his back, the outer iron corridor grille clicked shut softly, and Hauptmann Viktor Brandt slipped a heavy brass padlock through the staple from the stairwell side.

Karski spun violently around—pinprick pupils widening in instant animal panic—and saw two people standing motionless inside the room.

Beside the specimen shelves stood Sylwia in her dark wool coat, hands empty at her sides. And stepping slowly out of the shadowed archway behind Table Two, tall in an unmarked dark-charcoal civilian winter loden coat worn open over crimson-striped General Staff riding breeches, stood Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler.

For two heartbeats Karski's breath rattled dry through parted yellowing teeth. No trapped creature needed explanations; one look at Sylwia Kotarska standing unguarded beside the German colonel he had been reporting on to Otto Reinhardt for eight weeks told Roman Karski his tightrope had snapped over the pit.

His right hand plunged inside his white coat pocket and jerked out a blued-steel 6.35-millimeter pocket Sauer automatic—bought off a drunken Luftwaffe corporal in forty-one for suicide if Pawiak ever knocked, safety snapped off with a frantic *click* as the short barrel wavered wildly between Sylwia's chest and Ludwig's throat.

"Stay back—don't move a step, either of you!" Karski's voice cracked half an octave high, echoing thinly off cold tiles while morphine-starved sweat beaded shiny across his greenish forehead. "I knew it—Reinhardt suspected it from the first week! A Polish surgeon whoring for *Abwehr* behind locked doors! One pull of this trigger and I walk straight out to the SD post on Nowogrodzka and tell Sturmbannführer Reinhardt I caught you two conspiring against him!"

Neither Sylwia nor Ludwig raised a hand or backed half an inch.

Standing six feet across the grooved marble dissecting slab from the trembling pistol muzzle, Ludwig didn't even glance toward the gun. Reaching calmly inside his dark loden breast pocket with his right hand, he drew out two folded photostatic documents and slid them smoothly across slick white Carrara marble until they came to rest directly under the hundred-watt lamp two feet from Karski's elbow.

"Before you run to Nowogrodzka to collect your next five ampoules of Bayer morphine, *Jaskółka*," Ludwig said in quiet, level German devoid of threat or heat, "read Line Twenty-Nine under the lamp."

At his code name spoken aloud out of Ludwig Schoeler's mouth, Karski jerked as though struck across the face with wet wire. "I don't know what—"

"Read the sheet, Roman," Sylwia said softly in Polish. Not angry; horribly, unbearably tired. "We already photographed the quinine-anthracene ultraviolet dye on your right thumb from the courtyard payphone and the pharmacy safe two weeks ago. If we wanted you shot in an alley by Kedyw, you wouldn't have lived to eat Christmas Eve wafer on Friday. Look at the marble."

Hypnotized against his will by that terrible quietness in her voice, keeping the little Sauer wavering chest-high in his right fist, Karski edged one shuffling step sideways to the head of the autopsy slab and looked down under the glare of the porcelain shade.

Top sheet first: his own enlarged right thumbprint photographed beside a calibration ruler, glowing bright spectral white from fluorescent quinine crystals transferred off Schedule Alpha.

Beneath it lay the second photostat.

Eagle-and-swastika letterhead of *Der Kommandeur der Sicherheitspolizei und des SD für den Distrikt Warschau — Referat IV-A2*. Date stamp: **19. August 1943**. Heading: **Vollzugsmeldung — Sondertransport Hotel Polski (Liste B, Südamerika-Pässe)**.

Karski's free left hand gripped the cold raised blood-groove rim of the marble slab. His lips moved soundlessly over German administrative columns he understood all too fluently after four years of occupation decrees. Down past *Goldberg... Goldfarb...*

**29. Goldblum, Estera — Geb. 14.V.1916 Warschau (Paraguay-Pass Nr. 419-B). Sonderbehandlung vollzogen — Pawiak / Ruinengebiet Dzielna, 19.VIII.1943, 05.30 Uhr.**

*Effekten verwertet: 1 Damenmantel (Wolle), 1 Paar Schuhe, 1 Goldmedaillon (Geigenform, 14 Karat, 11,2 g).*

*One gold locket, violin-shaped, fourteen karat, eleven point two grams.*

And at the foot of the column, signed cleanly in flowing fountain-pen script: **O. Reinhardt, SS-Sturmbannf.**

Silence hummed inside the freezing pathology vault like high-tension current. Somewhere behind the brick wall, subterranean water dripped slowly into a floor drain—*plink... plink... plink*.

Karski let out a strange, rusty laugh that sounded like torn tin scraping stone, shaking his head rapidly from side to side while spittle wet his chin.

"Cheap *Abwehr* forgery," Karski whispered wildly, thrusting the little automatic two inches closer toward Ludwig's chest as though lead could shoot holes through August nineteenth. "Do you think I was born yesterday, Herr Oberstleutnant?! Typewriters take five minutes to fake! I saw Estera's own handwriting eight days ago at Dworzec Gdański—blue French stationery from Vittel camp in the Vosges! *Najdroższy Romku, dają mleko i czekamy na wymianę!* Nobody on earth knew she called me *Romku* with that curl on the *R* except me!"

"Nobody," Sylwia said very gently from six feet away, "except whoever read the three old letters sitting inside that gold violin-shaped locket when they stripped her coat off at Pawiak on August nineteenth, Roman. Did you ever tell Otto Reinhardt what shape locket you bought her on Nowy Świat when she passed her conservatory jury in thirty-eight?"

Karski's mouth stayed half open. No sound came out.

"And think back to that blue card Reinhardt flashed for two seconds before pulling it out of your fingers," Sylwia continued quietly, ruthlessly, cutting dead gangrene down to living bone because mercy lied and anatomy never did. "You showed me Estera's concert invitation once before the war. Educated in Lwów, wasn't she? Lwów girls cross the Polish *ł* right across the top tip of the stem like a T. How was *dają mleko* crossed on Reinhardt's blue paper?"

Behind Karski's bloodshot eyes, memory developed like photographic plate dropped into acid: cramped grey pencil lead on pale blue paper across a mahogany pharmacy table, *dają mleko*, the diagonal slash cutting straight through the middle belly of the *ł* in neat Warsaw clerk style—and Reinhardt sliding the card swiftly back inside calfskin before Karski's wet thumb could touch fresh graphite.

*Eleven point two grams. Violin-shaped.*

He had never once mentioned a violin locket to Otto Reinhardt.

Slowly, as if every tendon behind his knees had been severed simultaneously with a razor, Roman Karski slid downward along the white-tiled pedestal of Autopsy Table One until both knees hit frosted porcelain with a dull *thud*.

The cocked 6.35-millimeter Sauer dropped out of slackening fingers—bouncing once without discharging—and skidded across the tiles to stop against the toe of Ludwig's riding boot. Ludwig stooped unhurriedly, clicked the safety catch back on, and slipped the tiny pistol into his loden coat pocket.

Doubled completely in half with his forehead pressed flat against the freezing blood-gutter of the marble dissecting slab, hands clawing into his own thinning hair, Roman Karski made a low, protracted, sub-human howling sound deep behind clenched teeth—four months of filthy betrayal, selling Tomek's life, selling hospital nights, groveling in cinders for twenty-milligram vials of poppy juice while Estera's bones had lain dissolved under quicklime in the Dzielna rubble since high summer.

Sylwia stood watching him without moving. Part of her remembered Tomek's four torn nail-beds down in Cell Four and felt zero pity; the surgeon half looked down at a man whose soul Otto Reinhardt had vivisected alive without anesthetic.

After ninety seconds the dry retching subsided. Karski lifted a face aged twenty years—chalk-grey, smeared with tears and frost-condensation off the marble—and looked blindly up at Sylwia.

"Give me back that pistol, Sylwia," Karski whispered in Polish. "One round in the mouth over the drain—Władek washes the tiles in five minutes and nobody wastes rope on me."

"Killing yourself on my floor tonight gives Otto Reinhardt clean warning by nine tomorrow morning and buys Estera nothing," Sylwia answered coldly, kneeling on one knee three feet away from him so her storm-grey eyes locked straight into his shattered gaze. "Do you want to die clean like a dog hiding under stairs, Roman, or do you want to drive four inches of broken glass down Otto Reinhardt's throat before you go?"

Life—ugly, vengeful, blazing black—flared instantly inside Karski's hollowed sockets. "Tell me how."

Now Sylwia spoke with brisk, crystalline precision—switching back to German so Ludwig caught every syllable of the mechanism as she built it in midair:

"Reinhardt ordered you to bring him hard operational treachery before New Year's or Estera boarded a train on December twenty-eighth—tomorrow morning. At zero-eight-ten tomorrow morning, from the public payphone inside the Marszałkowska tram shelter, you ring Referat Four's emergency flash number using your *Jaskółka* parity code. Tell Krauss or Reinhardt breathlessly that ten minutes earlier an injured Home Army courier stumbled into Minor Dressing whispering that *Oddział II* High Command and three British radio officers are holding an emergency forty-minute courier rendezvous beginning at zero-eight-twenty-five sharp inside the cellars of Number Fourteen ulica Wilcza, corner of Koszykowa—with lookouts posted north to Piękna!"

Across the marble autopsy slab, Ludwig Schoeler went completely, monumentally still.

Not a blink betrayed him in front of Karski, yet Sylwia didn't even have to raise her eyes to feel his razor-sharp Prussian brain triangulate *ulica Wilcza, Koszykowa, lookouts north to Piękna at zero-eight-twenty-five Monday morning*—exactly one hundred eighty meters west of the Aleje Ujazdowskie and Piękna intersection where Ludwig's own staff Mercedes slowed to second gear every Monday morning at zero-eight-forty-five!

"Number Fourteen Wilcza is a bombed-out Lutheran parish Deaconess laundry empty since forty-one," Sylwia continued smoothly to Karski. "Hungry after failing in Kampinos, Reinhardt won't delegate a prize that fat to precinct police—he'll scramble four fast SD pursuit cars and two armored half-tracks straight up Aleje Ujazdowskie sirens screaming at eight-twenty-eight to throw a steel ring around Wilcza, Koszykowa, and Piękna! By nine-fifteen, when fifty frozen SS men smash down six empty cellar doors and find dead rats in front of Kutschera's observers, Reinhardt looks like a gullible fool manipulated by Polish shadows."

"And after eight-ten tomorrow morning, when I hang up that payphone?" Karski asked huskily. Within two hours of coming up empty on Wilcza, Reinhardt's execution squads would tear Warsaw apart hunting *Jaskółka*'s skin.

"In my satchel upstairs," Sylwia said quietly, "is an unstamped suburban parish baptismal pass under the name *Jan Kowalczyk* and two hundred zlotys for the nine-o'clock narrow-gauge *kolejka* train out toward Grójec. If you reach partisan country and scrub typhus floors under another name until the Russians come, what remains between you and God is your affair, Roman. Never set foot inside Warsaw again."

Karski stared at her for three silent seconds—amazed that she handed him even a thread of escape rather than aconitine—nodded twice rapidly, wiped his mouth on his sleeve, and walked woodenly up the basement stairs once Brandt unlocked the grille and handed over the parish slip upstairs.

Left alone for two minutes beside the frosted marble slabs while Brandt escorted Karski out toward the gate, Sylwia turned to pick up her lantern—and found Ludwig blocking the narrow tiled aisle between Table One and Table Two, hands resting inside the pockets of his dark charcoal loden coat, slate-blue eyes cold and bright as winter stars under the green porcelain shade.

"Eight-forty-five tomorrow morning at the corner of Ujazdowskie and Piękna," Ludwig said softly. Not a question.

Sylwia stopped with the brass bail of the lantern in her gloved hand, lifting her chin to meet his eyes head-on. Lying now was pointless.

"Six men," she answered quietly. "Three British Stens and two Gammon charges. Rawicz had Marek give me the exact corner and minute on Christmas Eve afternoon—so that if you canceled your Monday drive to Krakowskie Przedmieście, switched streets, or doubled your escort, Counter-Intelligence would know beyond doubt I warned you, and execute me by Monday noon."

For four full heartbeats Ludwig looked down at her in the freezing white-tiled morgue—comprehending in one blinding flash what she had carried silently behind her ribs all through Christmas Eve night while eating blood-orange segments out of his fingers and kissing the scar over his brow without breathing a single word to shift the burden onto his shoulders.

Instead, inside forty-eight hours, she had engineered a bank-shot off Otto Reinhardt himself: flooding the ambush staging block with fifty screaming Gestapo sirens fifteen minutes before H-hour so Marek's assault squad aborted cleanly without losing a single Polish fighter, Ludwig drove right past Piękna at eight-forty-five on schedule without altering one hair of his routine to trigger Rawicz's death warrant on her, and Reinhardt swallowed poisoned air.

"God Almighty, Kotarska," Ludwig breathed, stepping close enough in the formalin-scented dark to frame both sides of her cold face inside his warm ungloved palms, tilting her chin up fiercely. "You sat across the hearth from me Friday night holding sixty hours to my execution and yours without blinking once?"

"Had I told you Friday night," Sylwia whispered fiercely back, fingers gripping his thick wool lapels, "you'd have done something intolerably noble and Prussian like arresting me visibly or storming Rawicz's cellar and getting both of us shot! Promise me right now, Ludwig—drive out of Number Twelve tomorrow morning at eight-forty-one sharp with no extra escort, same Mercedes, same speed up Ujazdowskie. Once Reinhardt's sirens flood Wilcza at eight-thirty, Marek pulls the red abort cord before your wheels ever turn off Aleja Róż!"

"Agreed," Ludwig said steadily, kissing her cold mouth once—hard, brief, electric—before releasing her cheeks. "On one condition: keep your tall Polish neck inside Pavilion Three's operating theater until nine-thirty tomorrow morning. Nowhere near Ujazdowskie."

"I have a cholecystectomy scheduled at nine," she replied smoothly—which was entirely true, omitting only that at eight-thirty-five she intended walking past the *Apotheke Wendt* pharmacy annex two hundred yards north of Piękna with surgical gauze requisitions just far enough to hear Reinhardt's sirens arrive on time, because no surgeon worth salt closed an abdomen without inspecting the tie personally.

***

Monday, December twenty-seventh dawned steel-grey and bitter clear at minus twelve, frozen breath hanging like white gunsmoke four feet above packed snowbanks along wide, lime-lined Aleje Ujazdowskie.

Up behind the cracked attic dormer of Number Twenty-Two—a four-story corner tenement overlooking the waist-high municipal snow-barriers narrowing the Piękna intersection—Marek Zawadzki crouched on splintered rafters in a white painter's smock over his sheepskin coat, six-power Zeiss field binoculars pressed tight to his eyes and a red linen abort kerchief gripped ready beside the sill.

Down below, concealed inside a baker's delivery handcart on Piękna, a doorway coal-bin, and a second-floor balcony, six young Kedyw assault veterans checked Sten gun bolts under rags as the gilt hands on Marek's wristwatch crept across **08:29**.

Sixteen minutes until Ludwig Schoeler's black Wehrmacht Mercedes 320 turned north off Aleja Róż four blocks south.

*And Sylwia stays safe at Lindleya,* Marek thought fiercely, swallowing dry ash in his throat. *Let this bastard die clean on the tram tracks at eight-forty-five, Rawicz tears up her file, and this nightmare ends.*

**08:31.**

Suddenly, tearing northward straight out of Aleja Szucha half a mile south, erupted the unmistakable, blood-freezing three-tone banshee wail of SD pursuit sirens—not one car, an entire flying column!

Four open-topped grey Mercedes troop carriers bristling with black-coated SS riflemen, led by Otto Reinhardt's closed command sedan and followed by two roaring Sd.Kfz. 251 armored half-tracks mounting heavy MG-42 machine guns, thundered up Aleje Ujazdowskie at fifty miles an hour, slewing violently leftward on chained tires directly across Piękna and Koszykowa to screech sideways across ulica Wilcza one hundred fifty yards west! Whistles shrilled; barked German megaphone commands echoed off stone walls—*"Gesamtes Straßenviertel abriegeln! Dächer besetzen!"*—as sixty heavily armed SS troops poured across Koszykowa right behind the escape alley of Marek's assault squad!

"Christ's wounds—raid on Wilcza right across our rear exit!" hissed Marek's spotter beside him on the rafters. "If we fire a single Sten burst here with two armored MG half-tracks parked one block west, none of our boys clear Koszykowa alive!"

Marek didn't hesitate two seconds—throwing six crack Kedyw soldiers and three precious Stens away into sixty alerted SS machine-gunners violated every rule of underground command. Jerking the bright crimson abort kerchief twice outside the dormer slit—*Abort! Scatter singly east toward Wiejska!*—he watched five of his disguised fighters instantly melt away down eastbound courtyard passages before the outer SS cordon reached Piękna.

Only nineteen-year-old *Orzeł*—crouched farthest forward inside a deep snow-trench behind a municipal sand-bin right on the southwest curb of Piękna, view of the high dormer blocked by an overhanging icicle cornice and ears deafened by roaring half-track diesels—hadn't seen the red cloth yet!

Sweating in the freezing rafters, Marek raised two fingers to whistle sharp down into the street—and froze as his Zeiss lenses swept fifty yards north along the east sidewalk of Aleje Ujazdowskie.

Walking briskly southward from the corner German surgical-supply depot carrying a wrapped brown pharmacy parcel, tall in her familiar charcoal wool coat and dark furred Cossack cap, came **Sylwia Kotarska**—slowing instinctively at the edge of the crowd pinned back along the sidewalk curb by *Schutzpolizei* traffic whistles clearing the avenue for Reinhardt's follow-up trucks.

*What in God's name is she doing on Ujazdowskie at eight-forty-two?!* Marek's heart slammed against his ribs.

And at **08:44**, punctual to the German second despite sirens wailing one block west on Wilcza, out of Aleja Róż four blocks south turned Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler's solitary black Wehrmacht Mercedes staff car, crimson Generalstab pennant fluttering stiffly from the left front fender—proving beyond a shadow of a doubt to Rawicz's distant street observer back by Chopin's Park that Schoeler had received zero warning to alter his Monday timetable!

Down behind the sand-bin, isolated young *Orzeł* saw the crimson fender pennant slow to second gear twenty yards away—saw two SD motorcycle outriders suddenly swing around from Koszykowa behind it—realized the rest of his squad had vanished, panicked in nineteen-year-old desperation not to miss the target alone, and poked the stubby barrel of his British Sten gun over the snowbank!

Up in the dormer Marek roared *"NIE!"* into empty air.

*Brrrrp-pop!*

Frozen grease inside the cheap British sheet-metal magazine jammed hard on the fourth round—sending three wild nine-millimeter bullets stitching diagonally across the icy granite cobbles: one shattering the Mercedes's right front headlamp, the second punching straight through the left front tire in an explosive white burst of rubber and compressed air, the third whining in ricochet off a tram standard while *Orzeł* dropped the jammed Sten under snow and bolted cleanly down a cellar coal-passage toward Wiejska as SD motorcycles roared past in the wrong direction!

On packed sheet-ice, blowing a front tire at twenty-five miles an hour turned two tons of steel Mercedes into an unguided sled.

Driver wrenching desperately leftward away from an oncoming lorry, the big black staff car spun one hundred eighty degrees across the gleaming steel tram tracks in a screaming shower of ice and sparks, rear bumper smashing through a cast-iron streetlamp bollard ten feet from the eastern sidewalk where trapped pedestrians scattered screaming—sliding broadside straight toward the waist-high frozen snowbank pinning Sylwia against a stone courtyard pillar!

Before the slewing chassis had even ground to a dead halt against the snowbank—while overhead cast-iron streetlamp glass rained down in jagged twenty-pound shards and two panicked *Schupo* constables fifty yards off unslung carbines firing wildly toward shadows—the rear right passenger door of the canted Mercedes burst open.

Out onto the slick ice leaped Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler without his cap.

He did not dive behind his own steel engine block for cover against a second submachine-gun burst. He did not draw his pistol. Looking nowhere along the empty ambush windows across the street, moving on pure, blinding, instantaneous animal instinct before thought ever formed, Ludwig cleared the crumpled running board in one bound, caught Sylwia around the shoulders as five pounds of shattered cast-iron lantern housing crashed onto the sidewalk inches from her boots, dragged her straight down beneath him into the shelter of the stone pillar recess, and locked his right forearm and spread left hand tight over the back of her skull and neck—exposing the entire broad field-grey expanse of his own spine and unarmored back squarely to whatever gunmen still aimed down from Piękna's windows.

Eighty yards overhead behind the dark attic dormer, six-power German Zeiss lenses brought every millimeter of that embrace into razor-sharp, merciless magnification.

Marek Zawadzki saw Ludwig's ungloved fingers buried fiercely, protectively in Sylwia's dark hair to shield her temple from stone; saw Sylwia's bare left hand—her glove had fallen in the snow—clutching not away in terror from an enemy's weight, but gripping tight into the field-grey wool beneath Ludwig's right ribs as she lifted her face two inches under his chin to check instantly whether blood stained his throat.

Slowly, inside the freezing attic, Marek lowered the binoculars from his eyes.

Down in the street whistles shrilled, Brandt leaped from the front seat pistol drawn shouting *"Nicht schießen—Reifenschaden!"* to stop wild police fire, and inside thirty seconds Ludwig was already standing cold and imperious again, brushing snow off his field-grey knee without looking back at the Polish woman doctor picking up her spilled surgical-gauze parcel as if he had merely stumbled into a bystander leaving a wrecked car.

To fifty panicking policemen and bystanders on Ujazdowskie, it had lasted four seconds in smoke and flying snow.

Up in the dark rafters, leaning his back silently against frozen brick while winter wind cut straight through his ribs, Marek Zawadzki closed his eyes and felt the last warm ember inside his chest turn black and dead as cinders.
