# CHAPTER EIGHT

## Abitur

**Berlin — May to June 1930**

By the spring of nineteen-thirty, the champagne in Berlin had gone sour at the bottom of the glass, though the bands played twice as loud to drown out the marching boots.

Every morning as Counselor Kotarski's Mercedes carried Sylwia eastward from Kurfürstenstraße toward the Spree, pasted election bills peeled off damp hoardings in red and black shrieks—swastikas over Wedding, hammer-and-sickle fists over Neukölln, six million pairs of idle hands shoved into frayed pockets outside shuttered machine works after the New York crash. Even inside the tall, tranquil brick walls of Reichstagsufer Six, shadows lengthened: Ruth Oppenheim's father quietly transferred half his banking house to Amsterdam in April, and at supper on May tenth Kazimierz Kotarski laid down his silver fish-knife and told Sylwia that Foreign Minister Zaleski had recalled him to Warsaw effective June twenty-second.

"Germany has caught typhus of the soul, *córeczko*," her father said quietly, watching twilight turn the chestnut blossoms purple outside the tall windows. "Stresemann is dead, Brüning rules by presidential decree, and inside three years either the generals or the beer-hall screamers will own the Wilhelmstraße. You will sit your *Abitur* next month so no Prussian ever claims you left unfinished, and in October you will enter the medical faculty at the University of Warsaw."

Sylwia nodded calmly, buttered a slice of rye bread, went upstairs to her bedroom, shut the oak door, and stood staring out toward the Tiergarten for twenty minutes while an inexplicable hollow ache—sharp as hunger, humiliating as cowardice—clawed behind her ribs.

She told herself she was furious merely because three weeks left barely enough time to beat Ludwig Schoeler cleanly in the Prussian State *Abitur*.

At eighteen—she would turn nineteen in September, Ludwig nineteen in July—their legendary three-year war had drawn spectators from across the Berlin school board. Bets ran three-to-one in the masters' common room on a dead heat, and when the week-long written papers concluded on June twelfth, Privy Councilor Dr. Hagemann of the Provincial School Collegium took the unprecedented step of presiding personally over the final oral examinations in the Great Aula.

All morning under the tall portraits of Frederick the Great and Jean Calvin, Ludwig and Sylwia fenced across four disciplines like two young maîtres d'armes who had forgotten the rest of the room existed.

When Hagemann tested Latin sight rhetoric from Tacitus's *Agricola*—*Ubi solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant*, where they make a desert, they call it peace—Ludwig delivered an icy, architectural analysis of Roman frontier pacification that had two Reichswehr observers nodding approval; Sylwia followed immediately by dissecting Tacitus's syntax as an indictment of imperial self-delusion so razor-sharp that Herr Doktor Glass hid a grin behind three fingers. In organic chemistry they tied without a slip; in differential calculus Ludwig finished a surface-integral proof on the slate four seconds ahead of Sylwia, only for Sylwia to tap his third line with chalk on her way past and point out a dropped negative sign he corrected half a heartbeat before Hagemann turned his spectacles.

At five o'clock, after forty minutes behind locked oak doors while sixty graduating seniors sweated in stiff collars and white blouses down in the courtyard, Oberstudiendirektor Becker emerged onto the stone steps and read the cumulative three-year *Reifeprüfung* averages aloud.

*Schoeler, Ludwig Heinrich: Eins Komma Null Null.*
*Kotarska, Sylwia Maria: Eins Komma Null Null.*

An audible gasp ran across the courtyard. Never since the *Französisches Gymnasium* admitted female pupils had two candidates tied to the hundredth decimal at absolute perfection.

"Inasmuch," Becker continued stiffly, clearing his throat over the cream parchment while avoiding Doktor Glass's sardonic single eye, "as the statutes of the Humboldt Foundation provide for a single recipient of the silver *Primus Omnium* medallion and valedictory chair, the Collegium awards precedence to Senior Matriculant Ludwig Schoeler in recognition of continuous enrollment since *Sexta* and prospective service to the State."

Polite, scattered applause rose. Ruth Oppenheim grabbed Sylwia's cold wrist indignantly—*"It's highway robbery because you're a girl and Polish!"*—while ten yards away under the linden tree, tall in dark blue worsted, Ludwig stood holding the flat blue-velvet medallion box without opening it, looking straight across thirty bobbing heads into Sylwia's storm-grey eyes.

He was not smiling. For the first time in three years, winning tasted manifestly of ashes to him too.

"Pack your evening trunk last," Sylwia told her maid briskly on the afternoon of Saturday, June twenty-first—Midsummer Eve, ten hours before the seven-forty Danzig-Warsaw express pulled out of Friedrichstraße Station. "Leave out the red Paris crepe."

Kazimierz raised one silver brow over his strapped leather dispatch boxes in the stripped, echoing drawing room. "You still intend going out to Wannsee tonight? Generalmajor Schoeler's invitation was strictly pro forma to the graduating class—and his nephew Horst struts around Tiergarten in brown breeches now."

"If I stay home tonight, Papa," Sylwia said serenely, fastening small pearl drops at her ears before the bare mantel mirror, "Ludwig Schoeler will tell himself for the next fifty years that I hid under my bed crying over a piece of Prussian silver."

At nine-thirty that hot, oppressive June evening, air hung motionless and sultry as warm velvet above the black mirror of the Wannsee. Far to the west beyond Potsdam, silent copper lightning pulsed inside towering anvil clouds without thunder yet. Across the wide terraced lawns of the Schoeler villa on Große Seestraße, strings of glowing paper lanterns swayed faintly above white-draped champagne tables while a five-piece Berlin dance band played *Ich bin von Kopf bis Fuß auf Liebe eingestellt*—Marlene Dietrich's languid, shameless new song from *Der blaue Engel*—sending muted trumpet notes drifting out across the reeds and moored racing yachts.

When Sylwia descended the stone garden steps alone—her father had wisely pleaded farewell calls at the French Embassy and sent his chauffeur to wait outside the iron gates at midnight—conversation rippled and stalled across three tables.

She had left behind every trace of schoolgirl navy wool. The Parisian bias-cut gown of deep arterial-crimson silk crepe clung like poured wine over tall, long-limbed curves nineteen years had suddenly ripened, cut daringly low in a clean V down her bare back to the small hollow above her waist, dark chestnut hair brushed out of braids for once into heavy, shining waves pinned back behind one ear with an antique silver Mazovian leaf-clasp of her mother's.

Standing near the balustrade flanked by two blond *Herrenreiter* cousins and a plump girl in pink tulle hanging onto his sleeve, Ludwig stopped dead in the middle of lifting a champagne flute.

In white summer mess-style evening waistcoat and black tails, sun-bronzed along his lean cheekbones from June sculling on the lake, tarnished-gold hair gleaming under the terrace lamps and that thin white crescent scar pale above his right brow, he looked so arrestingly, furiously handsome that Sylwia's stomach dropped two inches before pride locked her spine straight.

For forty minutes neither approached the other. Sylwia drank one glass of dry Moselle, danced a brisk foxtrot with loyal Kurt von Bredow—who blushed crimson down to his collar stud holding her bare waist—accepted Doktor Glass's dry farewell blessing by the punch bowl, and waited until ten-fifteen when the first fat, warm drops of thunderstorm rain hissed against the paper lanterns, driving forty laughing guests indoors toward the ballroom buffet.

Slipping unseen down the hedged gravel path away from the house instead of up the steps, Sylwia walked swift and barefoot—carrying her crimson satin slippers in one hand so the heels wouldn't sink into wet turf—straight down onto the long cedar pier jutting into the dark lake.

Ten seconds later, rapid footsteps crunched down the gravel behind her.

She didn't need to turn around to know whose stride measured thirty-four inches. Thunder rolled low and heavy across the dark water as warm rain abruptly thickened from scattered drops into a pounding silver sheet, plastering crimson silk instantly against her hips and cooling her bare shoulders.

"Have you lost whatever wits Becker left you, Kotarska?" Ludwig called sharply through the roar of rain on lake water, catching her bare upper arm firmly at the pier head and pulling her sideways through the side door of the vaulted cedar boathouse before slamming the oak latch against the squall.

Inside, pitch darkness smelled intensely of cedar shavings, spar varnish, linseed oil, and wet lake weeds lapping softly inside the open central water-slip where his sleek single racing scull rocked gently against rope fenders. Above the high transom window, blue-white lightning flashed every ten seconds across the Wannsee, turning rain-streaked glass into strobes of silver fire.

Sylwia shook his hand off her wet arm, tossed her dry satin slippers onto the carpenter's bench beside a stack of canvas boat-cushions, and faced him across four feet of shadowed planking while rainwater ran warm from her dark hair down her collarbones.

"Seven-forty tomorrow morning from Friedrichstraße," she said without preamble, chest rising quick and shallow in the thunder-charged air. "I came to collect half that medal with a hacksaw before I shake Berlin dust off my shoes forever."

Ludwig didn't laugh. Standing tall in rain-spotted black wool tails, water gleaming on his golden lashes and high cheekbones when lightning flared through the transom, he reached slowly inside his white waistcoat pocket, drew out the flat blue-velvet box, snapped it open, and tossed the heavy two-ounce silver Humboldt medallion straight into her outstretched palm.

"Take the whole thing," he said quietly.

Sylwia stared down at the cold silver disc in her wet hand—engraved *PRIMUS OMNIUM — COLLÈGE FRANÇAIS BERLIN 1930*—and felt rage ten times hotter than defeat blaze behind her eyes.

"I don't take charity from Schoelers!"

"It isn't charity, damn your stubborn Polish skull!" Ludwig snapped, taking one sharp step closer across the cedar boards. "Half the school knows Becker rigged the statute on my father's telephone call so a foreign girl wouldn't read the Latin address from Frederick's podium! Do you think I want a rigged piece of tin sitting on my desk when you beat me on Tacitus in front of Glass?"

"You beat me by four seconds on the surface integral!"

"With a dropped minus sign you handed back to me instead of letting me hang in front of Hagemann!" His voice roughened suddenly, losing every trace of cool Dahlem polish under the drumming roar of rain on cedar shingles overhead. "Why the devil did you tap that chalkboard line, Sylwia? Three years you've tried to cut my throat over quarter-marks, and with the whole Prussian board watching you saved my proof!"

"Because if I beat you," she cried fiercely, throat closing hard around something unbearable she refused to name, "I beat you at full strength, never on a slip of chalk! Keep your wretched medal—choke on it at Königsberg or Potsdam or wherever your father marches you!"

Spinning half around toward the open rectangular water-slip in the middle of the boathouse floor, Sylwia drew back her arm and hurled the heavy silver medallion straight out through the open lake-gate—*plop!*—into six feet of black, rain-lashed Wannsee water.

"Are you completely mad?!" Ludwig lunged forward, catching both her bare wet shoulders from behind and spinning her squarely back around to face him at the lip of the dark slip.

"Yes!" she gasped, shoving both palms hard against his rain-damp white evening shirtfront—except instead of pushing him away, her fingers curled spasmodically into fine linen lawn over the violent, hammering thunder of his heart. "Three years in this damned arrogant city, and after seven-forty tomorrow morning who am I supposed to fight with among polite Warsaw fools who smile when I talk?!"

Lightning split the clouds directly over Peacock Island with a simultaneous, deafening *CRACK* of thunder that shook the cedar rafters—illuminating Ludwig's face inches above hers, stripped raw of every mask he had worn since sixteen.

"Nobody," Ludwig breathed harshly—and crushed his mouth down onto hers.

It was not gentle; gentleness would have been a lie after a thousand days of war.

His mouth took hers hot, starved, bruisingly hard, tasting of dry Moselle, rainwater, and salt, and before half a heartbeat passed Sylwia made a low, broken sound deep in her throat, flung both bare arms up around his neck sending her mother's silver hair-clasp clattering forgotten onto the cedar planks, and kissed him back with teeth and tongue and three years of dammed-up wildfire.

"Sylwia—Christ in heaven, Sylwia—" he groaned into her open mouth, lifting her clean off her bare feet by her waist and backing blindly away from the black water-slip until the back of her thighs struck the sturdy waist-high carpenter's bench piled thick with canvas yacht cushions. Setting her down onto the cushions so her eyes came level with his, Ludwig buried one trembling hand deep in her heavy wet chestnut hair while his other slid down her bare spine inside the deep V of crimson silk, pressing her arched body hard against his chest.

Every nerve in Sylwia's nineteen-year-old body went up like dry pine soaked in ether. Never—not once in Berlin or Warsaw—had she let any boy put more than polite gloved fingers on her waist at a dance; she had despised their soft hands and vapid compliments because none of them were *him*. Now her own fingers tore frantically at his pearl collar stud until it popped bouncing across the dark floorboards, ripping his stiff white wing-collar open so she could press her open mouth and teeth straight against the scorching, salt-slick pulse hammering at the base of his throat.

Ludwig shuddered violently from shoulders to boots. Catching the thin crimson silk straps at her shoulders, he dragged both downward past her collarbones; bias-cut Parisian crepe slipped whispering to her waist in the blue flash of the next lightning bolt, baring high, pale, rose-tipped breasts that had never known cool night air or a man's eyes, nipples tightening pebble-hard instantly before his hot palm cupped her left breast and his mouth dropped down to take the right cleanly between lips and tongue.

Sylwia's head fell back against the stacked canvas cushions with a sharp, shameless cry muffled only by thunder overhead, bare heels locking instinctively behind his lean thighs in black evening wool to pull his hips tight into the open cradle of hers. Through thin silk crepe rucked halfway up her thighs and damp French lace beneath, the thick, straining iron ridge behind his trouser fly ground hard directly against the aching center between her legs—sending such a shocking, liquid jolt of pure carnal heat through her belly that her fingernails dug straight through his linen shirt into the muscles of his shoulders.

"Look at me," Ludwig rasped against her wet breastbone, lifting his flushed, wild face in the shadowy blue dark while his right hand bunched crimson silk ruthlessly up to her hips, calloused rower's fingers sliding up the bare warm satin of her inner thigh past silk garter ribbons, hooking aside the narrow damp gusset of her lace knickers, and touching bare, slick, burning flesh for the first time.

Sylwia gasped his name aloud—*"Ludwig—oh God, Ludwig—"*—hips bucking involuntarily into his palm as two long fingers parted dripping folds, circling once over the swollen, hypersensitive bud at the apex until white sparks burst behind her eyelids before sliding one finger, then two, deep inside the tight, untried heat of her body.

"God help me, you're shaking—" he whispered feverishly, forehead pressed hard against hers, breathing raggedly into her open mouth while his thumb stroked slow, devastating circles over slick nerves in rhythm with the deep curl of his fingers inside her, watching her grey eyes go blind and black-pupiled as the first violent, shattering wave of pleasure in her life clamped her inner muscles tight as a fist around his hand. Before the last shudder stopped quivering down her thighs, his free hand tore open the black horn buttons at his fly, freeing himself hot and heavy between their tangled bodies, broad tip nudging slick and urgent right at the threshold of her entrance while she gripped his bare neck with both hands, whispering fiercely against his bruised lips: *"Don't talk—now, Ludwig, before tomorrow comes, I don't care if it hurts—"*

"Don't get on that train tomorrow," he breathed against her throat, half out of his mind, kissing her jaw, her eyelids, her mouth while easing half an inch forward against tight resistance so she wouldn't tear—"Tell your father Paris, Zurich, Heidelberg, I don't care where, I'll walk out of Dahlem tonight without one pfennig of my father's money if I have to—I can't wake up in this damned city knowing you aren't—"

Up on the terraced path forty yards above the boathouse, gravel crunched heavily under four pairs of boots, and a harsh, drunken baritone sliced straight through the slackening rain:

*"Look on the pier railing, Rudolf—silver silk wrap! Told you Counselor Kotarski's stuck-up little Warsaw filly slipped down toward the water!"*

Both of them froze dead—hearts slamming like trapped birds against bare skin, Ludwig buried half an inch at the threshold of her body, sweat turning to ice water inside a single second.

Through the narrow ventilation gap between cedar wall-boards ten feet away, yellow storm-lantern beams swung wildly down the gravel steps toward the pier head. Sylwia caught one glimpse of brown twill breeches, polished black jackboots, and a red-white-black swastika armband plastered damply around a thick bicep—Horst Schoeler, Ludwig's twenty-four-year-old cousin from the Charlottenburg *Sturmabteilung*, flanked by two burly older men in civilian trench coats wearing police reserve pins, while thirty paces higher up the slope Generalmajor Heinrich Schoeler's tall dark silhouette stood watching under an umbrella.

"Keep your voice down, Horst, the old man's on the path," muttered one of the trench coats right outside the pier planks. "If she's necking with young Bredow or one of the French boys in the boathouse, haul them both down to Precinct Twelve until ten tomorrow morning for public indecency—old Kotarski misses the seven-forty diplomatic courier express looking for his brat, and Abteilung IA gets twelve clean hours to search his unsealed baggage at Friedrichstraße for those Pomeranian artillery transcripts!"

Inside the dark cedar boathouse, Sylwia couldn't understand *Pomeranian transcripts*, only *Precinct Twelve* and *search her father's baggage*—and felt every muscle across Ludwig's bare chest and shoulders turn slowly from living flesh into six inches of solid, arctic Krupp armor.

Looking down at her through the gloom—crimson silk bunched around her waist, bare breasts still glistening from his mouth, grey eyes wide and trusting—Ludwig closed his eyes once, so tightly a muscle spasmed beside the scar over his brow.

*Three armed men outside, my father thirty yards behind them with friends at Police Headquarters, and if they drag her into Alexanderplatz tonight they get Kotarski's papers and break both of them.*

Carefully, silently, Ludwig withdrew from her body, fastened his trousers in two swift jerks, and pulled the crimson silk straps gently back up over her bare shoulders—smoothing the bias-cut crepe down past her knees before picking up her dry satin slippers from the bench and pressing them into her numb hands.

"Put your shoes on," he whispered so low the lapping water almost drowned it. "Whatever I say out on that dock in the next sixty seconds—keep your chin up, walk straight to your father's car at the gate, and do not say one word back. Swear it."

Confused, still dizzy with fading heat and sudden cold dread, she slipped her heels into satin. "Ludwig, what—"

Outside, Horst Schoeler's fist hammered thrice against the oak boathouse door. *"Hallo inside! Anybody drowning in there, or shall we break the latch?!"*

Ludwig deliberately raked one hand rough through his damp golden hair, left his white evening collar unbuttoned and dangling loose with a faint smear of Sylwia's carmine lip-salve visible near his throat, unbolted the oak door, and strolled out onto the rain-slick pier beneath the swinging storm-lanterns wearing a lazy, bored, insufferably aristocratic sneer.

"Lower that lantern out of my eyes, Horst, before I kick it into the Wannsee," Ludwig drawled coolly, fastening one waistcoat button at his leisure while blocking the narrow doorway so neither police crony could peer inside at rumpled boat cushions. "Can't a man finish saying goodbye to his graduating class without half the Charlottenburg beer-hall brigade tramping down the pier?"

Horst grinned nastily as Sylwia stepped out into the drizzling lantern light behind Ludwig's shoulder, crimson gown damp at the hem, dark hair tumbled loose over her bare shoulders without its silver leaf-clasp. Both trench-coated men stiffened greedily forward—*"Fräulein Kotarska of the Polish Legation, isn't it? Rather irregular hour—"*

"Save your breath, gentlemen," Ludwig interrupted with a short, contemptuous laugh that froze every drop of blood in Sylwia's veins solid to her fingertips. Reaching into his waistcoat pocket, he flicked a bright silver ten-Reichsmark piece lazily through the wet air; it rang *tink-spin* onto the wet cedar planks two inches from Sylwia's right satin toe. "Nothing left here worth a precinct blotter. Bredow bet me ten marks in *Obersekunda* that Warsaw ice wouldn't thaw before graduation night. Three years was tedious work, I grant you—Polish mares always kick hardest before they take the bit—but a Schoeler never leaves an open ledger in Berlin."

Horst stared two seconds from the ten-mark coin at Sylwia's feet to the lipstick smudge on Ludwig's open throat, and burst into a coarse, roaring bark of laughter, slapping his trench-coated police friend across the shoulder. "Ten marks! Christ, Ludwig, the old general worried you had monk's blood in your veins, and here you're settling Versailles reparations on your back! Leave her be, Rudolf—damaged goods won't stop old Kotarski's train now anyway, she'll cry into her pillow all the way to Posen!"

Twenty feet up the terraced steps under the umbrella, Generalmajor Heinrich Schoeler looked down once, nodded with faint, wintry satisfaction, and turned back toward the lighted villa.

On the wet cedar pier, Sylwia Kotarska stood motionless in crimson silk.

She did not tremble. She did not cry out. She did not slap him—because slapping him would prove to these grinning jackals that Ludwig Schoeler still possessed the power to hurt living nerves inside her body, and within those ten seconds everything nineteen years old and vulnerable inside Sylwia Kotarska burned cleanly down to white, sterile bone ash.

Looking straight through Ludwig Schoeler's pale, expressionless face as though empty lake water stood behind his waistcoat, she stepped neatly past the gleaming ten-Reichsmark coin without touching it with her hem, picked up her dropped silver shawl from the wet railing, and walked up the long gravel drive alone through the rain to her father's waiting car.

She never looked back at Friedrichstraße Station at seven-forty the next morning.

Therefore she never knew that at two o'clock in the morning, after the last car left Große Seestraße, nineteen-year-old Ludwig Schoeler knelt vomiting champagne and blood-tinged bile into the black reeds behind the boathouse; stripped naked in thunder-chill rain to dive seven times into black silt until his raw, bleeding fingers closed around both the silver Humboldt medallion and her mother's fallen Mazovian silver leaf-clasp on the boathouse floor; and at half past two walked into the stable courtyard where Horst Schoeler was smoking a cigar over his motorcycle and beat his brown-shirted cousin through three oak stall doors with a doubled brass stirrup-leather until four grooms dragged him off.
