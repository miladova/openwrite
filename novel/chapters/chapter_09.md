# CHAPTER NINE

## Aleja Szucha 25

**Warsaw — November 9, 1943**

Before nineteen-thirty-nine, Number Twenty-Five Aleja Szucha had housed the Polish Ministry of Religious Denominations and Public Enlightenment—a sleek, pale-limestone monument of nineteen-thirties modernist geometry with wide travertine staircases, brass handrails, and tall, airy windows designed to let light fall across school curricula.

The *Sicherheitspolizei* and SD had found the architecture admirably suited to their own enlightenment.

Sitting upright in the rear leather seat of a black Mercedes staff car as iron gates clanged shut behind the rear bumper at nine-forty on Tuesday morning, Sylwia looked once up the sheer limestone cliff of the courtyard and reached unobtrusively with two chilled fingers to touch the collar seam below her left jaw.

The paraffined glass cherry-stone was there. Eight seconds from tooth-crack to loss of consciousness; forty seconds to irreversible cytochrome oxidase blockade.

"Right this way, Frau Doktor," murmured *Kriminalsekretär* Krauss politely, holding the car door against thin November snow-flurries that melted instantly on the swept concrete. "Sturmbannführer Reinhardt dislikes keeping busy physicians away from their wards."

Twenty minutes earlier, Krauss and two heavyset subordinates had stepped into Infant Jesus Hospital just as Sylwia finished morning rounds—precisely fifteen minutes after Hauptmann Brandt's courier noted that Oberstleutnant Schoeler had been summoned across the Vistula to Praga rail headquarters for an urgent logistics inspection. No arrest warrant had been presented; Krauss had respected OKH *Sonderausweis* Number One-One-Four to the fastidious letter: *Merely twenty minutes' voluntary witness clarification regarding October pharmacy discrepancies, Frau Doktor; naturally, if you prefer we request formal military detention papers through Berlin...*

Refusing would have confessed terror on the spot. Leaving Halina a single coded sentence at the scrub sink—*"Tell Pan Doktor Majewski the Ujazdów linen delivery is forty minutes early"*—which sent Halina racing out the back coal gate to warn both Marek's lookout and Brandt's telephone exchange on Aleja Róż, Sylwia had put on her charcoal coat without changing out of her white hospital smock beneath and stepped calmly into the black car.

Inside the great marble entrance hall, pristine order reigned. Blonde Reich-German stenographers in neat sweater-sets clicked briskly past on low heels carrying manila folders and trays of fresh Viennese apricot pastries; typewriters rattled steadily behind oak doors; the air smelled of floor wax, roasted coffee—real coffee, an intoxicating, obscene ghost from dead peacetime—and damp wool.

Only as Krauss escorted her past the iron-grilled basement stairwell toward the lift did the other smell drift faintly upward on warm radiator drafts: stale sweat, unwashed terror, raw Lysol poured over concrete, and copper blood. Down in that brightly lit subterranean corridor—every child in Warsaw knew its name in nightmares: *tramwaj*, the "tramway"—two rows of bruised, silent Polish men and women sat upright on backless wooden benches bolted one behind another like streetcar seats, forbidden to turn their heads half an inch left or right while gramophones behind steel doors played cheerful Bavarian brass marches at full volume over the thud of rubber truncheons.

Sylwia did not glance down the stairs. She stepped into the mahogany-paneled paternoster lift, watched three floors glide smoothly past, and walked behind Krauss into Room Three-Fourteen.

Sunlight—thin, wintry, silver—fell across a spacious corner office furnished with a looted Persian Tabriz carpet, an upright black Bechstein piano against the side wall, and a broad walnut desk spotless save for a green leather blotter, a silver tray bearing a porcelain pot of steaming coffee, and a closed buff folder.

Seated at the Bechstein in crisp mouse-grey SD wool, Otto Reinhardt finished the quiet, crystalline closing measure of the *Aria* from Bach's *Goldberg Variations*, let the final G-major chord decay cleanly into silence, and rose with a pleasant smile.

"Forgive a bachelor's morning indulgence, Frau Doktor," Reinhardt said, gesturing courteously toward a leather armchair facing the tall windows—angled, as every detective since Vidocq angled witness chairs, so winter light struck the visitor's irises squarely while leaving the host in comfortable silhouette. "Busoni spoils Bach with pedal, don't you find? Pure counterpoint requires clean fingers—much like vascular surgery. Coffee? Fresh Santos bean from Lisbon via Geneva, and real cream."

"Thank you, no, Herr Sturmbannführer," Sylwia said evenly, sitting upright without leaning against the leather back, gloved hands folded loosely in her lap. "Rationed chicory ruins the stomach for real caffeine; I should have palpitations before noon."

"Admirable self-discipline." Pouring himself half a cup without sugar, Reinhardt seated himself behind the walnut desk, wiped his rimless gold spectacles once with yellow chamois, hooked them neatly behind small pale ears, and opened the buff folder.

Out slid two glossy photostatic sheets bearing the circular eagle stamp of *Polizeipräsidium Berlin — Abteilung Einwohnermeldeamt*, followed by an eight-by-ten photographic plate copied from a school annual.

Reinhardt turned both pages one hundred eighty degrees so Sylwia could read them right-side up.

There, staring up across thirteen years and two ruined capitals from the sunlit steps of Reichstagsufer Six in June nineteen-thirty, stood sixty eighteen- and nineteen-year-olds in stiff white collars and summer dresses. Front row center sat Oberstudiendirektor Becker and sardonic Doktor Glass; two places to Glass's right stood tall, arrogant young **Ludwig Schoeler**; two places to Glass's left, chin tilted high and storm-grey eyes daring the camera lens to blink first, stood **Sylwia Kotarska**. Below the photograph ran the certified Prussian *Reifeprüfung* protocol:

*1. Schoeler, Ludwig Heinrich — 1,00*
*2. Kotarska, Sylwia Maria — 1,00*

Silence ticked gently in the warm room. A gilt mantel clock chimed the quarter-hour.

"When we spoke in Pavilion Three on the morning of October twentieth," Reinhardt remarked mildly, sipping his coffee, "you mentioned four schoolgirl years in Berlin while your late father argued tariff law on Kurfürstenstraße. Memory plays odd tricks under fatigue, of course—yet it does strike a tidy mind as remarkable that you omitted spending three consecutive years in the identical thirty-student classroom with the man bleeding on your operating table."

Sylwia looked down at the nineteen-year-old girl in the photograph for three calm seconds, and then raised eyes colder than Baltic slate straight into Reinhardt's spectacles.

She did not stammer; she did not feign surprise; she let an ugly, razor-edged curve—pure, undiluted June nineteen-thirty Wannsee venom, preserved intact in cold storage inside her ribs for thirteen winters and requiring zero acting whatsoever—touch one corner of her mouth.

"Had you walked into Pavilion Three at seven in the morning and asked me whether I recognized the bastard I had just spent ninety minutes keeping alive at gunpoint, Herr Sturmbannführer, what answer did you expect?" Sylwia asked dryly. "If I announced to Hauptmann Brandt and yourself that Ludwig Schoeler and I despised the sight of each other across a school bench in nineteen-thirty, and that he died—suppose a stitch gave way Thursday—whose neck would your firing squad have stretched first?"

Reinhardt tilted his head appreciatively, intrigued rather than displeased by the absence of female flutter. "Despised, Frau Doktor? Young people tied at one-point-zero-zero across three years rarely remain indifferent."

"Indifferent?" Sylwia let out a short, bitter laugh so wholly genuine the hairs could have stood up on Ludwig's neck had he been in the room. "Ask SS-Obersturmbannführer Horst Schoeler on Prinz-Albrecht-Straße in Berlin how 'fond' the Schoeler family and Counselor Kotarski's daughter were of one another! Call Berlin on your teleprinter right now—Horst Schoeler will remember June twenty-first, nineteen-thirty on the Wannsee pier with great fondness!"

Behind his rimless lenses, Reinhardt's pale eyes sharpened half a millimeter. Offering an active senior SS officer on Prinz-Albrecht-Straße as a character witness was the last move on earth an underground conspirator hiding collusion ever dared make.

"Enlighten me," Reinhardt invited softly.

"Gladly," Sylwia said, voice ringing flat and hard as iron filings. "For three years Ludwig Schoeler could not stomach a Polish girl matching his marks in front of Prussian masters. When we tied down to the hundredth decimal on the *Abitur*, his father—Generalmajor Heinrich Schoeler—pulled strings at the Ministry so a Slav wouldn't carry the Humboldt Medal out of Berlin. And on graduation night at Wannsee, in front of his cousin Horst and two Berlin policemen, Ludwig Schoeler threw a ten-Reichsmark silver piece at my shoes to settle a dormitory wager that he could humiliate Counselor Kotarski's daughter before our diplomatic train left at dawn. Nine years later, men in uniform broke my father's hands in this very building and shipped his ashes home in a cardboard box."

She leaned two inches forward toward the green blotter, storm-grey eyes blazing cold into Reinhardt's face.

"So no, Herr Sturmbannführer—I did not gossip over bedpans about old school days. When Hauptmann Brandt shoved a Luger under old Professor Majewski's chin and your office threatened to murder a hundred Poles plus my nurses if Schoeler died, I sewed his artery shut because I am a surgeon and I do not murder patients—not even a Schoeler. And when he woke up choking on blood and forced to owe every breath in his proud Prussian lungs to my hands, I didn't mind keeping him under my roof one bit."

For ten long seconds Otto Reinhardt studied every muscle around her mouth and eyes, tapping one clean fingernail silently against the porcelain saucer.

It hung together. Psychologically, historically, down to verifiable names in RSHA Berlin, every joint of her story locked seamlessly—*almost* too seamlessly. And if Oberstleutnant Schoeler had afterward slapped an OKH requisition pass on her out of perverse aristocratic dominance—forcing the proud Polish enemy who saved him to trot into Number Twelve Aleja Róż every evening at six o'clock like an obedient housemaid changing bandages—why, Reinhardt knew half a dozen Prussian staff colonels vain enough to relish precisely that little cruelty.

*Unless,* whispered the patient Munich detective behind Reinhardt's freckled forehead, *the lady protests one shade too brilliantly.*

"Most illuminating, Frau Doktor," Reinhardt sighed, closing the buff folder neatly. "Prussian military families can be insufferably provincial, as a Bavarian like myself knows all too well. In fact, we need detain you no longer—save for one tiny medical courtesy on your way past the ground floor."

Every instinct along Sylwia's spine screamed warning, though her face remained carved alabaster as Reinhardt rose and picked up his cap.

"Our night patrol near the Bródno railway marshaling yards picked up an armed Polish courier at three this morning," Reinhardt chatted pleasantly as they rode the lift downward—past the sunny ground-floor exit, straight down one level lower into the concrete windowless basement. "Stubborn boy. Dislocated shoulder and two cracked ribs resisting arrest, and my overzealous assistants lack clinical subtlety. Tell me whether his heart will tolerate another hour's questioning before noon—our own police physician is testifying in Cracow today—and Krauss will drive you straight back to Lindleya."

Down here the Bavarian brass march blaring from a Bakelite loudspeaker bolted near the ceiling—*Alte Kameraden*, needle scratching rhythmically in worn shellac grooves—could not drown a hoarse, repetitive animal whimper coming from Cell Six, nor the choking chemical stench of Lysol, scorched hair, and voided bowels. Along the center of the whitewashed corridor sat fourteen motionless men and women on the wooden "tramway" benches staring straight ahead at white brick. None breathed loud enough to hear.

Krauss unlocked Iron Door Four at the end of the passage.

Inside a ten-by-twelve concrete wash-cell lit by an unshaded two-hundred-watt bulb dangling on twisted black cord, two shirt-sleeved SD interrogators with rolled cuffs stood smoking cigarettes beside a zinc bucket of pink water and a table laid with steel carpenter's pincers, braided bull-pizzles, and a hand-cranked field magneto.

Strapped half-upright across an inclined wet wooden slaughtering trestle by leather cuffs at both ankles and his left wrist—his right wrist hung temporarily unbuckled in a pool of clotting dark blood where four fingernails had been wrenched raggedly out of the nail-beds down to bare white phalanx bone—lay a slight nineteen-year-old boy stripped to tattered blood-soaked trousers.

His snub Powiśle nose was broken sideways across swollen purple cheeks; burns from magneto clips charred black circles around both nipples; his left eye was hammered shut into a livid plum slit.

*Tomek.*

*Wróbel.*

The freckled nineteen-year-old printer's apprentice who had whistled behind her through Kercelak mud, leaped off brick rubble onto a German gendarme's shoulders in Sienna Alley to save her life, and carried twenty messages between her cellar and Marek's hands.

Reinhardt did not look at the boy on the trestle at all. Standing two paces to Sylwia's right rear, Otto Reinhardt watched *exclusively* the side of Sylwia Kotarska's throat, her eyelids, and the radial edge of her bare hands as she stepped under the glaring two-hundred-watt bulb.

Inside Sylwia's chest, something tore silently from breastbone to spine—yet thirty thousand hours of holding retractors while children died on zinc tables kept her eyelids, nostrils, and fingertips frozen in absolute, terrifying stillness. She stripped off her leather street gloves, dropped them neatly into her coat pocket without haste, and stepped directly beside the bloody wooden trestle.

"Uncover his chest completely and turn off that gramophone outside if you want me to auscultate heart sounds without a stethoscope," she said briskly in clipped German.

At the sound of her German voice, Tomek's battered head stirred half an inch on the wet wood.

Slowly, painfully, his single unswollen right eye—clear hazel-brown under matted, blood-clotted sandy hair—slitted open against the blinding overhead bulb and focused twelve inches upward onto Sylwia's pale, expressionless face above her open charcoal coat and white hospital smock.

For one microscopic tenth of a second—invisible to Reinhardt standing behind her right shoulder because Sylwia kept her head angled cleanly between them—she saw shock flash across that single hazel eye, followed instantly by fierce, soaring comprehension: *They haven't got her yet. They're testing her on me.*

Sylwia laid her cool, bare right palm flat over his burned left precordium—letting him feel, through steady skin contact alone, all the love and grief and gratitude she dared never speak aloud on earth—while sliding her left fingers lightly along his purpled fifth and sixth ribs. Subcutaneous air crackled beneath her fingertips: broken rib puncturing lung.

"Left pneumothorax and flail fifth rib," she reported clinically over her shoulder. "Blood pressure is—"

Before she finished the word, Tomek dragged one rasping, blood-bubbling breath into his punctured lung, twisted his swollen purple mouth into a ferocious Powiśle gutter sneer, and spat a thick gobbet of crimson blood and broken tooth enamel squarely onto the white starched lapel of Sylwia's hospital smock.

*"Weź te szwabskie łapy, kurwo!"* Tomek croaked in loud, raw Warsaw street Polish that both bilingual SD thugs by the wall understood effortlessly. *"Take your Kraut-whore paws off me, bitch! Go suck off your German colonels upstairs—Poland shaves sluts like you bald before the rope!"*

One of the shirt-sleeved interrogators lunged forward roaring *"Verfluchter Hund!"* with a raised bull-pizzle to smash the boy's teeth in.

And Tomek—whose bloody, fingerless right hand was free and whose left wrist-strap the guards had loosened two notches ten minutes earlier when reviving him with a bucket of icy water—used that guard's step blocking Reinhardt's line of sight to snatch his left wrist clean through the wet leather buckle.

He didn't grab for a throat or a weapon. Nineteen years old, carrying nineteen underground street addresses and the true name of *Kora* behind bruised bone while magneto wires waited on the table for afternoon session, Tomek threw every remaining ounce of Powiśle muscle sideways off the high wooden trestle—diving headfirst, hands tucked voluntarily behind his own ribs so reflex wouldn't break his fall, driving the right temple of his skull and hyperextended neck with sickening, full-body velocity straight down onto the heavy, square cast-iron wheel-flange of the main steam-radiator pipe bolted six inches above the concrete floor.

The sound—a wet, crunching *THOCK-snap* like an axe splitting green oak—stopped every breath in Cell Four.

Tomek's thin legs jerked violently once, heels drumming twice against the zinc water bucket; bright pink cerebrospinal-and-lung froth bubbled softly from his nostrils onto dusty concrete; then every muscle went profoundly, mercifully slack.

"Scheiße—!" cursed Krauss, shoving past the guard as Sylwia knelt smoothly in the spreading dark pool beside the radiator pipe without hitching her wool skirt out of the blood, thumb lifting Tomek's limp eyelid—pupil blown wide, fixed, black, free forever of pincers and wires and Aleja Szucha—while two fingers confirmed zero carotid pulse beneath his broken jaw.

*Sleep easy, little Sparrow,* whispered her silent soul behind iron ribs while hot blood soaked slowly through the knees of her stockings on cold concrete. *God keep you where no dogs bark.*

Rising without haste, wiping Tomek's blood off her fingertips with a square of gauze from her pocket rather than trembling for a sink, Sylwia turned calmly toward Otto Reinhardt.

Reinhardt's pale freckled face showed neither outrage nor pity—only mild, fastidious irritation at untidy subordinates. Yet as his eyes searched Sylwia's face beneath the pitiless bare bulb, looking at Tomek's bloody spittle drying red across her white lapel and her rock-steady grey eyes, even Munich's sharpest hound found nothing whatsoever to sink teeth into.

"Depressed right temporal fracture with atlanto-occipital dislocation," Sylwia stated coldly. "Instantaneous brainstem arrest. If your men leave greased slaughtering benches unstrapped beside iron steam valves, Herr Sturmbannführer, you scarcely need physicians."

Before Reinhardt could reply, rapid boots clattered down the iron cellar stairs and a pale young SD *Unterscharführer* leaned breathlessly into the doorway, saluting sharply:

"Herr Sturmbannführer—forgive the interruption! Duty desk upstairs reports an armed detachment of Wehrmacht *Feldgendarmerie* has just entered the front courtyard from Praga, and Oberstleutnant Schoeler is in the main vestibule demanding—"

"Demanding nothing, Reinhardt," cut a cold, carrying baritone directly behind the young corporal's shoulder as tall boots rang firmly down the final three concrete steps into the basement corridor itself. "Informing."

Every head in the cellar passage—even two bruised shadows on the end of the "tramway" bench before guards barked their chins straight—stiffened at the apparition standing beneath the low whitewashed vault.

Dressed in freshly pressed field-grey General Staff uniform with broad crimson *Karmesinrot* collar tabs and trouser stripes blazing under the bare bulbs, peaked cap set razor-level above his pale, hawk-boned face, left arm out of its visible sling and hidden only by resting two gloved fingers casually inside the second button of his tunic just like Scharnhorst in an oil painting, stood Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler—flanked one pace behind by scarred Hauptmann Brandt and two six-foot Wehrmacht military police sergeants wearing silver gorgets over slung submachine guns.

How he had crossed two Vistula bridges from Praga inside twenty-eight minutes and walked up steps with an ununited collarbone without blacking out, Sylwia could only guess by the dead-white line bracketing his mouth and the tiny beads of cold sweat gleaming beneath his cap visor. His slate-blue eyes swept once—for less than a fifth of a second—into Cell Four: taking in the dead boy crumpled beside the radiator pipe, the bloody spittle drying on Sylwia's white hospital lapel, and the crimson circles soaking both knees of her dark stockings.

Nothing flickered in his face—yet the temperature in the cellar dropped twenty degrees.

"Oberstleutnant Schoeler," Reinhardt said smoothly, stepping out into the corridor and pulling Cell Four's iron door half-shut behind him while offering a courteous two-inch bow. "Remarkable recovery. We understood Praga rail inspection occupied your morning."

"Praga occupies ten minutes when Berlin teleprinters clatter," Ludwig replied icily, drawing a folded pink OKH top-priority *Blitzfernschreiben* flimsy from his leather gauntlet cuff and holding it six inches from Reinhardt's spectacles without surrendering possession of the paper. "Signed forty minutes ago at Mauerwald by Generalmajor Gehlen and countersigned by Chief of Army Feldpolizei: all medical and civilian personnel covered under *Sonderausweis* Series One-Fourteen remain under exclusive OKH counter-espionage cognizance pending completion of the Małkinia convoy inquiry. Any interference—including 'voluntary witness invitations'—requires personal countersignature from Reichsführer-SS Himmler's field liaison at Hochwald."

Reinhardt read the teleprinter stamp silently through his gold-wired lenses. A muscle the size of a grain of rice flexed once behind his neat jaw. Pulling Gehlen's name directly out of *Fremde Heere Ost* meant Schoeler still carried heavier steel in East Prussia than Reinhardt had calculated.

"No interference was dreamed of, Herr Oberstleutnant," Reinhardt purred silkily. "Frau Doktor Kotarska generously cleared up a minor pharmacy question and pronounced a clumsy suicide down here—saving my clerk forty lines of paperwork. She is entirely at your disposal."

"Good," Ludwig clipped. His eyes touched Sylwia with supreme, glacial ownership that made Krauss smirk knowingly behind Reinhardt's back. "Fetch your coat hem out of that filth, Frau Doktor. My pleural dressing is two hours overdue and I have zero intention of letting Ujazdów quacks ruin my afternoon. Good day, Sturmbannführer."

Nobody spoke a single syllable as they climbed out of the cellar stench into thin, whirling November snow, nor inside the closed Mercedes rolling south down Aleje Ujazdowskie with Brandt sitting rigid beside the driver beyond the glass partition.

Sylwia sat bolt upright staring blindly out at snow dusting the leafless lime trees, hands clenched inside her leather gloves until scars threatened to split, tasting Lysol and seeing nineteen-year-old hazel eyes smiling before a skull hit cast iron. Beside her on the grey broadcloth seat, Ludwig sat equally motionless, left shoulder braced stiffly against every cobblestone jolt, breathing shallow and silent through his nose.

Only when the iron garden gates of Number Twelve Aleja Róż clanged shut behind them, and Brandt dismissed both driver and sentries to the gatehouse before vanishing discreetly into the ground-floor cipher room, did Ludwig walk ahead of her up the wide curving oak stairs of the empty Danish villa, kick shut the heavy double doors of his second-floor bedroom-study, turn the brass key in the lock, and lean one hand heavily onto the mahogany table as his knees nearly buckled.
