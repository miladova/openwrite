# CHAPTER FOUR

## The Ice on Halensee

**Berlin — January 1929**

The winter of nineteen-twenty-nine came down out of Russia like an iron hammer and froze Berlin solid to its cellar pipes.

By the middle of January, thermometers along Unter den Linden hung at twenty-two degrees below zero Celsius at noon. The Spree locked fast from Moabit to Köpenick under eighteen inches of black-green ice; municipal work gangs burned coke braziers at tram switches so the steel tongues wouldn't snap like glass; dray horses dropped dead in harness along Leipziger Straße with white rime crusting their nostrils, and starving women from Wedding hacked steaks off the frozen carcasses with kitchen cleavers before the police arrived. Yet three blocks away behind double velvet curtains at the Romanisches Café and the Kakadu Bar, saxophones wailed *Mackie Messer*, champagne corks popped beneath crystal lustres, and girls in knee-length silver lamé danced the Charleston until four in the morning as though the floor beneath Weimar Germany were six feet of granite instead of tissue paper stretched over a volcano.

At seventeen, Sylwia Kotarska and Ludwig Schoeler had refined mutual torment from a blood sport into an exact science.

Everyone at the *Französisches Gymnasium* kept score. In the masters' smoking room, Herr Doktor Glass maintained a private chalkboard behind the cupboard door tracking cumulative decimals between *der Preuße* and *die Polin*: after five consecutive terms, out of twelve hundred possible marks across Greek, Latin, French, mathematics, history, and natural sciences, Schoeler stood at eleven hundred eighty-four point five and Kotarska at eleven hundred eighty-four point zero.

Neither of them slept properly before examination weeks, and neither scrupled at total war.

In the first week of January, when Oberstudiendirektor Becker announced the prestigious Humboldt Medal Essay for *Unterprima* on the physiological foundations of modern cellular pathology—a prize practically tailor-made for Sylwia, who already spent Saturday afternoons perched in the visitors' gallery of Sauerbruch's surgical amphitheater at the Charité watching chests opened—Sylwia walked into the Prussian State Library on Unter den Linden on Monday afternoon to request Rudolf Virchow's *Die Cellularpathologie* and all four volumes of Cohnheim's *Lectures on General Pathology*.

"Checked out at nine this morning, Fräulein," said the elderly clerk behind the brass grille, peering over his pince-nez at the call slips.

Sylwia's grey eyes narrowed. "All five volumes? What about the Friedrich-Wilhelms-Universität medical reading room?"

"Borrowed on inter-institutional Generalstab privilege card through the end of January, Fräulein."

Turning slowly from the counter, Sylwia looked straight up at the wrought-iron second-tier gallery above the great domed reading room. Leaning negligently on the brass rail forty feet overhead, chin resting on one hand, stood Ludwig Schoeler—who had zero intention of becoming a physician, cared nothing for cellular pathology, and lifted two fingers in a silent, mocking salute while holding Virchow's maroon-bound first edition tucked prominently beneath his elbow.

Any ordinary girl in Berlin would have wept or complained to Doktor Glass.

Sylwia merely smiled up at him—a slow, curved, utterly terrifying Polish smile that actually made Ludwig straighten half an inch from the railing in instant tactical distrust—walked six blocks north across the frozen Spree to the Charité Anatomical-Pathological Institute on Luisenstraße, and presented herself in faultless French and German to Privy Councilor Lubarsch's crusty seventy-year-old preparator, Herr Wenzel. Within twenty minutes, having dazzled the lonely old technician by identifying tuberculous caseation versus syphilitic gumma at ten paces and listening reverently to his memories of Virchow's cigars, Sylwia was granted a stool inside the locked glass-jar museum itself—sketching original wet histological preparations from eighteen-fifty-eight that didn't appear in *any* printed textbook in Berlin.

When the Humboldt Medal rankings went up on the oak board on January fifteenth, Kotarska stood first with twenty out of twenty and a special commendation from the Charité referee; Schoeler stood second with nineteen.

Passing him on the crowded stone stairs between bells, Sylwia murmured sweetly without turning her head: "Do return Virchow before Friday, Herr Schoeler. Overdue fines are ten pfennigs a day, and book-hoarding is so bourgeois."

"Wait until Friday's diplomatic reception at Pariser Platz, Kotarska," Ludwig murmured back at her shoulder, cold slate eyes glinting down at her from two steps higher. "I understand Monsieur l'Ambassadeur has invited your father and mine into the same salon. Do try not to trip on your hem."

The warning was not idle. On the evening of January eighteenth, inside the blazing white-and-gold ballroom of the French Embassy overlooking the snow-shrouded Brandenburg Gate, French diplomacy attempted one of its periodic, hopelessly optimistic experiments in Locarno reconciliation: mixing Reichswehr brass, Wilhelmstraße secretaries, and Eastern European legation counselors over champagne and Debussy string quartets.

Wearing her first grown-up evening gown—deep slate-blue silk crepe bought in Paris by her Aunt Hélène, cut straight and simple, leaving her long arms bare and turning her eyes the color of storm-lit pewter—Sylwia stood beside her father near the tall malachite urns and felt Ludwig's gaze strike her bare shoulder blades across sixty feet of waxed parquet before she even turned around.

He wore black evening tails for the first time she had ever seen—cut with severe Prussian elegance across shoulders that had broadened noticeably since autumn through sculling and winter gymnasium work, white piqué tie immaculate at his throat. Beside him stood two figures: a slight, bright-eyed fourteen-year-old boy with Ludwig's tarnished-gold hair and none of his winter frost—his younger brother Konrad, grinning eagerly at the trays of *petits fours*—and, towering half a head above most of the room, Generalmajor a.D. Heinrich Schoeler.

Sylwia needed only three seconds looking at the father to understand the anvil on which the son had been hammered.

At fifty-eight, retired Generalmajor Schoeler wore civilian evening dress as though it were field-grey armor, the blue-enameled Maltese cross of the *Pour le Mérite*—the Blue Max won under Hindenburg in Masuria—hanging cleanly below his stiff white collar. His cropped hair was iron-white, his face seamed flat and spare, his eyes the exact pale Baltic slate of Ludwig's minus every trace of youth or irony.

Inevitably, maliciously steered by a mischievous Quai d'Orsay first secretary, the two groups converged beside the champagne buffet.

"Herr Generalmajor Schoeler—may I present Counselor Kazimierz Kotarski of the Polish Legation, and Mademoiselle Kotarska, who shares honors with your elder son at the Collège Français?" purred the Frenchman, and promptly vanished to watch the fireworks.

Heinrich Schoeler executed a bow mathematically calibrated to three degrees—precise enough to satisfy protocol, stiff enough to remind everyone within ten feet that West Prussia and Posen remained stolen soil. "Herr Rat. My son mentions the Collège seldom, though I understand foreign legation children find its French curriculum convenient while temporarily stationed in Berlin."

Kazimierz Kotarski inclined his silver-threaded head with velvet Warsaw grace. "Most convenient, Herr Generalmajor. French remains so durable—unlike empires, which require redrawing every generation or two."

Young Konrad choked slightly on an almond macaroon; Ludwig's face remained an unreadable mask, though his eyes met Sylwia's across the white damask tablecloth with a silent, sharp warning: *Leave it alone, Kotarska. This isn't the schoolyard.*

Had Generalmajor Schoeler stopped there, she would have left it alone for her father's sake. Instead, the old general's wintry gaze slid over Sylwia's Parisian slate-blue silk and down to her hands.

"And Mademoiselle studies cellular pathology, I am told by Oberstudiendirektor Becker," Heinrich Schoeler remarked dryly. "Ambitious for a young lady. In Prussia we still prefer women who mend linen rather than dissect frogs—though doubtless Warsaw requires unusual improvisations to staff its new institutions."

Kazimierz's smile hardened to steel, about to cut—when Sylwia opened her silk evening reticule with tranquil poise.

"Oh, I read history as well as anatomy, Herr Generalmajor," she said in crystalline Berlin German that turned four Reichswehr officers' heads nearby. "In fact, your son was generous enough to lend our study bench his personal annotated volume of Clausewitz's *Vom Kriege* last Tuesday—I brought it tonight lest he miss it over the weekend."

Across the buffet, Ludwig went utterly, dangerously still. *His Clausewitz had vanished from his coat pocket after Wednesday's debate.*

Smiling like an angel painted on church glass, Sylwia held out the worn half-calf volume open to Book Eight, where Ludwig's unmistakable, razor-neat black fountain-pen script filled the margin beside Ludendorff's 1918 commentary—only two weeks earlier Ludwig had argued in front of three classmates that Ludendorff had thrown away the German Army in March nineteen-eighteen through political megalomania, scrawling in the margin: *Operational brilliance in tactical vacuum = suicide by vanity. Had Bismarck lived, he would have had Ludendorff shot at Spa before April.*

Generalmajor Heinrich Schoeler—who had served three years as deputy quartermaster on Ludendorff's own operational staff—took the open volume automatically, glanced down at his son's handwriting beneath the chandelier glare, and turned slowly the color of chipped granite beneath his winter tan.

Around them, Major von Bredow's father cleared his throat violently and discovered urgent business near the cello section.

"Most illuminating," Heinrich Schoeler said softly, snapping the leather volume shut with a sound like a pistol breach locking. "Thank you for returning my property, Fräulein. Counselor Kotarski. Come, Konrad. Ludwig."

Twenty minutes later, slipping down the quiet marble corridor behind the conservatory in search of the ladies' cloakroom, Sylwia paused abruptly behind a fluted Corinthian pilaster at the sound of voices inside the half-open smoking anteroom.

No shouting. Generalmajor Schoeler did not shout.

Only one sharp, flat *crack*—a heavy signet-ringed backhand striking flesh hard enough to knock a tall seventeen-year-old boy half a step sideways into a gilded console table—followed by dead silence.

Through the two-inch crack beside the brass hinges, Sylwia froze, breath caught in her throat.

Ludwig stood straight again instantly, heels together, chin level, hands steady down the seams of his black dress trousers while a thin bright thread of blood welled at the left corner of his mouth. He did not touch it.

"Twice in one week," Heinrich Schoeler said in a low, arctic monotone three inches from his son's face. "First Becker informs me a Polish diplomat's brat takes the Humboldt Medal out from under your nose. Tonight you hand Ludendorff's name to Slavs and Frenchmen on a silver tray to show off clever schoolboy ink. Your brother Konrad has his mother's soft heart; nobody expects steel from him. You carry my name into the Generalstab. If you lack the discipline to crush a girl in classrooms, what good will you be when we wipe that bastard state off the Vistula?"

"It will not happen again, Father," Ludwig said evenly.

"See that it doesn't. Wipe your mouth before your mother sees you."

Heavy footsteps strode out through the far archway toward the ballroom. Left alone under the dim wall sconce, Ludwig stood motionless for five seconds before lifting a white linen handkerchief to the corner of his split lip—staring straight into the tall gilt mirror above the console, looking neither cowed nor sorrowful, only colder by ten degrees than any seventeen-year-old boy on earth had any business looking.

Behind the marble pilaster in the shadows, Sylwia pressed her spine against cold stone, her stomach twisting into an unfamiliar, sickening knot. She had meant to sting his insufferable pride—not hand his father a whip, and certainly not hear an old Prussian general speak quietly in candlelit Berlin of wiping Poland off the Vistula as casually as draining a ditch.

She slipped back to her father without using the cloakroom, and did not look toward the Schoelers once before leaving.

***

Twenty-four hours later, at half past six on the evening of Saturday, January nineteenth, freezing river fog rolled thick and sulfurous off the frozen Spree across the Marschallbrücke.

Sylwia had stayed late in the empty second-floor biology room finishing histological ink plates before walking down toward the Friedrichstraße tram stop, her steel-bladed figure skates slung by tied white laces over her left shoulder—she had planned to meet Ruth Oppenheim at the Westend ice rink before Ruth came down with bronchitis—and her heavy satchel tucked under her arm. Streetlamps glowed like blurred yellow moons wrapped in dirty wool; the cold bit straight through wool stockings and lined kid gloves at minus nineteen, stinging nostrils shut with every breath.

As she turned down the quiet snow-packed embankment steps beside the dark brick bridge arch to take the shortcut toward Dorotheenstraße, four figures in heavy loden overcoats and peaked student caps stepped out of the lee of the bridge pier, blocking the narrow shoveled path between waist-high snowbanks and the iron river railing.

Even in yellow coal-fog she recognized the tall, fleshy nineteen-year-old blocking the center with a glowing cigarette clenched in his teeth: Otto von Radowitz, senior speaker of the nationalist *Königstädtisches Realgymnasium* union, whom Sylwia had carved into small, hilarious pieces during last month's municipal rhetoric contest when Radowitz forgot the date of the Peace of Thorn and she supplied it in three languages while forty girls giggled.

"Well, boys, look what skated down off the Vistula," Radowitz drawled, whiskey-thick vapor steaming off his scarf as his three companions closed half a semicircle behind his shoulders, wearing the black-white-red lapel pins of the *Stahlhelm* youth league. "All alone without Herr Doktor Glass to hide behind, Fräulein Kotarska?"

Sylwia stopped four paces back. Her heart sped, clean and cold, measuring distances: snowbank chest-high on her left, steep icy cobbles behind, not a tram bell or policeman's shako visible in the fog for two hundred yards. Keep the chin up; wolves bite runners first.

"Step aside, Herr von Radowitz," she said crisply. "You're blocking the footpath, and cheap grain schnapps smells worse in frost."

Radowitz's laugh died ugly. Two steps closed the gap; his gloved fist knocked her leather satchel spinning out of her arm onto the packed snow, scattering pencils and Latin notebooks across the ice. "Big mouth on every Pole in Berlin lately—stealing Silesian mines, strutting into Pariser Platz salons, taking prizes built with German endowments. High time somebody taught Counselor Kotarski's stuck-up little *polnische Hure* to drop her eyes on German pavement."

Sylwia swung her slung steel skates off her left shoulder like a flail—catching the nearest boy a glancing crack across the forearm that made him curse—before Radowitz lunged inside her swing, clamping one beefy hand over her mouth and slamming her shoulders back against the frosted iron railing above the frozen river while a second youth grabbed her hair-braids, ripping three tortoiseshell pins loose and tearing the black astrakhan collar half off her winter coat.

She did not scream; she bit straight down through Radowitz's wool glove until Radowitz howled *"Verdammte Katze!"* and drew back his free fist to smash her across the teeth.

The blow never landed.

Out of the yellow river fog behind the bridge arch stepped a tall shadow moving at a silent, dead sprint across packed snow—no warning shout, no gentlemanly challenge, just the sickening, explosive *whack-crunch* of a twelve-pound brass-cornered mahogany Leitz microscope case swung two-handed squarely across the bridge of Otto von Radowitz's nose.

Cartilage burst like a stepped-on plum. Radowitz went sideways into the snowbank spurting black-red blood between both hands before he hit the ground.

"Schoeler—!" gasped the stocky boy holding Sylwia's torn coat, releasing her so fast she stumbled against the iron rail. "Are you out of your mind—she's a Pole—!"

Ludwig didn't waste one syllable of breath. Dropping the cracked mahogany box onto the snow, unbuttoning nothing, he stepped inside the stocky youth's clumsy haymaker with the flat-footed, murderous economy he had learned taking punches from unemployed slaughterhouse apprentices in Wedding boxing cellars behind his father's back—*left hook deep under the floating ribs, right cross straight through the point of the chin*—dropping the boy flat on his spine with boots drumming once against the cobbles.

When the remaining two boys fumbled wildly inside their loden pockets—one half-drawing a brass knuckle-duster—Ludwig stooped smoothly, picked up one of Sylwia's fallen steel figure skates by its boot-ankle, and held the eleven-inch sharpened Sheffield blade waist-high under the yellow gaslight, slate-blue eyes devoid of every trace of civilization.

"Try it," Ludwig invited softly. "Please."

One look at those pale Baltic eyes above a split lip from the night before decided the debate inside two seconds. Grabbing their dazed comrade under the armpits and dragging Radowitz bleeding like a stuck pig through the slush, the four vanished wheezing up the Dorotheenstraße steps into the fog.

Silence fell under the Marschallbrücke, broken only by the creak of eighteen-inch river ice shifting against stone piers below and the harsh rasp of two seventeen-year-olds breathing white smoke into twenty-below air.

Sylwia stood pressed back against the iron railing, half her heavy dark-chestnut hair tumbled loose down over one torn shoulder, Radowitz's wool lint bitter on her bruised lower lip, heart hammering so hard in her throat she could scarcely swallow. Humiliation burned ten times hotter than fear: that *he*—of every human soul in Greater Berlin, *Ludwig Schoeler*, twenty hours after she watched his father strike him—had walked out of the fog to pull four drunken louts off her like some helpless Gretchen in a penny novelette.

Ludwig stooped, gathered her scattered Teubner Latin texts out of the snow without looking at her, brushed frost neatly off the covers with his bare scraped knuckles—his right glove had split across the metacarpals—and shoved the satchel brusquely against her ribs before pulling a clean linen handkerchief from his breast pocket.

"Wipe your mouth," he said curtly, anger vibrating rough and unfamiliar beneath his clipped baritone. "What the devil possesses you to walk the river arch alone at half past six when every beer hall around Friedrichstraße is full of half-drunk *Stahlhelm* apes? Have you straw between your ears for brains when there isn't an exam paper in front of you?"

Every shaken nerve in Sylwia's body ignited at once. She slapped his offered handkerchief away into the snow.

"Keep your linen and your lectures, Schoeler! Nobody asked you to play knight-errant with your precious microscope—if you hadn't shoved yourself in front of me, I had my knee halfway into his groin!"

Ludwig stared down at her as though she had lost her mind—taking in her torn astrakhan collar hanging by four threads, the crimson smear on her split lower lip, her tumbled dark hair catching frost crystals under the yellow gaslight, and her blazing grey eyes refusing him one half-inch of gratitude. Something dark and volcanic snapped behind his controlled Prussian mask.

"Knight-errant?" he repeated softly, stepping one long stride closer until his heavy charcoal overcoat brushed her knees. "Don't flatter yourself, Kotarska. If Radowitz wants to break windows in Wedding I couldn't care less—nobody ruins your day in this city except me. Now button your coat and walk ahead of me to the cab rank before they come back with six friends and an iron pipe."

"I wouldn't step into a Berlin cab with you if the Spree caught fire!"

"You'll get in the cab if I have to throw you over my shoulder like a sack of Silesian potatoes!" He reached out both hands—rough, peremptory, furious—and gripped the torn wool lapels of her coat to pull it closed over her throat.

Blind reflex—Radowitz's hands still burning on her skin, pride screaming, raw electricity cracking through wool—made Sylwia jerk both arms violently upward between them to shove his chest away, forgetting entirely that her left hand now held the slung pair of figure skates he had handed back with her satchel.

The heavy steel heel-spur of the swinging right skate flashed upward in the yellow fog and sliced cleanly across the outer ridge of Ludwig's right eyebrow.

Neither of them made a sound.

For half a heartbeat the cut remained white as chalk in the bitter cold; then bright, hot scarlet welled along a half-inch crescent just above the tail of his dark-gold brow, spilling in a single heavy crimson track down his lean cheekbone toward his jaw.

"Oh—God—" Sylwia gasped before she could stop herself, fury evaporating in instant medical reflex as her bare fingers rose halfway toward his bleeding temple.

Before her fingertips touched him, Ludwig caught both her wrists in midair and slammed them upward—not hurting her bones, yet pinning her bare palms flat and immovable against the frosted granite pier of the Marschallbrücke six inches above her shoulders, driving his tall, lean body squarely forward until six feet of hard muscle and heavy winter wool trapped her solid from collarbone to thigh against the stone.

Her breath broke straight in half inside her lungs.

Through two layers of winter wool—impossible at twenty below zero, delirious, terrifying—she felt every hard line of him: his broad chest heaving fast against her flattened breasts, his lean hips wedged hard against the notch of her pelvis to hold her still, the scorching furnace heat pouring off his throat and bruised mouth three inches above her upturned face. A single hot drop of blood from the crescent cut above his right eyebrow fell onto her cold left cheekbone and slid slowly down toward the corner of her parted lips.

Down in those winter-Baltic eyes staring straight into hers from three inches away, the slate ice had shattered completely—revealing black, dilated pupils and a wild, starved, bewildered fury that mirrored the violent thudding low in her own belly.

His eyes dropped from her gaze down to her bruised, parted mouth where Radowitz had split her lip, then to the drop of his own red blood resting warm against her skin. His breathing ragged out into white clouds mingling directly into hers; his thighs pressed an involuntary half-inch tighter against hers; for four catastrophic, endless seconds his head dipped downward until his cold nose brushed hers and she smelled cedar soap, clean sweat, iron blood, and winter river fog, her own eyelids fluttering half-shut while every nerve from her throat to the backs of her knees screamed *yes* and *never* in the same heartbeat—

Down on the bridge overhead, an electric tram clanged its brass gong three times—*clang-clang-clang!*—scattering blue sparks from its overhead trolley wire across the fog.

Ludwig jerked back from her as violently as if someone had pressed a white-hot branding iron between his shoulder blades.

Letting go of her wrists instantly, stepping two full paces backward onto the snow with his chest heaving and blood freezing dark along his temple, he stared at her for two silent seconds with horror and disgust—at himself, at her, at whatever madness had just opened beneath the ice under their boots—before snatching up his cracked mahogany microscope case from the snow without wiping his brow.

"Get on that tram, Kotarska," he said, his voice raw as gravel.

She didn't wipe his blood off her cheek until she was four blocks away in the rattling wooden tramcar, staring blindly at her pale reflection in the frosted glass while her knees shook all the way to Kurfürstenstraße. Neither of them ever spoke of the Marschallbrücke at school on Monday, and when Herr Doktor Glass asked dryly how Schoeler had acquired half an inch of black silk stitches above his right eyebrow, Ludwig looked straight ahead at the blackboard and answered calmly: *"Careless turn on thin ice, Herr Doktor."*
