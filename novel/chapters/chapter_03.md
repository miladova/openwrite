# CHAPTER THREE

## The Pink Posters

**Warsaw — October 20, 1943**

SS-Sturmbannführer Otto Reinhardt disliked rain, theatrical gestures, and unfinished arithmetic.

Standing under the dripping stone arch of Pavilion Three at twenty-five minutes past seven in the morning while his orderly folded his black wet umbrella, Reinhardt took a small tin box from his waistcoat pocket, placed half a yellow Bavarian pear drop neatly upon his tongue, and studied the bullet-chipped angel carved above the hospital lintel.

At thirty-nine, the chief of Referat IV-A2 at Aleja Szucha Twenty-Five looked far more like an assistant diocesan treasurer or a provincial Gymnasium mathematics master than a man whose signature sent forty souls a week to the execution sandpits behind the burnt Ghetto wall. He stood five feet nine, narrow-shouldered and immaculately brushed in mouse-grey SD service cloth bearing the silver diamond on the left cuff, rimless gold-wired spectacles resting cleanly across a pale, freckled nose. Twelve years in the Munich *Kriminalpolizei* before Himmler absorbed the Bavarian detective bureau had taught Reinhardt one enduring truth: loud men with riding whips merely bruised flesh, whereas quiet men with cross-indexed card files owned souls.

And this morning, Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler was an intensely irritating blot on an otherwise tidy card file.

*Two Parabellum bursts at four meters,* Reinhardt reflected mildly as he climbed the worn marble steps flanked by two giant SD *Oberscharführer* and fat, puffing SS-Sturmbannführer Doktor Krantz from the Ujazdów *Lazarett*. *Any ordinary passenger bleeds out before the Horch rolls to the curb. Only a damned Masurian mule from Canaris's stable takes iron under his collarbone and finds the one Polish hospital within half a mile capable of tying a subclavian artery in the dark.*

Worse, Reinhardt knew precisely why Berlin had sent Schoeler sniffing into Warsaw with a carte blanche signed by General Zeitzler's operations staff at Mauerwald. For seven weeks, British bombers and Soviet partisans east of Brest-Litovsk had been hitting ammunition trains routed through the Warsaw junction with uncanny precision—the handiwork of an underground Polish intelligence network London called *Kronos*. OKH was livid. Canaris's *Abwehr* blamed SD security lapses in the General Government; Kaltenbrunner's RSHA in Berlin blamed treasonous leaks inside Wehrmacht Army Group Centre itself.

What neither Mauerwald nor Canaris knew—and what Otto Reinhardt had zero intention of permitting Ludwig Schoeler to discover—was that Reinhardt had already identified two outer couriers of *Kronos* three weeks ago and deliberately left them running on a slack leash, feeding half-true rail manifests through a broken Polish informant inside this very district—code-named *Jaskółka*, the Swallow—in order to trace the radio transmitters all the way up to the Home Army's High Command before springing one glorious, career-making trap before Christmas.

A meddlesome General Staff bloodhound with direct cipher access to Mauerwald could ruin three months of delicate gardening in forty-eight hours. Which was why, last evening at twenty-two hundred hours, a seemingly casual telephone remark in the hearing of *Jaskółka*'s cut-out had informed a hot-headed Kedyw hit squad exactly when an unspecified high-ranking German staff car would roll off the Małkinia loop onto Aleje Jerozolimskie.

Win or lose, the gambit should have paid dividends: a dead *Abwehr* colonel blamed squarely on Polish terrorists, twenty hostages shot to appease Kutschera's bloodlust, and Schoeler's inconvenient files impounded neatly into Reinhardt's own safe at Aleja Szucha "pending investigation."

Instead, Schoeler was breathing upstairs behind two *Feldgendarmerie* submachine guns.

On the second-floor landing outside Isolation Room Two, Hauptmann Viktor Brandt stepped squarely across the threshold, scarred cheek stiff above a fresh-buttoned collar, clicking his heels with faultless, glacial Wehrmacht correctness that stopped half an inch short of yielding the doorknob.

"Heil Hitler, Herr Sturmbannführer," Brandt said flatly. "Oberstleutnant Schoeler is conscious. Only medical personnel are permitted inside at one time."

Reinhardt offered his small, sympathetic Munich clerk's smile, rolling the pear drop behind his molars. "Quite understandable after such an outrage, Hauptmann Brandt. Happily, Brigadeführer Kutschera has personally dispatched Sturmbannführer Doktor Krantz with a heated Sanka ambulance downstairs to remove Oberstleutnant Schoeler out of these squalid Polish quarters to the German wing at Ujazdów immediately."

"Out of the question before next week," said a cool, contralto woman's voice from six paces down the tiled corridor.

Reinhardt turned his head smoothly.

Walking toward them alongside white-bearded old Professor Majewski came a tall woman in a freshly boiled white coat buttoned to the chin over a dark wool skirt. Even dead on her feet—and Reinhardt's detective eye noted at once the violet hollows under her storm-grey eyes and the faint yellowing iodine smudge along her right forefinger—she carried her spine like an aristocratic lance. Thirty-two, said the brief night report telephoned from the *Feldgendarmerie* post; unmarried; surgical chief of service in Pavilion Three.

"Frau Doktor Kotarska, I presume," Reinhardt murmured pleasantly, inclining his neat head. "Allow me to congratulate you. Brigadeführer Kutschera himself was impressed that Polish hands moved swiftly enough last night to spare eighty of your countrymen an unpleasant dawn appointment. Only twenty criminals from Pawiak will be required at noon for the slain Wehrmacht driver."

Not an eyelash flickered in Sylwia Kotarska's pale face, though old Majewski beside her swallowed convulsively. *Twenty,* raced behind Sylwia's eyes like cold fire. *Twenty human beings still going against a wall at noon, and this polite little reptile expects me to curtsy for an eighty-percent discount.*

"My business is living tissue, Herr Sturmbannführer, not police arithmetic," Sylwia replied evenly. "If Doktor Krantz wishes to inspect the patient's chart and dressing, he is welcome. If he puts Oberstleutnant Schoeler onto a stretcher today, he will deliver a corpse to Ujazdów."

Fat Doktor Krantz puffed out his jowls indignantly, leather gloves creaking in his fist. "Now see here, Frau Doktor, we are hardly operating a bush dispensary at Ujazdów! A closed chest wound stabilized six hours ago—"

"Was not a simple chest wound," Sylwia cut across him without raising her voice by half a tone, pushing open the door of Isolation Room Two so Reinhardt, Krantz, and Brandt stepped into the stark whitewashed chamber together.

Propped at thirty degrees against three hard horsehair bolsters, naked to the waist beneath a folded linen sheet save for the broad white adhesive strapping and Velpeau bandage immobilizing his left shoulder and upper thorax, Ludwig Schoeler looked like a wounded hawk nailed by one wing to whitewashed plaster—pale as bone, dark-gold hair combed damply back from his high forehead, slate-blue eyes cold, lucid, and perilous.

As the four of them entered, Ludwig's gaze swept past Sylwia without the faintest tremor of private history—sliding over her as impersonally as if she were an iron bedpost—and locked straight onto Otto Reinhardt's rimless spectacles.

"Reinhardt," Ludwig rasped. His voice was hoarse, deliberate, metered carefully between shallow breaths so the rubber Pezzer tube curving from under his sheet down into the floor bottle barely stirred. "Prompt of you. One might almost think your office knew which intersection to sweep before the brass hit the cobbles."

A tiny silence crackled in the carbolic air. Brandt's hand rested motionless on his holster flap by the door.

Reinhardt smiled gently, removing his peaked cap. "Warsaw is a viper's nest, Herr Oberstleutnant, as I warned Mauerwald by teleprinter last Thursday. Had your adjutant notified my escort detachment before switching off the main station onto the suburban loop at Małkinia—"

*Check,* Sylwia heard silently in the sudden stillness—even without knowing the full cipher, she caught the thrust and parry instantly: *Nobody knew about Małkinia except whoever Brandt wired, and Schoeler just saw Reinhardt admit knowing it.*

"—my cars would have met you at the platform," Reinhardt finished smoothly. "Precisely why I cannot leave a senior officer of *Fremde Heere Ost* lying in an unfortified Polish municipal ward crawling with orderlies and coal-heavers. Doktor Krantz has a suspended-spring Mercedes ambulance waiting in the courtyard."

Ludwig's pale mouth curved into an unpleasant Prussian sneer. Without looking up at Sylwia, he flicked two fingers of his splinted right hand dismissively toward the foot of his bed. "Explain to this butcher in uniform why I am still lying on Polish straw, Frau Doktor—in words short enough for an SS medical officer to follow."

Under her starched pocket, Sylwia's fingernails bit briefly into her palm—not at the insult, which she recognized in a tenth of a second for pure, seamless Berlin theater, matching every double-play they had ever pulled on Oberstudiendirektor Becker when caught out of bounds, but at the sheer effortless nerve of the man playing court politics with four hundred milliliters of borrowed blood keeping his brain oxygenated.

"Gladly," Sylwia said, her tone cold enough to frost Krantz's spectacles. "A nine-millimeter jacket fragment fractured the middle third of the left clavicle, driving bone into the anterior wall of the left subclavian artery at the origin of the thoracoacromial trunk and lacerating the apical pleura. I resected two centimeters of comminuted clavicle for proximal control and closed a four-millimeter longitudinal arterial defect with five interrupted Carrel sutures of five-zero silk—preserving lumen patency without ligating the main vessel."

Doktor Krantz blinked rapidly, glancing down at the oscillating red fluid column in the water-seal bottle. Even an SS medical bureaucrat who hadn't held a vascular needle since thirty-six understood what repairing rather than tying off a subclavian artery meant: tying it off risked gangrene of the left arm in thirty percent of cases, whereas suturing the wall left a paper-thin anastomosis resting directly behind two mobile, jagged ends of broken collarbone.

"Without an intact clavicular strut," Sylwia continued mercilessly, fixing Krantz with eyes like surgical steel, "every vertical jolt of an ambulance axle along Marszałkowska shears the sharp lateral bone fragment across unhealed arterial silk. Should one stitch pull through, he exsanguinates into the left pleural cavity in forty seconds. Furthermore, tipping his water-seal bottle above the sixth intercostal space during stairway transport sucks room air straight back into his chest and collapses the left lung. Of course, if Sturmbannführer Doktor Krantz wishes to sign the clinical assumption-of-risk protocol right here in duplicate—one copy for Hauptmann Brandt to wire directly to Generaloberst Zeitzler's surgeon-general at Mauerwald—Nurse Nowak will fetch pen and blotter at once."

Beautiful. Deadly, impregnable, anatomical checkmate.

Ludwig did not glance at her; if he looked at her right now, the ghost of fierce, wicked admiration in his eyes might betray fifteen years in half a second. Instead he kept his cold slate gaze nailed to fat Doktor Krantz, who had begun sweating faintly around his silver collar runes.

"Well, Krantz?" Ludwig asked softly. "Sign the protocol for Zeitzler's files, or get out of my light. Which is it?"

Krantz cleared his throat twice, shooting an apologetic glance at Reinhardt. "Under the specific anatomical circumstances—lateral clavicular instability adjacent to a primary subclavian suture—six to seven days of absolute dorsal immobilization prior to transport would... medically speaking... be prudent, Sturmbannführer."

For perhaps three seconds Otto Reinhardt stood quite still, rolling the last sliver of yellow pear drop until it cracked softly between his teeth. His pale eyes moved from Ludwig's bandaged shoulder to Doktor Krantz's glistening forehead, and finally rested—thoughtfully, unhurriedly—on Sylwia Kotarska.

"Seven days, then," Reinhardt agreed amiably. "We must not jeopardize such delicate needlework. I shall assign two of my own plainclothes *Kriminalassistenten* to assist Hauptmann Brandt's sentries on the ground floor and inspect every laundry basket and soup kettle entering Pavilion Three. By the way, Frau Doktor Kotarska..."

Paused with one gloved hand on the brass porcelain handle, Reinhardt tilted his head like an appreciative music critic.

"Your German has none of the usual Warsaw palatalization—nor Vienna, where Herr Professor Majewski studied. Berlin, unless my ear deceives me?"

Behind Reinhardt's back, propped against his pillows, Ludwig's jaw remained carved marble; only the pulse at the base of his right carotid struck once against his skin.

"Four years as a schoolgirl before nineteen-thirty, Herr Sturmbannführer," Sylwia answered calmly, hiding nothing that any twenty-minute check of municipal *Kennkarte* archives wouldn't show by noon anyway—because in German Warsaw, concealing a verifiable fact was suicide, whereas handing it out wrapped in boredom disarmed curiosity. "My late father served as commercial-legal counsel at the Polish Legation on Kurfürstenstraße during the tariff negotiations. He insisted I learn to pronounce my vowels properly."

"Ah, Kurfürstenstraße. Charming chestnut trees before the widening," Reinhardt murmured. "And Herr Counselor Kotarski today?"

"Died of pneumonia at Oranienburg-Sachsenhausen in February nineteen-forty," Sylwia said, her voice as flat and clean as winter glass.

"War is regrettably wasteful of legal minds," Reinhardt sighed, bowing two inches from the waist. "Until Tuesday, Herr Oberstleutnant. Good morning, Frau Doktor."

The door clicked shut. Heavy boots receded down the marble staircase.

Inside Isolation Room Two, nobody spoke for ten full seconds while Hauptmann Brandt stepped out to verify that the SD men had cleared the second-floor landing. Left alone with Ludwig, Sylwia stepped swiftly to the bedside—not looking at his eyes—and pressed two cool fingers beneath the edge of his chest strapping to check for subcutaneous surgical emphysema. Crisp dry skin; no crackling air bubbles under the fascia.

"He knows," Ludwig murmured barely above a breath, staring straight ahead at the cracked plaster wall.

"About Małkinia, or about us?" she whispered back without pausing her clinical check of his radial pulse—ninety-two, steadier, fever climbing to thirty-eight point four Celsius.

"Nothing about us yet—thank your quick tongue on Kurfürstenstraße. Had you lied, he'd have pulled the Reichstagsufer rolls by tonight." Now Ludwig turned his head slightly on the bolster, looking straight up at the sharp profile of her jaw six inches from his shoulder. "He set those gunmen onto my car last night, Sylwia."

Her fingers stilled for half a beat over his pulse, then resumed counting against her lapel watch. "You're feverish. Since when does the Gestapo hire the Polish Underground to shoot German colonels?"

"Since an *Abwehr* colonel gets too close to a Gestapo double-agent," Ludwig whispered coldly. "Keep your friends away from this building for six days, Kotarska—and watch your own doctors. Somebody in Warsaw wears two collars."

The corridor latch rattled; Brandt returned, frowning over a cipher pad, and Sylwia stepped smoothly back to the chart hook, scrawling *Strict immobilization; fluids per os; sulfonamide 1g q.6.h.* before sweeping out of the room.

***

At ten minutes to twelve, wearing her threadbare charcoal wool autumn coat with the collar turned high against cold drizzle and her leather doctor's bag gripped in her chilled hand, Sylwia walked north up Nowy Świat toward Świętokrzyska.

Thirty-eight hours without sleep made the slick granite paving stones seem to breathe slightly beneath her sensible low heels. Around her hurried the gaunt, furtive midday crowd of Polish Warsaw: women in wooden-soled shoes clutching string bags of sprouted potatoes, pale clerks with frayed cuffs constantly scanning two blocks ahead for the gleam of green *Schutzpolizei* shakos—the street radar every child in Warsaw learned before multiplication tables.

Halfway past Ordynacka, a solitary bicycle rickshaw driver pedaling furiously southward swerved toward the curb, white-faced beneath his peaked cap, hissing through clenched teeth without slowing: *"Łapanka! Budy jadą z Krakowskiego—w bramę, szybko!"*

*Roundup. Police trucks coming down from Krakowskie Przedmieście.*

Panic rippled down both sidewalks like wind flattening rye. Shop iron grilles clanged half-shut; three boys bolted down an alley behind a bakery; men and women dove into courtyards—only to recoil in horror as twin shrill police whistles blasted simultaneously from *behind* them at the Smolna intersection as well.

Not an ordinary labor roundup.

Four covered grey-green Wehrmacht and *Ordnungspolizei* trucks screeched across both ends of Nowy Świat, sealing two hundred yards of boulevard into a stone box. Instantly, thirty helmeted *Schutzpolizei* troopers and black-collared SS gendarmes leaped over the tailgates, jackboots pounding wet pavement, MP-40s leveled at hip height, roaring through bullhorns:

*"Stehenbleiben! Alle an die Hauswand! Gesichter zur Straße! Wer rennt, wird erschossen! Hüte ab!"*

*Stand still! Everyone against the building walls! Faces to the street! Whoever runs gets shot! Hats off!*

Sylwia's back flattened hard against the cold, wet cast-iron shutter of a shuttered pre-war optician's shop. Beside her, an elderly violinist clutching a battered case removed his felt hat with trembling blue-veined fingers; to her left, a young pregnant woman pressed both hands silently over her belly, teeth chattering uncontrollably. A burly *Schupo* sergeant stalked past the line thrusting his submachine gun inches from Polish chins; Sylwia held her red-stamped *Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus — Chirurg* pass open against her breast without speaking. The sergeant barely glanced at it—because today they were not loading bystanders into the trucks.

Today, they were making Warsaw *watch*.

A fifth truck—heavy canvas tarpaulin tied down at the corners, accompanied by a glistening black Mercedes convertible out of Aleja Szucha—backed slowly onto the tram tracks directly opposite the shattered facade of a bombed café thirty yards from where Sylwia stood. Two gendarmes tossed red sand out of wooden buckets onto the wet asphalt beneath the brick garden wall.

Then they dropped the tailgate.

Out onto the rainy street stumbled twenty men, roped together two by two at the wrists with thin telephone wire.

They wore no shoes and no coats—only torn gray prison shirts or blood-caked civilian trousers, several stripped to white paper-bandage wraps hastily wound over broken ribs from Pawiak's basement interrogation cells so they wouldn't bleed on the truck floorboards before the show. None of them shouted. None of them sang the anthem. None of them could: across the lower half of every prisoner's face, from cheekbones to chin, hardened white gypsum plaster had been bound tight over stuffed rags, turning twenty living human heads into blind-mouthed chalk masks above wide, staring, agonized eyes.

Beside Sylwia, the pregnant woman began weeping without a sound, tears sliding into her coat collar while the *Schupo* sergeant screamed *"Hinsehen! Augen auf!"* at an old man who tried to look down at his boots.

Sylwia did not look down. Surgeons did not close their eyes when blood ran, and witnesses owed the dying their faces.

She counted them down the line as SS guards shoved the barefoot pairs backward onto the red sand against the pocked brick wall: one, two... elderly railwayman... tall blond boy who couldn't stand straight on a smashed knee... fourteen... seventeen...

Nineteen.

At the far right end of the line, swaying slightly beside a burly carpenter who held him upright by their shared wrist-wire, stood a slight nineteen-year-old boy with unmistakable copper-red curls matted dark with rain above freckled temples and terrified, blazing green eyes.

*Zbigniew.*

Little Zbigniew Milewski, second-year clandestine medicine, whose mother sold hot barley soup behind Hale Mirowskie so her only son could own Cunningham's *Manual of Practical Anatomy*. Eight nights ago in the morgue cellar beneath Pavilion Three, Sylwia had slapped Zbigniew's knuckles gently with blunt forceps because he confused the musculocutaneous nerve with the median cord: *Look twice before you cut, Zbyszek; you can't tie a nerve back together like shoelace.*

Across thirty yards of grey Warsaw drizzle, Zbigniew's wild green eyes swept desperately along the line of helpless Poles pinned against the shop shutters—looking for anyone, a mother, a friend, one familiar human face before the dark—and struck Sylwia's tall figure against the black iron grille.

His eyes widened violently above the white plaster gag.

Sylwia did not dare nod—one visible sign of recognition under fifty watching German rifles meant arrest on the spot—so instead she lifted her bare chin half an inch higher in the rain, took her right hand slowly off her leather bag, and rested her fingers flat over her own collarbone where the median cord ran, holding Zbigniew Milewski's terrified green eyes with fierce, unwavering, maternal steadiness so he would not go into the dark alone.

Above the white gypsum mask, the boy's frantic blinking stopped. Two tears spilled down into the hardened plaster, and his thin shoulders squared back half an inch against the brick.

An SS officer in glistening leather raised a megaphone, rattling off forty words in harsh German followed by mangled Polish: *For the cowardly Polish bandit attack of October Nineteenth upon German Wehrmacht personnel at Aleje Jerozolimskie... twenty criminals pledged to the service of Moscow and London...*

Ten riflemen and two *Scharführer* with Schmeisser submachine guns stepped to the white chalk line on the tram rails.

*"Legt—an!"*

*Keep looking at me, Zbyszek,* Sylwia screamed silently inside her skull, fingernails cutting crescents of blood into her bare left palm. *Look only at me—it takes less than a tenth of a second when it hits the brainstem—don't look at the barrels—*

*"Feuer!"*

The crash tore between the five-story stone facades like a cannonade, rolling echoes all the way down toward the Vistula and startling a flock of wet pigeons frantically upward into the low grey clouds. Bricks burst into orange dust behind jumping bodies; white plaster masks shattered red; twenty men folded backward and sideways onto the wet red sand in tangled, jerking heaps like marionettes whose strings had been slashed with one sweep of a scythe.

Zbigniew went down instantly, copper hair striking the curbstone, one bare white foot twitching twice in a rainwater puddle already turning dark crimson and sliding in thin, branching ribbons down into the grooved steel flange of the Number Nine tram rail.

For sixty seconds the SS captain walked casually down the sand pile putting single 9mm *Fangschuss* rounds from his Walther behind motionless ears—*crack... crack... crack*—before blowing two brisk blasts on a nickel whistle. Five minutes later, Pawiak's black corpse-lorry winched the tangled pile aboard under canvas; hoses washed sand and brains half into the gutter grate; police trucks roared off toward Plac Trzech Krzyży; and Nowy Świat stood open again in the rain as silent women dropped to their knees beside the crimson puddle dipping white handkerchiefs into martyrs' blood before the next patrol rounded the corner.

Sylwia did not kneel. Walking mechanically, knees locked stiff as iron splints, she turned left into Świętokrzyska, slipped down three broken cellar steps behind a burned-out antiquarian bookshop, gave two short rings and one long scrape on a rusted gas pipe, and stepped into the pitch-black coal vault behind a sliding shelf of mildewed ledgers.

A match scraped. Yellow tallow candlelight flared across vaulted seventeenth-century brick and the lean, unshaven face of Marek Zawadzki.

At thirty-four, the man *Oddział II* knew only as *Grot* wore a damp railwayman's corduroy jacket over wide, whipcord-lean shoulders; a faded bluish powder burn from the thirty-nine defense of Modlin peppered his left temple beneath cropped dark hair. On an upturned crate between them lay a stripped Vis wz. 35 pistol smelling of fresh gun oil and two cups of cold, bitter roasted-acorn coffee.

One look at Sylwia's chalk-white face and the four bleeding fingernail crescents in her left palm brought Marek swiftly to his feet. "Nowy Świat?" he asked quietly, catching her freezing hands between both of his rough, calloused palms. "We heard the volley three minutes ago. Did they touch you?"

"Zbigniew Milewski was on the end of the rope," Sylwia said. Her voice did not shake, which frightened Marek far more than tears ever had back in the winter of forty when they shared one coal stove and one narrow mattress on Puławska. "Nineteen years old. Barefoot, with gypsum crammed down his throat, for a German driver shot four blocks from my hospital."

Marek's dark jaw tightened hard, releasing her hands slowly as she pulled back a half-step—she had not let him kiss or hold her since spring forty-one, when both of them realized attachment in *Konspiracja* only gave the Gestapo two levers instead of one, though the banked heat in Marek's dark eyes whenever she walked through a cellar door alive had never quite cooled.

"Had Kedyw's grenade blown two seconds later under the rear axle instead of the front fender," Marek said grimly, picking up an oiled rag to wipe the Vis slide, "it wouldn't have been just the driver. We would have bagged the bastard in the back seat too—which brings us straight to why I dragged you out without sleep, Kora."

Sylwia unbuttoned her wet collar half an inch—her throat felt suffocated—and sat on an ammunition crate. "You knew whose car that squad hit?"

"Not at ten-forty last night," Marek admitted, eyes gleaming sharp in the candle flame. "Wola's boys acted on a snap tip—too damned snap, frankly, Counter-Intelligence is already asking where that tip dropped from—thinking they were hitting Kutschera's new chief of staff. This morning at nine, London sent an urgent triple-cipher flash after intercepting OKH Mauerwald's casualty query. Do you have any idea who you sewed back together on your table last night, Sylwia?"

Beneath her wet wool coat, Sylwia's heart gave one slow, heavy thud. *Careful,* whispered the ghost of Kazimierz Kotarski in her ear. *Show half your hand and keep the ace up your cuff.*

"Oberstleutnant Ludwig Schoeler," she said steadily. "Generalstab, attached to *Fremde Heere Ost* or *Abwehr*. And SS-Sturmbannführer Reinhardt tried to drag him out of Pavilion Three at seven-thirty this morning—which Schoeler fought tooth and nail to prevent."

Marek whistled softly between his teeth, leaning forward over the crate with both fists planted on rough pine. "Reinhardt tried to pull him out? Christ, that confirms half of London's cable. Listen closely—nobody below sector level hears a syllable of this. Schoeler isn't routine Wehrmacht staff. He was Tresckow's sharpest counter-intelligence planner at Smolensk before Kursk, transferred forty-eight hours ago with personal authority from Mauerwald to Tear *Kronos* out by the roots—our entire Eastern Front rail network from Dęblin to Białystok."

Sylwia stared into the yellow tallow flame, watching wax bleed down the bottleneck like red sand into a tram track. *He really was sent to destroy us.*

"If Schoeler gets six healthy weeks behind a desk in Warsaw," Marek continued, voice dropping to steel wire, "thirty rail observers and three London transmitters go to the gallows before New Year's. Yet while he's pinned flat on his back in *your* Pavilion Three, cut off from Ujazdów and distrusting Aleja Szucha, his adjutant Brandt has to bring every single Mauerwald courier pouch straight into Room Two!"

"Meaning?" Sylwia asked quietly.

"Meaning you kept him alive on the table last night at just the right hour, Kora—even if twenty men paid the butcher's bill today." Marek slid the heavy steel magazine home into the butt of the Vis with a sharp, final *clack*. "Keep him immobilized under your roof until Sunday. Watch who visits Brandt, find out where Brandt locks the cipher briefcase when Schoeler sleeps, and get me one clear look at the *Kronos* suspect list he brought from Königsberg. We already have a watch team taking rooms across Lindleya."

"And on Sunday," Sylwia said very softly, lifting her storm-grey eyes across the candle flame into Marek's hard face, "once you have his list?"

Marek didn't flinch. Four years of watching boys like Zbigniew Milewski hosed into Warsaw storm drains had burned every ounce of mercy out of him long ago.

"You're the finest chest surgeon in Warsaw, Sylwia," Marek said quietly. "Ten cubic centimeters of air in an intravenous line, or one stitch giving way in the night just as you warned the SS doctor it might—nobody in Berlin blames the hospital after five days, and no hostages go to the wall for a natural post-operative thrombosis. Bleed his files dry by Sunday, and then put Ludwig Schoeler in the ground for Zbigniew."
