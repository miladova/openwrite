# RANA

## Chapter One — The Officer on the Table

### Warsaw, November 1943

The snow came the way it always came that autumn — without warning, without mercy, and on the wrong side of curfew.

Sylwia Kotarska stood at the surgical amphitheatre window and watched it come down past the one gas lamp her conquered city had been permitted this winter: a single hooded slit of amber, because the street must be visible enough for the authorities to see who walked in it, and not so visible that anyone might walk it unseen. The Germans called this *Verdunkelung*. The Poles, when they called it anything at all, called it simply *the dark*, and said it the way they said everything in those years — flatly, without hope, the way one names a chronic illness.

She was thirty-five years old. She had been a surgeon for seven of them. She had kept her oath in the cellar of this hospital while the shells of September 1939 knocked the plaster from the ceilings and the windows in one long groan. She had kept it while the hospital was *rezerwowany dla Reichswehrs* — seconded to the Wehrmacht, which meant her instruments were inventoried, her chloroform weighed, her theatre reserved every other Tuesday for wounded Germans who arrived with their blood still warm from the front and their paperwork already drawn up for the Fatherland's glory. She had kept it, God help her, while the trucks came in the autumn of 1942 to empty the eastern wing, bed by bed, the old Professor Adam Rabinowicz among them — who had taught her laparotomy, who had sewn his own patients with salt in his voice and kindness in his hands, who had taken the second-best bedpan and the first-class place in every lecture hall — and whose bed she had stripped and disinfected herself because she could not bear to let a stranger touch his sheets. And she kept it through the spring of 1943, when the guns began again across the wall, four kilometres north of here, and went on for three weeks, and the night sky over Muranów went the colour of a forge, and no one in this hospital spoke of what was burning, or who was burning with it, because the hospital had a rhythm of silences now, and every one of those silences had a name.

She had told no one where the trucks had gone. She had not needed to. By then, no one in Warsaw asked that question aloud.

She kept the oath still. She was good at keeping things.

Her wards were full that evening. On the second floor, in Room 4, lay two German corporals and a Wehrmacht truck driver, their boots polished to a lying shine by some orderly's doomed policy of appeasement. In Room 9, behind a screen and under a false name, lay a boy of perhaps seventeen with a bullet in his thigh and a laminated railway pass that said he was a clerk's son from Łódź — which he was not; he was the son of the stationmaster at Radzymin, and according to Marcin Walski, who had brought him in on Tuesday wrapped in a coal tarpaulin, *he knows too much and not enough, and both are why they want him dead.* In the linen store off the laundry basement slept a Jewish child of six whom the night watchman called, without conviction, "my nephew," and whom every nurse on the night rota fed a little of her supper and no nurse ever mentioned at the wrong hour. Kamler knew. Kamler had learned to choose his Veronal, and his blindness, and his timing of breakfasts, with equal and exquisite care, the way a careful man walks a rail laid over a drop.

She had been doing this arithmetic for four years now — the ledger of what could be saved and what could not, of whom to treat first, of which German captain asked after his mother with a tremor in his voice and which one kicked a Polish orderly for breathing too audibly, of who in the Gestapo district list was cruel and who was merely frightened. The arithmetic never balanced. She balanced it anyway, every day, in the margins of her rounds, the way her father had once balanced the books of a railway company that no longer existed, for a country that no longer did.

Her hands, which had held the slippery cords of an infant's heart, which had gone into open chests and open bellies and open skulls, which had tied off arteries in the dark of a blacked-out cellar with nothing but a hurricane lamp and a cook's spoon for a retractor — her hands did not tremble. She had made a point of that, years ago, the way other women made a point of dressing well or praying well or remembering birthdays. Her hands did not tremble. It was the first thing she had noticed about herself, and the last thing that would betray her.

"Doctor." Sister Urszula appeared in the theatre doorway. Her cap had gone crooked, the way it did when the world became unbearable; she did not fix it, because the world had just become unbearable again. "They are here."

"How many?"

"One. Not a column. A staff car and an Opel truck. They say it is an officer. They say — " Urszula's voice dropped. "They say do not touch him, do not let him die, and do not let anyone else touch him."

"Those are not medical orders, Sister."

"No, Doctor." Urszula's face did something complicated. "They are not."

Sylwia reached for her apron and her instruments. "Tell Kamler."

"Kamler is asleep. He took Veronal at nine. His heart."

"Then he stays asleep," said Sylwia, who had decided that for both of them. "If this goes badly, it is better that the director of this hospital was a dead man in his bed. And if it goes well, we are all wide awake and the truth is nobody's responsibility." She snapped her gloves. "Bronia?"

"Fetching the blood book, Doctor."

"Good girl."

Below, in the courtyard, the hooded lamp did its sullen work. A Horch staff car stood in the falling snow with its lights off and its engine ticking as it cooled — a big black machine, high-sided, official, the kind of car that had not been seen this close to a Polish hospital since the occupation began. Behind it stood a grey Opel truck with canvas sides, tailgate down, its tailboard stencilled in white: **WEHRMACHT**. Snow was already softening the hood and the doors, rounding the hard angles, as if the city itself were trying to powder over the fact of the thing.

No one moved. Then figures did move — three German soldiers in the grainy dark, greatcoats and steel helmets, their boots grinding on gravel that the morning crew had not swept properly, as if sweeping for them would be a kind of welcome. Two of them carried a litter between them.

The man on the litter was tall even in stillness, one arm folded across his chest, the other trailing and knocking loosely against the litter's edge each time they jostled. An Oberleutnant's shoulder boards showed through the field grey, dark with melted snow and with something darker that the snow could not dilute. The chest dressing was thick and foul and applied with the urgent competence of men who had been taught to hold a man together with their hands and their prayers until professionals arrived.

The driver of the staff car climbed down. He was older than the others — a Leutnant, gloves in one hand, face blotched scarlet by cold and anxiety, eyes ringed with sleeplessness. He came up the stairs with the particular gait of a soldier who has driven too long too fast and does not know what to expect from a Polish hospital at midnight.

"Frau Doktor Kotarska?" He had been told her name. She noted this as the Germans intended it to be noted: as a courtesy with teeth. And they had sent not the district doctor or a general surgeon but *the* woman, the only female surgeon with operating privileges in this hospital, which meant that someone in the German hierarchy had made inquiries, and knew precisely who she was, and what she had trained under, and where she came from — Berlin, the papers would say; daughter of an engineer; Catholic; Polish. Someone had chosen her. Someone had decided she was the one they wanted holding this man together.

She did not yet know whether that was a compliment or a rope.

"I am she. Show me your man."

"Herr Oberleutnant Ludwig Schoeler, Second Artillery Regiment, Legion"— a German word she caught despite herself — "*of the Eastern Sector operations staff*," the Leutnant said. "He was — " He stopped. He had seen through the open doors behind her: the oxygen cylinders, the anaesthetic machine with its copper kettle beginning to steam, the operating table under its green sheets, the instrument tray — already laid, her instruments already chosen, though no one had yet said aloud what the wound was.

"You are prepared," he said, with something that was almost reverence.

"I am a surgeon, Herr Leutnant. We are always prepared. Bring him up."

They carried him up the stairs, and the litter swayed, and something in the patient's breathing hitched — a bubbling, wet catch that she heard from three metres away and had been hearing for twenty years in every war she had ever known, though this was the first war she was losing from inside her own country. Each man carried a carbine in the crook of his arm, half raised, unsure of whom he was supposed to be guarding the officer against: the hospital, or the officer, or the dark itself.

And on the second orderly's hip, chained through its handle to his belt, rode a leather document case, black, military-issue, its corners gone soft with use and spattered with what the snow outside had not had time to wash clean. The orderly kept one glove flat against it as he climbed, the way a man keeps a hand on a holster. Sylwia saw it. She filed it. She filed everything; filing was a habit of survival now, second nature, like breathing.

They laid him on the table and stepped back as if from a fire. One of the orderlies said something low and rapid in German that Sylwia did not bother to translate, and both turned their faces away from his chest. The Leutnant lingered one moment longer on the threshold.

For a moment, no one in the room breathed. The theatre's high windows rattled once in their frames, very softly, as some distant gun spoke east of the river.

Sister Urszula came to her side and looked down at the patient and crossed herself before the man on the table could object to such an intimacy. Bronia hovered in the doorway with the blood book clutched to her chest like a shield. Snow whispered against the high theatre windows. Somewhere below, a German engine cleared its throat and went silent.

"Frau Doktor," the Leutnant said from the threshold, and there was, underneath the exhaustion, something almost apologetic in him. "Forgive me. I was told only: get him to a surgeon, get him there fast, and if he dies, answer to *Kriminalrat Brenner*." He swallowed. "I do not know what that means. I am a transport officer. Artillery." A pause. "We were bringing him back from the eastern road when it happened. He would not let us stop. He said —" The Leutnant stopped, as if only now realising he was about to quote his commanding officer to a Polish woman in her own hospital.

"Say it," said Sylwia.

"*Liefert ihn nicht,*" the Leutnant recited, in the flat, careful German of a man repeating something memorised, "*the words* — " He reassembled it in his own tongue, stumbling. "*Do not deliver him until the surgeon has seen his eyes.* Those were his words. Herr Oberleutnant's. Before the morphine. He made me swear it. That is why I drove the way I did."

The theatre was very still. Ether fumes curled up from the kettle. Outside, the hooded lamp threw its thin amber blade across the courtyard snow.

"Get. Out." Sylwia's voice was quiet and it was level and it had iron under it. "Leutnant Hesse — is it Hesse?" He blinked. "Hesse. Your man is going into surgery. If I need you, I will find you. If I do not find you, it means I am working, and that your Oberleutnant is alive, or was, and that either way you will speak of tonight to no one outside these walls — not Brenner, not your general, not your priest, not your wife. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Hesse said, very quickly. "Yes, Frau Doktor."

"Then go and keep your soldiers from freezing to death, and count the gravel in that courtyard until I call you."

The doors sighed shut behind Leutnant Hesse and his men, and the leather document case went with them — not with the patient. Sylwia noted it with a surgeon's detached interest: the case was not his to carry into the theatre, then, but theirs to carry for him. Which told her everything, and nothing.

"Bronia," said Sylwia. "O negative, if the cabinet holds it. He comes before our own wounded tonight. That is an order you will regret taking from me if you think about it, so do not think about it."

"Yes, Doctor." Bronia backed out, hugging the book.

"Urszula. Rib spreader. Long clamps. Silk. The drain trocar. And if I need wire, wire."

"Wire," said Urszula, "is on the second tray, Doctor, where it has been for three weeks," which was her way of saying *I already know, I always know, I have known since before you came here*, and Sylwia almost smiled at that, because it was true.

Then she stepped to the table and looked down at the enemy of her life, and the room seemed to go very quiet, the way it does when a wave draws back before it breaks.

He was older, his face thinner, the fine bones she had once known twisted into the harder architecture of a man who had commanded men and buried boys and not slept properly in years. The hair at his temples had gone iron. There was a scar through his left brow she did not remember from Berlin, and there, on his right hand, flaked black at the edges, was the small burn that a schoolboy once got holding a magnesium ribbon at a chemistry demonstration — a ribbon she had thrown at him, actually, on purpose, in the winter of 1929, an act for which the school had very nearly expelled them both.

Ludwig Schoeler lay with his eyes open and fixed on the lamp above him with the ferocious unseeing stillness of a man spending the last of himself on staying conscious. The hooded lamp showed her the face she had last seen on the far side of a schoolyard in Berlin in the summer of 1932 — and time had done to it what time does, which is to take the arrogant youth out of it and leave the man, which is a far more dangerous and more interesting animal.

His eyes moved. They found her.

They moved again — focused, disbelieved, focused again.

"*Sylwia*," he said. It came out like something dragged over gravel — low, dry, and under it, even now, even like this, even in a Polish hospital at midnight between the occupiers and the occupied, a thread of amusement, frayed and defiant and unmistakably his. "*Of all the theatres in Warsaw — of all the surgeons on earth — they send me to you. Diogenes went looking for an honest man. The Wehrmacht, it seems, just wanted the best knife in the district.*"

"Doctor," said Urszula sharply, because the patient's breathing had caught again, worse this time — a sucking, tearing rhythm.

Sylwia raised one hand without looking. "He is mine, Sister. Stand by. Bronia — "

The blood cabinet was opened below. Somewhere, distantly, a door slammed and a Polish voice shouted a German order at another Polish voice. The snow went on falling.

"Bronia," repeated Sylwia, louder. "O negative, now."

Steps fled down the corridor. The anaesthetic kettle began to purr. Urszula slipped the gauze into Sylwia's palm, and their fingers touched, and Urszula said, in a whisper pitched below the ether-hiss and the snow: "Doctor. If he does not pull through — if he dies on your table tonight — the hospital is finished. Kamler's heart, the eastern wing, all of it. They will not distinguish between accident and— " She did not finish. She did not need to.

"Then he does not die," said Sylwia.

"He is a German officer, Madam."

"He is a patient on my table, Sister, and there is no nation in his wound." Sylwia laid the back of her cold hand for one second on Ludwig's brow — one second, no more; she would not give him the comfort of warmth, not yet, perhaps not ever — and felt the heat of him, and the hammering of his heart against her wrist like something trapped and furious. "Begin the ether. I am going to look at my enemy."

She took his wrist and held it and watched his eyes. They watched her back. In Berlin, in the old days, they had watched each other across classrooms and quadrangles and exam halls for five years, and she had never once been able to look away first.

"Ludwig von Schoeler," she said softly, in German, so that if the orderlies were outside the door with their ears against the wood they would hear nothing that was not already known. "You insufferable, arrogant, fencing-mad—"

"—*beautifully exquisite coil of wire and ether and steel,*" Ludwig finished for her, with immense effort, and a wet, bubbling cough, "*to be in your hands again, Fräulein Kotarska. Do try not to savour it.*"

"Quiet," she said, in Polish, and saw to her private astonishment that the corner of his mouth moved — that the old feud, the legend of it, the Berlin of gaslight and chalk dust and two furious young animals orbiting each other, had not died in this man, not even with a bullet in him, not even now.

"Doctor." Urszula's voice had gone strange. "His colour. It is going."

It was. She saw it too. The grey of shock had climbed from his lips to his cheekbones. His fingers twitched on the sheet. The wet catch in his breathing was becoming a liquid gurgle. Blood, she understood suddenly, was collecting inside his chest cavity — the bullet was not merely a hole; it was an anchor tearing at something inside of him with every heartbeat, and the pleural space was filling, and the heart — his stubborn, Prussian, impenitent heart — was working harder and harder against a rising tide of its own blood and being asked, politely, by the world, to stop.

"Bronia! Where is that blood?"

"Coming!" The girl's footsteps on the stairs, skidding once. A crash of the cabinet door. The book slapped open. "Two units! I have two units of O negative!"

"Hang it. Cross-match be damned. Urszula — open the set."

Urszula was already moving. The needle found the vein in his arm with the smooth, unglamorous professionalism of a woman who had started IVs by candlelight in cellars and never once dropped one. The line ran. Ludwig's eyes had not left Sylwia's face, and in them, behind the fear and the pain and the grey, something came alight that she knew from across fifteen years and two languages and one continent:

The look of a man who had not yet been beaten, and knew it, and had been waiting — though he would die before he said so — for her to be the one to find out whether he could be.

"Bronia," said Sylwia. "The eastern wing keys are in my apron pocket. If — if there is a problem — " A pause. "No. Nothing. Watch the line."

She took the dressing scissors. She cut away the filthy field dressing in black strata — layer by layer, gauze gone stiff and brown, the smell of blood and cold iron and ether and the faintly sour note of a wound gone too long without light. Beneath it: the upper chest, right side, near the shoulder. Entry wound small and puckered, exit large and smashed. Powder stippling around the entry — which meant the muzzle had been close, almost intimate, a pistol pressed to flesh, not a rifle fired from a field. Air hissed at the wound's edges with every breath: a pneumothorax, and not a small one. The lower border of the pectoral muscle was torn. The third rib was cracked. Something inside had been gouged, and whatever it was, it was filling his chest with blood.

She heard herself say the numbers — pressure, pulse, respiration, colour, response — and heard her own voice come out level, clinical, and nothing like the animal that at seventeen had wanted to slap this face until it stopped smirking. Sweat stood on her own brow. The ether was sweet in the cold air.

Her father, Adam Kotarski, dead these four years in a cellar of this hospital while the shells walked the streets, had said to her in the last week of his life, holding her wrist with his thin, fevered hand: *Oath first, Sylwia. Always, oath first. The enemy dies like a man. The patient dies like God permits. But the oath does not die. If you keep the oath, the rest of you has a chance of surviving it.*

"Oath first," she said aloud, to the room and to no one.

"Doctor?"

"Nothing." She laid her hand flat on the wound, just above the entry, feeling the wet drag of the torn tissue and the horrifying, unmistakable flutter where the bullet had clipped something near his lung. "Begin the ether. I am going in."

Urszula lowered the mask. Bronia held the basin. The blood dripped, and dripped, and dripped.

Ludwig stirred as the ether took him down. His eyes found hers through the blur — through the shine of sweat, through fifteen years, through Berlin and through the war and through everything that had happened between them since the day two furious young fools had collided in a corridor with chalk dust on their cuffs.

"*Sylwia,*" he said, very quietly, as though testing whether it was really her, or whether he was already somewhere else entirely. A thread of blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. "*The last time I saw you — you were winning something.*"

"Sleep, Ludwig," she said.

"*I have been trying to,*" he whispered, "*for —* "

The anaesthetic closed over him like dark water.

Sylwia stood for one second with the scalpel in her hand and looked down at the face she had hated for half her life — hated it with an intimacy greater than friendship, greater than most loves, with the total, all-consuming, magnificent attention that only truly can be paid between two people who recognise, with horror, that the other is the only one in the world who is exactly as clever, exactly as proud, and exactly as doomed as they are.

Below them, in the courtyard, the staff car ticked in the snow. The sentry stamped his feet and wished he were elsewhere. Berlin's lamps burned on. Warsaw's did not. The eastern wing was empty and swept and silent. Somewhere, in a cellar in Wola, a German officer of another kind was drinking schnapps and counting the days.

She did not know, yet, what she knew. She knew only what her father had taught her, and what the ether knew, and what her hands, which did not tremble, were already preparing to do.

She set the scalpel to Ludwig Schoeler's chest — the chest of an officer of an army that had burned her city's archives, her hospitals, her eastern wing, her law, her sky — and cut.

The steel sang once, low and certain, and in that sound, every version of the life she might have lived — Berlin, 1928; Berlin, 1932; a life she had not chosen; a war she had not wanted; an enemy she had not expected — began, at last, to open like a wound.
