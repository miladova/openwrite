# CHAPTER FIVE

## Morphine and Glass

**Warsaw — October 22–24, 1943**

By ten o'clock on Friday night, Ludwig Schoeler's fever stood at thirty-nine point six degrees Celsius, and a hand's-breadth of skin four inches below his left nipple had turned the angry, taut plum-violet of deep cellulitis.

Sylwia stood over Isolation Room Two's iron bedstead in the pooled amber cone of a gooseneck examination lamp, pressing two ungloved fingertips lightly along the upper border of his fifth rib at the anterior axillary line. Even before her fingers met the hot, brawny induration beneath the skin, she felt the microscopic flinch lock every muscle across his flat abdomen and good right shoulder.

"Deep fluctuation," she said in Polish to Halina, who held the enamel instrument kidney-dish beside the gooseneck lamp. "A piece of uniform cloth or secondary chrome splinter sheared downward along the serratus plane during the blast before he ever reached us. If that pus tracks upward beneath pectoralis minor tonight, it bathes the subclavian silk in streptococcus by tomorrow noon and dissolves the arterial wall."

Propped at forty-five degrees, naked to the waist save for the adhesive strapping higher on his collarbone, Ludwig watched her face intently. Fever burned two dark hectic coins high over his cheekbones and turned his slate-blue eyes unnaturally bright, yet his mind had missed neither her tone nor the glance toward the scalpel dish. He did not know twenty words of Polish—another childhood point of stubborn Prussian pride that had infuriated Counselor Kotarski—but he knew surgical steel when he saw Halina lift the sterile towel.

"Speak German when you plan to carve my ribs, Frau Doktor," Ludwig rasped. Sweat gleamed in the hollow of his throat and darkened the tarnished-gold hair at his temples. "Foreign debris?"

"A secondary fragment pocketed over the fourth intercostal space," Sylwia answered coolly, reaching for the carbolic spirit swab. "Five centimeters deep under serratus anterior. I have to open it, extract whatever foreign body seeded the abscess, and pack it open with iodoform gauze tonight. You have basal rales in your left lower lobe—twenty minutes of general ether will give you fatal pneumonia before Sunday. Nurse Nowak has drawn fifteen milligrams of morphine for your deltoid; in twenty minutes I'll infiltrate one-percent procaine and—"

"Empty the morphine into the sink," Ludwig said.

Halina paused with the Pravaz syringe poised, shooting an exasperated look at Sylwia. For seventy-two hours this impossible German had refused anything stronger than aspirin and boiled willow-bark tea while enduring chest-tube suction that made grown stevedores howl into their pillows.

"Don't be an adolescent fool, Herr Oberstleutnant," Sylwia snapped, impatience roughening her cool clinical mask for the first time since Wednesday morning. "One-percent procaine blocks cutaneous nerves; acidic pus in deep intercostal muscle neutralizes local anesthetic completely. Once my forceps touch inflamed periosteum on that fourth rib, you will feel every millimeter as live iron."

"Then cut fast," Ludwig said quietly. Reaching across with his good right hand—the basilic cut-down had been capped off yesterday, leaving a bandaged bruise at his elbow that mirrored hers—he unbuckled the heavy brown leather Wehrmacht officer's belt hanging over his bedpost where Hauptmann Brandt had slung his holster before going off to Mauerwald telegraph headquarters twenty minutes ago. Folding the thick saddle-leather strap double away from the silver double-claw buckle, Ludwig laid it squarely beside his hip. "Reinhardt's two jackals down in your lobby have already tried bribing your night porter twice with Polish vodka to learn when I sleep. While Brandt is across town with the cipher pouch, I don't go under poppies for you, for Reinhardt, or for Almighty God."

Out in the corridor beyond the frosted glass, rapid footsteps clicked—not the heavy paced tread of the lone *Feldgendarme* sentry Brandt had left behind, nor hospital clogs, but sharp leather civilian oxfords ascending the main stairs.

Halina slipped noiselessly to the door crack, listened three seconds alongside the German sentry's guttural challenge, and turned back white-lipped. "Pani Doktor," she whispered urgently in Polish, "one of Reinhardt's plainclothes SD men from downstairs is arguing with Dr. Karski at the nurses' desk—demanding to inspect the narcotics ledger and Recovery Ward charts right now! And old Władek hasn't finished moving the soiled bandages up from the boiler room chute!"

Ice shot through Sylwia's veins. *If an SD man wanders past Recovery toward the back service stairs while Władek has the cellar trap unbolted—*

"Go," Sylwia told Halina in rapid, flat Polish while busily uncapping the iodine bottle as if issuing routine ward instructions. "Intercept the SD bastard at the nurses' desk, bury him in six years of German-stamped typhus ledgers, and drop a bedpan down the laundry chute—three clangs—so Władek bolts the iron door from inside. Do not come back until the lobby is clear."

Halina laid the sterile tray beside the gooseneck lamp, vanished out the door, and pulled the latch shut behind her.

Sylwia stood alone in the amber pool of lamplight with Ludwig Schoeler.

Without turning around, she stepped backward and slid the heavy brass interior privacy bolt home with a sharp, solid *clunk*.

From the bed, Ludwig's bright, fever-honed gaze tracked the bolt, tracked her pulse at the base of her throat, and missed nothing. "Trouble with the house guests?" he murmured.

"Routine police curiosity about morphine tallies," Sylwia lied smoothly, snapping sterile rubber gloves over her bare hands and drawing up ten cubic centimeters of one-percent Novocain. "Which means you just saved the hospital fifteen milligrams for someone with sense enough to appreciate it. Roll twenty degrees onto your right hip and hook your right arm around the iron upright above your head. If you jerk while my blade is over the intercostal bundle, I'll open the intercostal artery or puncture the pleura a second time."

Ludwig shifted his weight onto his right flank with a low, controlled hiss between clenched teeth as his shattered left collarbone took the torque. Gripping the cold painted iron bar of the headboard above his crown in his right fist, he set the folded brown saddle-leather strap squarely between his back molars. Even wasted ten pounds by four months of Kursk retreat rations and seventy-two hours of trauma fever, his bare torso in the oblique amber lamplight made Sylwia's breath catch against her will before surgical detachment locked down: wide, flat-muscled shoulders tapering to a narrow waist, faint tarnished-gold hair dusting the center of his sternum and trailing down into the linen sheet at his navel, and low along his right flank an ugly, puckered four-inch purple crescent from a Russian mortar splinter three months healed.

*Meat and fascia,* she ordered herself fiercely, painting brilliant brown-gold tincture of iodine over the swollen violet mound below his pectoral edge. *Skin, subcutaneous fat, serratus digitations, fourth rib. Nothing else.*

She raised the Novocain needle. "Sting."

Four rapid intradermal wheals raised pale orange-peel dimples along the skin line. He didn't blink. Waiting ninety seconds for skin anesthesia—sixty seconds longer felt intolerable in the thick, rain-muffled silence where the only sound was his fast feverish breathing and the *glug-tick* of his chest tube—she picked up a #10 scalpel.

"Now," she said quietly, and drew a clean four-centimeter incision straight through skin and superficial fascia.

Through skin, nothing moved save a tightening of his forearm tendons on the iron headboard. Dark venous blood welled, followed immediately as she parted the deep fascia with blunt Mayo scissors by a sudden gush of thick, blood-streaked grey pus under pressure. She swabbed swiftly with peroxide sponges—good, she had caught it twelve hours before streptococcal necrosis spread upward—and slid her gloved left index finger straight into the hot, raw cavity between muscle fibers to palpate the fourth rib.

The instant her finger brushed deep inflamed periosteum, every muscle in Ludwig Schoeler's body bowed rigid off the mattress like a drawn yew bow.

Leather creaked horribly between his clamped jaws; veins stood out thick as whipcords along his neck and right bicep; sweat burst in shining sheets across his forehead and ribs, sliding down into the hollow beneath his collarbone. Deep at the bottom of the burning pocket, wedged flat under a digitation of serratus anterior right against bare costal bone, her fingertip struck jagged metal—half an inch of twisted nickel window-trim packed around a sodden wad of dirty grey wool.

*Hold on,* she caught herself thinking fiercely, her left hand steadying flat against his slick, burning lower ribs to anchor him while her right slid curved Kocher forceps down along her guiding finger. *Two seconds, Ludwig—don't move—*

Metal clicked against metal inside live flesh.

With one ruthless, precise twist to free the hooked barb from periosteum, she wrenched the shard out through the muscle track.

A raw, animal groan tore choked and muffled around the doubled leather belt in Ludwig's teeth; the iron headboard rattled violently against the plaster wall as white paint flaked under his crushing right knuckles; then the splinter dropped *tink* into the enamel basin, and he collapsed flat back against the bolsters, chest heaving raggedly, eyes squeezed shut for three seconds while waves of grey vagal nausea washed chalk-white under his fever flush.

Working at lightning speed before spasm set in, Sylwia irrigated the pocket twice with warm Dakin's solution, plucked out the last wet thread of uniform wool, packed two inches of yellow iodoform ribbon gently into the cavity so it would granulate cleanly from the bottom up, and taped a dry gauze pad across the incision.

When she stripped off her bloodied gloves and reached up with a cool damp linen towel to wipe the blinding sweat out of his eyes before it ran into his collarbone dressing, Ludwig spat the tooth-marked leather strap onto the blanket and caught his breath in ragged, shivering pulls.

His eyes opened, staring straight up into hers from ten inches away as her hand paused with the damp towel against his temple—half an inch from the white crescent scar above his right brow.

"Still cut like a butcher, Kotarska," he whispered, voice shredded to dark velvet gravel, trying to mock her while a fine, uncontrollable tremor of adrenergic shock ran through his chest beneath her knuckles.

Sylwia pulled the towel back as though his skin had blistered her fingers, stepping to the basin to drop the linen and fetch the glass of water. Because her hand wanted to shake too, she kept her voice colder than snow. "Drink half of this before your pressure drops. Your splinter is in the dish; your fever will break before morning."

"Keep the water." Supporting himself slightly on his right elbow despite the pallor around his mouth, Ludwig looked past the amber lamp at the bolted door, and then slowly, searchingly up at her tall silhouette against the whitewashed wall. "Tell me the truth, Sylwia. Not the ward speech you gave Brandt."

"I don't tell truths to German officers. They put people against brick walls for far less."

"Why didn't you kill me on Tuesday night?"

The quiet directness of it hung like gunsmoke between them. Outside, October wind slammed a loose tin gutter thrice against brick.

Sylwia stood motionless beside the instrument stand, arms folded loosely across her blood-spotted white coat. "I told you on Wednesday morning. Your butcher at Aleja Szucha promised a hundred dead Poles at dawn, including my nurses."

"Before Brandt's sergeant brought that telephone message into the theater," Ludwig said softly, eyes narrowing with terrifying precision. "Brandt told me every word of the sequence yesterday when I questioned him. You already had your hand inside my wound holding the artery closed for three full minutes before the sergeant came up the stairs with Reinhardt's threat. One slip in the dark before anyone knew who was bleeding—why didn't you let go?"

For five heartbeats the mask of Dr. Kotarska strained at its rivets. All the horror kept chained down since noon Wednesday—bare white feet twitching on red sand, green eyes staring above white plaster, copper curls soaked crimson in the Number Nine tram rail—rose up like black bile behind her teeth.

She stepped straight back to the edge of his mattress, leaning both palms on the iron foot-rail so the amber lamp lit the furious, stripped desolation in her grey eyes.

"Would you have preferred I let go, Ludwig? Because by noon Wednesday I wished to Christ I had!" Her whisper lashed across the four feet between them, raw and unfiltered in the Berlin German nobody else on earth heard from her throat. "Twenty men still went against a wall on Nowy Świat at twelve o'clock for your dead driver! I stood thirty paces away in the rain with your green policemen pointing submachine guns at my ribs and watched them shoot a nineteen-year-old medical student of mine—barefoot, with plaster of Paris stuffed down his throat so he couldn't even cry out for his mother before your bullets took the back of his skull off! Nineteen! Younger than Konrad was when you left Berlin!"

At his brother's name, something flickered violently behind Ludwig's pale slate eyes—like lightning striking far out behind dark sea-ice. He did not interrupt her; his jaw set like stone, taking every blow full across the face.

"You want to know why I clamped your artery before the telephone rang?" Sylwia breathed, leaning closer, hatred and exhaustion vibrating through every bone in her body. "Because for two minutes before Halina wiped the soot off your face, you were tissue on my table, and once I saw that scar over your eyebrow—God forgive me—some arrogant, seventeen-year-old devil inside me refused to let Ludwig Schoeler cheat me by dying asleep under ether without knowing a Pole owned his life! And nineteen-year-old Zbigniew Milewski is rotting in an unmarked lime pit tonight while you lie in clean sheets hunting Warsaw railwaymen!"

Silence roared in the narrow room after her voice broke off.

For a long moment Ludwig looked at her hands gripping the iron rail—white-knuckled, scarred with carbolic cracks, stained faintly brown with iodine—and then slowly lowered his gaze to the bandaged inner crease of his own right elbow.

"Your father," Ludwig said very quietly. "You told Reinhardt Oranienburg-Sachsenhausen, February forty. He had diplomatic immunity through thirty-four—why didn't he leave Warsaw with Beck's ministry through Romania in September thirty-nine?"

"Because he was sixty years old and refused to run while Warsaw burned!" Sylwia snapped, throat aching so savagely she would have died sooner than let one tear spill in front of him. "He stayed behind to help burn the municipal reserve rosters before your *Einsatzgruppe IV* marched down Aleje Ujazdowskie with alphabetical arrest lists! Four polite gentlemen in black coats—very much like your friend Reinhardt—took him out of our flat on Mokotowska in November, broke six of his fingers in the basement of Aleja Szucha asking for French Embassy cipher keys he hadn't touched in nine years, and shipped him north in an unheated cattle car at twenty-four below zero! When the Red Cross sent his ashes back in a cardboard margarine carton, they charged me twelve Reichsmarks forty pfennigs postage! Did Generalmajor Schoeler drink a glass of hock to it in Dahlem?"

"My father died of a stroke over his morning newspapers in November forty-one," Ludwig answered, his voice flat, stripped bare of every inflection save iron fact, "two weeks after calling Hitler a Viennese corporal lunatic—not because my father pitied Poles or Russians, mind you, never mistake that—only because Hitler forgot to issue winter grease before Moscow. And Konrad burned to death inside a Panzer Mark Four outside Orel on the fourteenth of July. They didn't send a margarine carton; there wasn't enough left of him to scrape off the turret ring."

The air went suddenly, freezing empty between them.

In her mind's eye flashed the bright-eyed fourteen-year-old boy in evening dress choking happily on an almond macaroon beside the malachite urns at Pariser Platz—*Your brother Konrad has his mother's soft heart; nobody expects steel from him.* Burned alive in blackened steel in Russian wheat.

Neither offered pity. Between Prussia and Poland in October nineteen-forty-three, pity would have been an obscenity; yet across that pitiless honesty, some raw nerve recognized its mirror in the dark.

"Then why are you still wearing their crimson stripes and hunting Polish shadows in Warsaw, Ludwig?" she whispered.

Ludwig looked straight up into her storm-grey eyes, fever sweat cooling along his bare collarbones. "Because if a certain breed of lunatic finishes burning Europe down from the inside, Sylwia, neither Warsaw nor Berlin will have two bricks left standing on top of each other by forty-five—and dead men at Orel don't hold levers. Speaking of shadows..."

His voice dropped so low that even an ear pressed directly against the corridor keyhole could have heard nothing over the rain:

"Tell your night porter that dragging fifty-kilo coal sacks *up* from a cellar boiler room at two in the morning makes an altogether different scrape on stone steps than carrying stretchers *down*. Even through six inches of brick floor, a man lying awake without morphine has ears, Kotarska."

Sylwia's heart stopped dead. Not a muscle in her face moved, yet every drop of blood seemed to drain out of her palms.

"I have no idea what hallucinations your fever is inventing," she said evenly. "The typhus isolation laundry is in the sub-basement."

"Of course it is," Ludwig murmured dryly, easing himself painfully back flat onto the damp bolsters and closing his eyes as exhaustion finally dragged anchors onto his chest. "Only make damned sure your laundry doesn't groan when Reinhardt's two jackals walk past the coal grating tomorrow morning—because if Aleja Szucha finds wounded *Armia Krajowa* boys under my mattress while I'm still pinned to this bed, Reinhardt hangs all of us on the same wire. Unbolt the door on your way out, Frau Doktor. And take that splinter with you."

***

Forty-eight hours later, on Sunday dusk half an hour before curfew, rain filmed the plate-glass front of the *Apotheke Wendt* in the German quarter off Marszałkowska, where a frosted back-parlor door marked *Privat — Laboratorium* smelled pleasantly of camphor, dried chamomile, and hot Turkish tobacco.

Dr. Roman Karski sat on the edge of a horsehair plush chair with his damp trench coat still buttoned to his chin, squeezing his knees tightly together so SS-Sturmbannführer Otto Reinhardt would not notice the rhythmic, humiliating tremor jumping in his right calf.

Sixteen hours since his last eighth-grain injection. His stomach felt lined with cold greasy copper; every scrape of Reinhardt's silver teaspoon stirring real sugar into two porcelain cups of Dutch cocoa scraped straight across Karski's raw auditory nerves like a razor across slate.

"Drink while it is hot, Herr Doktor," Reinhardt urged kindly across the mahogany table, pushing the steaming cup toward Karski's clammy hands. "Autumn damp is atrocious for the bronchi, and you doctors neglect yourselves scandalously."

"Have you word from Vittel?" Karski blurted in rough, desperate German, ignoring the cocoa entirely, bloodshot eyes fixed hungrily on the slim calfskin folder beside Reinhardt's elbow. "You promised last Thursday—it has been nine weeks since the Hotel Polski train pulled out of Dworzec Gdański, Herr Sturmbannführer! Estera's lungs were weak even in July—"

"Patience, my dear fellow. Wartime postal censorship through Alsace moves at a snail's pace." Smiling sympathetically behind his rimless spectacles, Reinhardt opened the calfskin folder six inches—just far enough for Karski to glimpse a yellow *Internierungslager Vittel* registry slip typed neatly with *GOLDBLUM, ESTERA — Geb. 14.V.1916 Warschau, Paraguay-Pass Nr. 419-B*—and drew out a small square of pale blue stationery bearing three lines in cramped, penciled Polish script:

*Najdroższy Romku — Jest tu zimno, ale dają mleko i czekamy na wymianę. Nie trać nadziei. Twoja E.*

*Dearest Romek — It is cold here, and they give milk and we wait for the exchange. Do not lose hope. Your E.*

A ragged, wet sob broke in Roman Karski's throat before he could choke it back. Reaching across the table with shaking fingers to touch the blue paper—Reinhardt deftly slid it back inside the calfskin folder half a second before Karski's wet thumb could smudge the fresh pencil lead produced forty minutes earlier by Referat IV's Jewish handwriting forger down on Aleja Szucha—Karski pressed his knuckles over his mouth, tears leaking hot down his unshaven cheeks.

*She's alive. Thank Christ, thank the Virgin, Estera is alive in France.*

"Next Friday's Red Cross diplomatic pouch carries winter parcels to Vittel," Reinhardt murmured gently, laying two pristine cardboard boxes of Bayer *Morphium hydrochloricum* ampoules—ten twenty-milligram vials per box, clean factory seals unbroken—beside Karski's untouched cocoa. "Warm woolens, Swiss chocolate, cod-liver oil. Provided, naturally, my superiors see concrete justification for maintaining special protection on File Four-Nineteen-B."

Karski's pinprick pupils darted from the closed calfskin folder to the twenty gleaming glass vials of salvation on the mahogany table, and every vestige of medical pride collapsed inward like rotted timber.

"Tell me what you need," Karski whispered hoarsely.

"Oberstleutnant Schoeler," Reinhardt said, taking half a yellow pear drop from his waistcoat tin. "Tuesday marks seven days. Yet Hauptmann Brandt informed Ujazdów this morning that Frau Doktor Kotarska insists on retaining the colonel through Thursday due to a secondary abscess."

"That part is clinically genuine," Karski hurried to confirm, eager to sound reliable. "Friday night—forty-five minutes past ten—he spiked nearly forty degrees. Kotarska drained a deep intercostal pocket over the fourth rib and extracted a piece of car metal."

"With Nurse Nowak assisting?"

"No. Nowak came down to the desk when your man Krauss checked the ward books. Kotarska bolted Isolation Two from the inside and stayed alone with him for forty minutes."

Behind his gold-wired spectacles, Otto Reinhardt's pale eyes went completely still. "Bolted from the inside? While operating on an un-drugged German officer?"

"He refused morphine," Karski muttered bitterly, resenting even the memory of wasted ampoules poured down porcelain. "And there's something strange between them, Herr Sturmbannführer—I can't put my finger on it, she bites his head off whenever anyone's listening, cold as ice, yet she checks his chest tube herself at four in the morning instead of letting me near him! And Brandt never leaves the black leather dispatch case inside Room Two when he goes to telegraph headquarters—except for ten minutes at six each morning when Brandt washes and shaves in the doctors' lavatory down the hall, locking the briefcase inside the iron medicine safe in Room Two's anteroom."

"An iron medicine safe," Reinhardt repeated softly, pleasurably, like a connoisseur catching the first cello theme of a Brandenburg Concerto. "To which the chief surgeon of Pavilion Three possesses a duplicate key?"

"Hanging on her belt ring," Karski whispered, reaching with trembling hands for the two Bayer boxes as Reinhardt pushed them smoothly across the polished wood.

"Keep your eyes open tomorrow and Tuesday, *Jaskółka*," Reinhardt said warmly. "And drink your cocoa before you walk into the rain. We must keep Estera's future husband in robust health."
