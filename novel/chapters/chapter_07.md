# CHAPTER SEVEN

## The Melian Dialogue

**Warsaw — November 1, 1943**

On the night of All Saints, Warsaw belonged to ghosts.

Standing shirtless in the unlit window embrasure of Isolation Room Two with his field-grey greatcoat draped loose across his braced shoulders against the draft, Ludwig Schoeler parted the edge of the black paper blind by half a finger's breadth and looked down into ulica Lindleya.

Sleet had given way at twilight to a thin, freezing ground-mist smelling of wet dead leaves and coal clinkers. Four hundred meters east toward Marszałkowska, wherever pink execution posters clung peeling to brick walls above scuffed patches of red sand, small points of yellow light bloomed out of nowhere in the gathering dark. Old women in black shawls and ten-year-old girls darted out of tenement archways after the seven-o'clock patrol passed, setting down stub ends of tallow candles sheltered inside cracked jam jars, whispering a five-second *Wieczny odpoczynek*—Eternal rest grant unto them—and melting back into the mist before hobnailed boots rounded the corner again to smash glass and wax into the gutter.

Ten minutes later, out came three more jam jars in the exact same spot.

*Father never understood this city,* Ludwig thought quietly, leaning his good right shoulder against the cold window plaster while a dull, familiar ache throbbed beneath his healing left collarbone. *None of our eastern-march theorists ever did. You can break a Russian army if you encircle its railheads; you can cow Berlin with three police ordinances and a pension cut. Crush Poles under fifty divisions and they turn graveyards into assembly points.*

Twelve days since the ambush on Aleje Jerozolimskie. Yesterday morning Sylwia had pulled the rubber Pezzer tube out from between his sixth and seventh ribs in one smooth, stinging tug on peak expiration and slapped down petrolatum gauze; four days of morning-and-evening intravenous *Cibazol* had dropped his temperature to thirty-seven point one and cleared his pleural effusion clean. He had lost twelve pounds he couldn't easily spare, his left arm still hung strapped across his ribs in a crisp linen sling to keep the ununited collarbone quiet behind those five microscopic threads of black arterial silk, and walking twenty paces left sweat prickling his spine—yet his head was clear as winter crystal.

Behind him, the corridor door opened twice after the regulation knock, and Hauptmann Viktor Brandt stepped inside, shaking sleet from his leather dispatch cape before bolting the latch.

"Well?" Ludwig asked without turning from the tiny candle-sparks down in the mist.

Brandt crossed to the porcelain stove, warming chilled blue fingers against the glazed white tiles while lowering his voice beneath the hiss of steam in the iron standpipe. "Two items, Herr Oberstleutnant. Neither pleasant. First, I took Schnabel out for schnapps behind the Wehrmacht signal exchange at five. Referat Four-A's courier pouch from Berlin Precinct Mitte cleared Poznań at noon; it reaches Aleja Szucha tomorrow afternoon at fourteen hundred hours."

Ludwig let the blackout paper fall shut, cutting off the street candles, and turned into the amber glow of the desk lamp. "Containing?"

"Certified photostats from Reichstagsufer Six—enrollment, class standings, and examination protocols for the graduating *Oberprima* of nineteen-thirty." Brandt hesitated half a beat, his scarred East Prussian face troubled as he met his commander's level gaze. For four years, from the Meuse crossings through Smolensk and Orel, Viktor Brandt had trusted Ludwig Schoeler with his life and—far rarer in nineteen-forty-three—with treasonous truths that would hang both of them from meat hooks in Plötzensee within an hour if whispered to the wrong ear. "You never told me the name of that Polish diplomat's daughter from your Berlin school days, Herr Oberstleutnant. Only old Bredow joked about her once over French brandy outside Vyazma before the mortar got him. *Die Polin*, Bredow called her. Said she was the only human being alive who ever drew blood on you."

Automatically Brandt's glance flicked half an inch toward the thin white crescent above Ludwig's right eyebrow—and then down toward the surgical chart hanging at the foot of the iron bedstead.

"It is the same woman," Brandt said quietly. Not a question.

"Yes, Viktor," Ludwig said.

Brandt exhaled slowly through his nose. "God in heaven. Does Reinhardt suspect?"

"Reinhardt suspects everything that breathes, on principle; tomorrow at two o'clock he stops suspecting and owns paper proof that the woman who saved my life and argued successfully against transferring me to Ujazdów spent three years in the same Berlin classroom with me." Ludwig walked slowly to the oak table, picking up a tiny, curved crescent of snapped amber glass—the scored ampoule neck he himself had spotted gleaming beneath the radiator foot when his pencil rolled onto the linoleum yesterday afternoon. Etched in microscopic white acid numerals around the collar ran: **418-B**. "And your second item?"

Brandt drew a folded carbon flimsy from his inner tunic pocket. "Precinct Four *Ordnungspolizei* fished two missing *Volksdeutsche* auxiliaries out of a flooded tannery cellar off Sienna Alley at noon today. Missing since Thursday evening—six-thirty, half an hour before curfew. No gunshot wounds. One died of a garrote wire plus a single narrow-bladed stab downward behind the right clavicle severing the subclavian artery and trachea; the larger man had his left common carotid and pharynx laid open from behind the jaw angle in one stroke. The police surgeon wrote—I quote—*anatomical precision strongly indicative of professional surgical or slaughterhouse training*."

Silence settled over the warm room like ash.

"Thursday evening at eight-fifteen," Brandt continued very softly, "Frau Doktor Kotarska began your intravenous *Cibazol* course—using unwrapped five-milliliter ampoules from Lot Four-Eighteen-B. The exact batch number reported stolen from Ujazdów supply depot last Tuesday and hawked through Wola black-market channels on Thursday afternoon."

Brandt laid the police flimsy beside the tiny amber glass collar on the oak blotter. He did not reach for a pistol, nor suggest calling the *Feldgendarmerie* outside the door; he simply stood waiting, watching the pale, severe mask of the officer he followed.

"How many copies of this tannery pathology memo reached Aleja Szucha?" Ludwig asked calmly.

"Routine precinct blotter so far—buried among eleven dead railway guards and four shot informers since Thursday," Brandt replied. "Nobody at Precinct Four has connected Sienna Alley to Lot Four-Eighteen-B at Ujazdów, because Ujazdów's quartermaster hushed up the theft to save his own skin from court-martial."

"Good. Burn that carbon in the stove ash-pan before we leave tomorrow morning." Ludwig buttoned the top two buttons of his white linen shirt one-handed—an irritatingly slow exercise he had forced himself to master since yesterday—leaving the lower half open across the broad white adhesive strapping around his ribs. "Have the Mercedes and an armed squad from Wehrmacht Town Major's motor pool—regular *Heer* infantry, Viktor, nobody who answers to SS or Polizei—at the courtyard gate tomorrow morning at zero-eight-hundred. Requisition Order Seven-B confirms Number Twelve Aleja Róż?"

"Confirmed and swept for microphones an hour ago, Herr Oberstleutnant. Empty Danish trade-attaché villa, brick garden wall, three minutes from Ujazdów Park. What about tonight?"

Ludwig glanced at his wristwatch: seven-ten. Five minutes until evening ward rounds.

"Step down to the ground-floor vestibule when Frau Doktor Kotarska comes up, Viktor," Ludwig said evenly. "Keep Reinhardt's two jackals occupied with cigarette rations for twenty minutes, and instruct our corporal outside this door that even if he hears porcelain break, nobody turns that handle unless I call."

Brandt picked up the police carbon, dropped it into the glowing coal slot of the porcelain stove until orange flame licked the grey flimsy to curled black ash, clicked his heels softly, and went out.

Four minutes later, wooden-soled hospital clogs clicked steadily down the second-floor tiles.

Sylwia entered carrying her white enamel dressing tray, nodding briefly to Brandt as the scarred captain passed her on the landing and descended the stairs. Stepping inside Isolation Room Two, she expected to find her patient propped against his bolsters waiting impatiently for his final evening *Cibazol* gram.

Instead, the latch clicked shut behind her—and a warm male hand reached past her shoulder to slide the interior brass bolt home.

Sylwia spun half-around, tray balanced effortlessly against her hip, storm-grey eyes flashing upward as she realized how absurdly different Isolation Room Two felt with Ludwig Schoeler standing upright on his boots.

Even pale, twelve pounds lighter, left forearm cradled cleanly in white linen across his chest and field-grey greatcoat slung dark over his shoulders, six feet two inches of upright Prussian staff officer swallowed half the narrow whitewashed room. Four hours earlier, down in the laundry sluice room, Halina had pressed a tiny rolled tissue spill from Marek into Sylwia's wet palm before burning it: *Brandt moves him tomorrow morning. Our watchers confirm Aleja Róż. No second chance inside German district. Photograph briefcase tonight or finish the vein before midnight. For Zbyszek. — G.*

On her white enamel tray, beside the loaded five-milliliter Pravaz syringe of clear *Cibazol*, rested a clean, empty twenty-cubic-centimeter glass Luer syringe—ostensibly for aspirating any recurrent pleural fluid. Twenty cubic centimeters of room air driven briskly into an antecubital vein foamed right-ventricular blood into compressible froth inside twelve heartbeats: acute pulmonary air lock, indistinguishable at clinical bedside from massive saddle thromboembolism out of a pelvic vein after twelve days' bedrest.

Her fingers had been icy since four o'clock. Now, looking up into Ludwig's clear, perilous slate-blue eyes across four feet of yellow lamplight, every survival bell in her skull clanged at once.

"Get back on that mattress, Herr Oberstleutnant," Sylwia said sharply, setting the enamel tray down on the oak table with a crisp *clink*. "Standing twenty minutes on an unhealed clavicle pulls the lateral trapezius upward and stresses—"

Her voice stopped.

Resting squarely in the center of the oak blotter under the gooseneck lamp, six inches from where she had just set her tray, lay a tiny snapped collar of amber glass etched in white acid: **418-B**.

Sylwia did not gasp, did not look down twice, did not touch her pocket. Only her jaw set one degree harder as she raised her grey eyes straight into his.

"Careless housekeeping on Thursday night, Kotarska," Ludwig murmured conversationally, leaning his lean hip slightly against the edge of the oak table while his unbound right hand rested inches from her tray. "Half of Lot Four-Eighteen-B went missing from Ujazdów Reserve-Lazarett last week. Two *Volksdeutsche* gendarmes who stopped a tall, Berlin-spoken Polish woman doctor in Sienna Alley at six-thirty Thursday evening turned up in a tannery cistern today—one with his carotid opened from behind the mandible, the other stabbed cleanly downward behind the right collarbone into the subclavian trunk."

His gaze dropped momentarily to the twenty-cubic-centimeter empty glass syringe glinting on her sterile towel, then rose calmly back to her face.

"Remarkable symmetry, wouldn't you say? Five days after sewing a left subclavian shut in the dark, somebody opens a right one in pitch-black mud without missing by two millimeters. Tell me—was the twenty-milliliter syringe on your tray tonight meant to tap my chest, Sylwia, or blow twenty cubic centimeters of Warsaw air into my right ventricle?"

Silence stretched so tight between them the hiss of the steam pipe sounded like escaping gas.

Any denial was beneath both of them; fifteen years ago in Berlin they had abandoned lying to each other once caught red-handed because lies wasted valuable seconds better spent counter-attacking.

"Would anyone in heaven or earth blame me if I did?" Sylwia asked very softly, standing straight as a surgical probe two paces in front of him, hands relaxed at her sides where three inches separated her fingers from the scalpel forceps on the tray. "Those two animals in Sienna Alley ripped my blouse open to rape me before handing me over to Aleja Szucha for carrying twenty ampoules of sulfa—ten of which saved *your* rotten Prussian pleura from empyema since Thursday! And my people outside know why Mauerwald sent you here, Ludwig. They know about *Kronos*."

Not a muscle flickered around Ludwig's eyes at the code word—which told Sylwia instantly that Marek's London intercept was dead accurate.

"Your people outside," Ludwig replied, voice dropping to cold, lethal velvet, "know precisely what Otto Reinhardt spoon-fed them! Use that brain Doktor Glass spent three years polishing, Sylwia! Who tipped your Wola gunmen off that a senior staff car switched onto the Małkinia loop on October nineteenth? Who wants *Kronos* kept running on a dirty string long enough to trace London's transmitters while blaming Wehrmacht intelligence for leaks? And what do you imagine happens to *you* at two-fifteen tomorrow afternoon if I die in this bed tonight?"

"I already know what arrives at Aleja Szucha tomorrow afternoon," Sylwia shot back coolly, enjoying the fractional narrowing of his slate eyes as she matched his intelligence network card for card. "Photostats from Precinct Mitte. Reichstagsufer Six, nineteen-twenty-seven through nineteen-thirty. Ignacy Słowik's bird on Szucha sang three days ago."

"Magnificent," Ludwig breathed, genuine, fierce admiration flashing across his lean face despite the abyss yawning directly beneath both their boots. "God in heaven, only you would have a spy inside Reinhardt's own teleprinter room while dodging hangmen in Wola! Then finish the equation, *Frau Doktor*! Suppose you put twenty cubic centimeters of air into my arm right now—go ahead, I have one arm strapped to my ribs and twelve pounds gone, you might even manage it before I break your wrist. At eight tomorrow morning I am found dead of 'embolism.' Six hours later, Otto Reinhardt opens the Berlin envelope, discovers Ludwig Schoeler's deadliest childhood companion held the syringe all week, drags you straight down into the cellars of Aleja Szucha before curfew, finds those wounded boys groaning behind your typhus boilers downstairs, and hangs every doctor and nurse in Pavilion Three!"

Every word struck like cold iron hammer-blows because every single word was mathematically irrefutable. She had been turning that exact nightmare cube over in her skull since Chłodna.

"Whereas if you walk out of here alive tomorrow morning to Number Twelve Aleja Róż," Sylwia countered bitterly, "Reinhardt *still* opens that Berlin envelope at two o'clock and arrests me by three!"

"No, he doesn't," Ludwig said. Reaching inside his open white shirt pocket with his right hand, he drew out a stiff cream card stamped in crimson Generalstab eagle-and-swastika wax over Generaloberst Zeitzler's counter-intelligence seal and tossed it beside the amber glass ring.

Sylwia glanced down without touching it.

**OBERKOMMANDO DES HEERES — ABWEHRSTELLE / SONDERAUSWEIS NR. 114.** By authority of OKH Special Investigation Staff, **Dr. med. Sylwia Kotarska** of *Szpital Dzieciątka Jezus* was hereby commandeered under exclusive Wehrmacht military jurisdiction as attending thoracic surgeon to Oberstleutnant i.G. Ludwig Schoeler at Aleja Róż Twelve, immune from civil or *Sicherheitspolizei* detention save upon counter-signature of Army Group Centre Counter-Espionage.

Sylwia stared at the crimson seal as if it were a live cobra coiled on oak. "Are you out of your mind? Slapping an OKH protection warrant on my back the exact day Reinhardt learns we knew each other in Berlin? That practically screams conspiracy in his ear!"

"Let it scream," Ludwig said icily. "Reinhardt is an ex-Munich homicide detective, not a berserker like Kutschera. When he sees an open, brazen Generalstab requisition signed twenty-four hours *before* his Berlin mail arrives, he pauses—because arresting an OKH-commandeered surgeon without hard treason proof starts a jurisdictional war with Mauerwald, AND he instantly assumes I am either blackmailing an old Berlin acquaintance into spying on Warsaw doctors or sleeping with her! Either theory keeps you out of Pawiak's basement while his hounds shadow us—provided we find his mole *Jaskółka* first."

*Jaskółka.* Second time he had spoken of a double-agent; first time he had named it.

"You expect me," Sylwia whispered, incredulous fury rising hot in her chest, "to walk through the German quarter every evening into your villa under Marek's binoculars—looking to the entire Polish Underground like a Gestapo colonel's kept whore—to help *Abwehr* hunt a spy?"

"Not my spy—*Reinhardt's* spy inside your own house!" Ludwig snapped back, stepping one long pace around the corner of the oak table until barely eighteen inches separated them. "Somebody inside or touching this hospital tipped off my train *and* told Reinhardt within forty-eight hours that you bolted this door on Friday night! Until *Jaskółka* stops singing, neither your cellar boys nor my people leave Warsaw alive!"

"*Your* people?" she challenged sharply. "Wehrmacht intelligence come to crush *Kronos*?"

"Wait until we're behind stone walls I swept myself before you presume to know why I came to Warsaw, Kotarska!"

"I won't wear a German leash—not Reinhardt's and never yours!" And before her cooler brain could check the fierce, lifelong reflex never to leave evidence sitting under Ludwig Schoeler's hand, Sylwia darted her right fingers sideways across the blotter to snatch the `418-B` amber glass ring and crush it under her clog heel.

Even one-armed, Ludwig moved faster than eyesight.

His right hand shot down, intercepting her left wrist—no, her right wrist inches above the glass—and as she twisted violently leftward bringing her free hand toward the blunt bandage shears to force him back, Ludwig stepped squarely inside her swing, caught her left forearm against her own ribs by pinning her backward with the hard wall of his braced chest and right shoulder, and backed her two swift steps flat against the tall, hot glazed-porcelain tiles of the corner stove.

"Don't—" Sylwia gasped, breath knocked half out of her as smooth hot porcelain met her shoulder blades through thin starched cotton and six feet two inches of hard, lean muscle locked her immovable from hip to collarbone.

His unbound right hand slid rapidly up to trap both her crossed wrists firmly against the upper swell of her own chest just below her throat—careful even in fury not to twist radius bones, utterly relentless in holding her fast—while his right thigh wedged squarely between her knees, spreading her stance three inches on the linoleum so she couldn't drive a knee into his groin as she had boasted sixteen years ago on the Marschallbrücke.

Instantly, from the sudden violent exertion of pinning a tall fighting woman one-handed, fresh hot arterial-bright blood seeped from his Friday-night fourth-rib incision through the white strapping and bloomed in a warm crimson coin the size of a silver mark directly through his unfastened white linen shirt—pressing wet and hot right against the thin cotton covering her left breast.

Both of them felt the wet heat strike through cloth at the exact same second.

Both of them froze.

Thirty-two years old, stove heat baking into Sylwia's spine, sleet ticking against blackout paper three yards away, and suddenly sixteen years vanished into thin smoke. Up this close, Ludwig was no phantom from Berlin classrooms nor distant uniform across a ward; he was overwhelming, suffocatingly real—broad shoulders blocking out the room, fever-pared jaw dark with evening golden stubble two inches above her forehead, chest rising and falling hard and ragged against her breasts with every breath, smelling of clean skin, hot linen, iron blood, and winter wool.

"Let go of me, Ludwig," she whispered—except something dark and treacherous down in her own lungs robbed the command of half its ice, turning her voice into a low, uneven scrape that made his slate-blue eyes darken almost to black.

"So you can stab me with Lister shears or blow air into my heart?" he murmured back, breath hot across her cheekbone and parted lips, neither moving back an inch nor loosening his grip on her crossed wrists. "Look at me and tell me you didn't feel that blood soak through onto your skin just now, Sylwia. My blood—or yours, God knows which half is whose anymore."

"Get off me before your subclavian tears—"

"Let it tear," he breathed harshly, head dipping an inch lower until the thin white crescent scar above his right eyebrow brushed a loose dark tendril at her temple, his thumb shifting half an inch upward off her wrists to rest against the frantic, traitorous hammering of her carotid pulse just beneath her jaw.

And then his calloused thumb slid one centimeter lower along the starched seam of her high smock collar—and struck the hard, unmistakable cherry-stone cylinder of paraffined glass sewn inside the linen.

Ludwig went stone-still from head to heel.

Every staff officer who had ever interrogated captured British SOE agents or Soviet parachutists knew the exact dimensions and feel of an eight-grain potassium cyanide ampoule stitched behind a collar lapel.

Slowly, terribly, Ludwig lifted his eyes from the white linen seam at her collarbone straight into her wide storm-grey eyes. What flared in his face now wasn't tactical calculation, nor schoolboy rivalry, nor even anger—it was a raw, ferocious blaze of sheer possessive fury that shook Sylwia harder than the scalpel fight in Sienna Alley.

Pressing his thumb squarely between the cyanide bead and her jaw so she felt the hard outline against her own skin, Ludwig lowered his mouth until his lips barely brushed the corner of hers in a ghost-touch hotter than open flame.

"Listen to me, Sylwia Kotarska," he whispered straight against her mouth, every syllable vibrating down through her ribs into the pit of her stomach. "You will walk through the gate of Twelve Aleja Róż at eighteen hundred hours tomorrow carrying that black bag of yours. We will find *Jaskółka* before Reinhardt touches a hair of your head. And if you ever put your teeth near this glass bead while my heart is still beating in this city, I swear before God I will break every door down between Mauerwald and Pawiak and pry your jaws open with my bare hands."

For two endless, dizzying seconds neither of them breathed—her lips parted an eighth of an inch beneath the heat of his, her pulse thundering wildly under his thumb, fifteen years of hatred twisting into an ache so sharp and dark she didn't know whether she wanted to sink her teeth into his lower lip or pull his head down with both hands.

Outside in the corridor, heavy boots shifted once beside the frosted glass as Brandt returned up the stairs and cleared his throat loudly before knocking.

Ludwig released her wrists cleanly, stepped back two paces to the oak table, picked up the `418-B` amber glass collar, dropped it under the iron heel of his riding boot, and ground it into unrecognizable powder on the floor.

"Eighteen hundred hours tomorrow, Frau Doktor," he said in crisp, impersonal Generalstab German just as Brandt rapped twice on the frame. "Leave the *Cibazol*; we are finished here for tonight."
